Medical practice licensing: the paperwork that has to exist before a patient can be seen.

Before a practice can bill anyone — a payer, a patient, anyone — a stack of licenses and registrations has to exist and line up with each other: the business entity, each provider's state medical license, DEA registration if controlled substances are prescribed, NPI numbers, and often a facility-level license like a CLIA waiver. None of this is credentialing yet. It's the legal authority to operate that credentialing gets layered on top of, and getting the sequence wrong is the single most common reason a practice opening takes six months instead of ninety days.

nppes.cms.hhs.gov
DEA registration

Key takeaways

  • Licensing is not credentialing. It's the legal authority to exist and practice; payer credentialing is a separate process layered on top of it.
  • Sequence matters more than speed. DEA registration needs an active state license first; credentialing needs licensing done first. Starting out of order stalls everything downstream.
  • The MATE Act training attestation is a one-time requirement, not annual — but it has to be on file before the DEA will process a first-time or renewal application filed after June 2023.
  • Every state license is separate. There is no national medical license; the Interstate Compact speeds up getting several, it doesn't replace needing them.

Choosing the entity type

Common entity types for a medical practice. State rules on physician ownership vary significantly — confirm with the specific state before choosing.
EntityTypical useWhat to check first
PLLC (Professional LLC)Solo or small-group practices in states that allow itNot every state offers a PLLC; some require a PC instead for licensed professionals
PC (Professional Corporation)Practices in states requiring a corporate form for licensed professionalsOwnership is typically restricted to licensed practitioners of the same or related profession
Standard LLCAncillary or non-clinical entities affiliated with a practiceMany states bar a standard LLC from directly billing for professional medical services
Corporation (Inc.)Larger, multi-shareholder practice groupsMore formal governance requirements (board, bylaws, meeting minutes) than a PLLC/PC

Most states also enforce some version of the corporate practice of medicine doctrine, which restricts non-physicians from owning or controlling a medical practice, in varying strength from state to state — some states enforce it strictly, others barely at all, and a structure that's routine in one state can be outright prohibited in another. This is precisely the kind of decision to confirm with counsel licensed in the specific state rather than copying a structure that worked somewhere else. Full detail on entity types, EIN timing, and how corporate practice of medicine restrictions actually shape the choice, is in our entity formation and EIN guide.

The four licenses (or license types) almost every practice needs

Formation, state licensure, DEA, and NPI aren't optional add-ons for most practices — they're sequential prerequisites, each one a dependency for something downstream. Getting the business entity registered lets you open a bank account and apply for an EIN. The EIN and entity are what a state licensing board and, later, a payer credentialing application both expect to see. The individual provider's state medical license is what the DEA application requires before it will even accept a submission. And the NPI — both the individual's Type 1 and the organization's Type 2 — shows up on literally every claim, every credentialing application, and every PECOS or CAQH profile from that point forward.

Full walkthrough of forming the business entity itself — LLC vs. PLLC vs. PC, EIN timing, and what a state licensing board wants to see from the entity before it will license an individual to practice under it — is in our entity formation and EIN guide.

State medical board licensure

Every state runs its own medical board, its own application, its own primary-source verification process, and its own timeline — there is no national medical license, and a license in one state carries zero weight in another unless that state participates in a specific compact (more on that below). A first-time applicant should expect primary-source verification of every credential claimed: medical school, residency, prior state licenses, board certification, and any history of discipline anywhere the applicant has practiced. Because that verification runs through third parties — the medical school registrar, the residency program, the prior state board — the timeline is often gated by how quickly those outside parties respond, not by anything the applicant controls directly. Full state-by-state process detail, what primary-source verification actually checks, and the renewal-cycle tracking that keeps a license from lapsing years after it was granted, is in our state medical board licensing guide.

DEA registration and the MATE Act training requirement

A DEA registration is required to prescribe any controlled substance, and it's a federal registration layered on top of — not a replacement for — a state controlled-substance registration that most states also require separately. As of 2026 the registration fee is $888 for a three-year term, and the application requires an active, unrestricted state license already in hand; applying before the state license is final simply won't process. Since the Medication Access and Training Expansion (MATE) Act took effect on June 27, 2023, every DEA registrant — new applicant or renewal — has had to attest on Form 224 or 224a to completing eight hours of accredited training on treating and managing patients with opioid or substance use disorders. It's a one-time attestation, not an annual requirement, but a registrant renewing for the first time since 2023 who hasn't completed the training yet will find that out at the worst possible moment — mid-application. Full detail on who needs the training, which prior credentials exempt a registrant from it, and the renewal timeline to plan around, is in our DEA registration guide.

NPI: Type 1 and Type 2, and why a practice needs both

The National Provider Identifier is a free, ten-digit number issued through NPPES, and nearly every downstream process — Medicare enrollment, commercial credentialing, every claim submitted — references it. Individual practitioners get a Type 1 NPI; the organization itself gets a separate Type 2 NPI, and a group practice needs both, because claims and enrollments distinguish between the person delivering care and the entity that bills for it. A common early mistake is applying for the individual NPI and assuming it covers the practice too, then discovering during Medicare or payer enrollment that the organizational NPI was never obtained. Full detail on the application itself, updating taxonomy codes when a provider changes specialty focus, and the deactivation risk from letting NPPES records go stale, is in our NPI registration guide.

Multi-state licensing and telehealth

A practice seeing patients — in person or by telehealth — across state lines generally needs a full, separate medical license in every state where the patient is physically located at the time of the encounter, regardless of where the provider is sitting. The Interstate Medical Licensure Compact exists specifically to speed this up for physicians: instead of a full separate application in each state, an eligible physician gets an expedited pathway through participating states via a Letter of Qualification issued by their state of principal license. It doesn't waive state licensure requirements or create one universal license — the physician still ends up holding a distinct license in each compact state, just faster than applying cold. Membership has grown to 40-plus states as of 2026, but it changes, and not every state or every provider type is eligible, so confirming current membership before promising a client a specific timeline matters. Full detail on compact eligibility, the states currently outside it, and how non-compact multi-state licensing actually gets done, is in our multi-state licensing guide.

Facility-level licenses: CLIA and beyond

Some licenses attach to the location, not the provider. Any practice performing even simple in-house lab tests — a rapid strep, a urine pregnancy test, a fingerstick glucose — needs a CLIA certificate before running them, and for the simplest test category that's a Certificate of Waiver, filed on Form CMS-116 through the state agency, valid for two years once issued. Practices doing more complex testing, imaging, or ambulatory procedures may need additional facility-specific licenses on top of that, layered onto the practice's core registrations rather than replacing them. Full walkthrough of the CLIA waiver application, what tests actually qualify as "waived," and the other facility licenses that come up most often, is in our CLIA and facility licensing guide.

Renewal cycles: the part that gets forgotten after the opening rush

Everything above gets careful attention during the opening scramble, and then, once the practice is seeing patients, tends to fall off everyone's radar until a renewal notice shows up — or doesn't, because it went to an old address or a staff member who's since left. State medical licenses renew on a cycle set by each board, typically with continuing medical education (CME) requirements attached that have to be completed and documented before the renewal will process, not just claimed. DEA registration renews every three years and requires the same active-state-license check as the original application. CLIA certificates of waiver renew every two years. None of these renewal dates line up with each other, which is exactly why a single missed one is so easy: nothing on this list renews at the same time as anything else.

What lapses if a renewal is missed, and how hard it is to fix.
LicenseTypical cycleIf it lapses
State medical licenseSet by state board, often 1-2 yearsPracticing on a lapsed license is a serious compliance issue, not just an administrative one
DEA registration3 yearsCannot prescribe controlled substances; reinstatement requires a fresh application

NPINo expiration, but can be deactivated for inactivity or unresponsiveness to NPPESDeactivation disrupts claims and enrollment records tied to that NPI

Step by step: sequencing it correctly

  1. Form the business entity and obtain the EINLLC, PLLC, PC or corporation depending on the state and specialty; the EIN follows once the entity is registered and is required for almost everything after this step.
  2. File each provider's state medical license applicationThis runs on the individual, not the entity, and can start in parallel with entity formation — but primary-source verification means the timeline is often the longest single step.
  3. Apply for the Type 1 and Type 2 NPIFree, done through NPPES, and can be done early since it doesn't depend on the state license being finalized.
  4. File the DEA registration once the state license is activeRequires the MATE Act training attestation on file and the $888 fee for the 3-year term; cannot be submitted before the state license is final.
  5. Apply for facility-level licenses once the location is confirmedCLIA waiver if any in-house testing is planned, plus any other facility-specific license the specialty or location requires.
  6. Hand off to credentialingOnce the licensing layer is in place, payer credentialing and Medicare/Medicaid enrollment can begin — not before.

Where licensing hands off to credentialing

Licensing and credentialing are sequential, but practices frequently start the second before the first is actually finished, on the assumption that a "mostly done" license is close enough. It usually isn't. Medicare enrollment through PECOS, most commercial payer credentialing, and every state Medicaid application all ask for an active, unrestricted state license number as a hard field on the application — not a pending one. Submitting a credentialing application with a license still in process either gets rejected outright or sits in limbo until the license clears, which wastes the processing time rather than saving it. The practical approach is to treat the state license as a genuine gate: credentialing timelines should be planned to start the week the license is confirmed active, not the week the application was filed. Once licensing is in place, the process moves into payer credentialing and enrollment, which is a distinct set of processes covered in our Medicare, Medicaid and commercial payer guides.

Pro tip

Start the state medical license application before anything else that depends on it, even if entity formation isn't finished yet — it's almost always the longest step because it depends on third parties (schools, prior boards) responding to verification requests, not on how fast your own paperwork moves.

Do and don't

Do
  • Start the state medical license application as early as possible — it usually sets the whole timeline.
  • Apply for both the Type 1 and Type 2 NPI, not just the individual's.
  • Confirm MATE Act training is complete before submitting a DEA application.
  • Confirm current Interstate Compact membership before promising a multi-state timeline.
  • Diarise every renewal date — license, DEA, CLIA — the moment each is issued.
Don't
  • Don't file a DEA application before the state license is fully active.
  • Don't assume one state's license or verification carries weight in another.
  • Don't start payer credentialing before the licensing layer is actually in place.
  • Don't run in-house lab tests without a CLIA certificate on file, even simple ones.
  • Don't let a license lapse assuming a grace period will cover it — reinstatement is harder than renewal.

Frequently asked questions

What's the difference between licensing and credentialing?

Licensing is the legal authority to exist and practice at all — your business entity, your state medical license, your DEA registration, your NPI. Credentialing is a separate process where individual payers verify you meet their network standards and agree to pay you. You need licensing in place before most credentialing can even start, but being licensed doesn't make you in-network with anyone — that's a payer-by-payer process covered in our credentialing guides.

What order should these steps happen in?

Entity formation and EIN first, since the business has to exist on paper before almost anything else can reference it. State medical board licensure for each practicing provider runs in parallel, since it depends on the individual, not the entity. DEA registration requires an active state license first. NPI can be obtained early and independently. CLIA and other facility licenses can run alongside the others once the practice location is confirmed. Sequencing these wrong is the single most common cause of a 90-day opening turning into six months.

Do you handle practice licensing for us?

Yes. We coordinate entity formation, state licensing, DEA and NPI registration, and facility-level licenses like CLIA waivers, sequencing each so nothing sits waiting on a step that hasn't started yet, then hand straight into credentialing and enrollment once the licensing layer is in place.

Opening a practice, or adding a location?

We sequence entity formation, licensing, DEA/NPI registration and credentialing in parallel wherever possible, so nothing sits waiting on a step that hasn't started yet.
