Vascular surgery billing and coding: the complete 2026 guide.

Vascular surgery runs on two things most other specialties don't have to think about at the same intensity: a territory-by-territory hierarchy that decides which of dozens of near-identical codes applies to a single vessel, and a dialysis access practice that generates some of the highest denial volume in the specialty while getting almost no coverage from the billing guides written about it. This guide covers the full revenue cycle — what changed in 2026, every core procedure family from venous ablation through EVAR/TEVAR to dialysis circuit intervention, the exact LCD citations by Medicare Administrative Contractor, and the gaps we found nobody else in this niche publishes correctly.

Reviewed by Phares A., MD · Physiotherapist, CPC (AAPC) · Updated August 2026

Key takeaways

  • 2026 replaced 37220-37235 with 46 new codes across four vascular territories — iliac (37254-37262), femoropopliteal (37263-37279), tibial/peroneal (37280-37295), and a brand-new inframalleolar territory (37296-37299). Nearly every competing guide names the block and stops there; the territory-by-territory breakdown is what actually determines the code.
  • There is no LCD L33822. A widely circulated guide cites that number as Medicare's venous ablation coverage policy. We checked CMS coverage data directly — it doesn't exist. The five real LCDs are named below, by contractor.
  • Dialysis access billing is nearly absent from every vascular billing guide we reviewed, despite being one of the highest-volume, highest-denial procedure families a practicing vascular surgeon bills. This guide gives it a full section plus a dedicated spoke.
  • The territory hierarchy is itself a bundling structure. Stacking atherectomy and a stent add-on on the same vessel is one of the most common and most avoidable denial patterns in the 2026 code set.

Vascular surgery billing at a glance

Vascular surgery denies for reasons that cluster around three structural problems: a 2026 code set that rewrote the specialty's highest-volume procedure family from the ground up, dialysis access work that carries dense internal bundling almost nobody documents correctly, and diagnosis coding where laterality and clinical stage — not just the presence of disease — decide whether Medicare pays. Here's the shape of it before the detail.

Two gaps drive most of the lost revenue we see when we audit a vascular surgery client's claims. First, coders still reaching for deleted 37220–37235 codes or treating 37254–37299 as a single undifferentiated block instead of a four-territory hierarchy, which produces both invalid-code denials and stacking denials on the same visit. Second, dialysis access claims billed as if 36901–36906 were independent line items rather than a mutually exclusive hierarchy, which is close to the single most common bundling denial in a practice with any meaningful dialysis access volume.

2026 brought the largest structural rewrite to peripheral vascular coding since the specialty's codes were first organized by territory. Two changes matter operationally, not just as trivia for a coding newsletter.

Global periods, E/M, and staged procedures

Vascular surgery runs two global-period tracks side by side, and mixing them up is one of the most common modifier errors in the specialty. Open procedures — open aortic aneurysm repair, open bypass, carotid endarterectomy — carry the standard 090-day major-surgery global period. Most endovascular add-on and second-look codes, by contrast, carry a 000-day global, because they're built around a single encounter rather than a recovery period.

  • 1Modifier 58 — staged or related procedure, same global period. Use it for a planned completion angiogram after an open bypass, or a planned second-stage lower extremity revascularization treating a different territory than the first session, when the second procedure was anticipated at the time of the first and documented as such.
  • 2Modifier 24 — unrelated E/M during a global period. A 090-day EVAR or open bypass patient seen for an unrelated condition three weeks later is billable with 24 attached; an E/M addressing the surgical site or a surgical complication during the same window is not separately billable.
  • 3Modifier 25 — same-day E/M alongside a diagnostic vascular study or minor procedure. Appropriate when a significant, separately identifiable E/M happens the same day as a 000-day-global diagnostic study (an ankle-brachial index or duplex ordered and interpreted during an office visit that also addressed unrelated symptoms) — never on an E/M billed the same day as a 090-day major open procedure.

The failure mode we see most in vascular practices with high procedure volume: staff default to modifier 25 out of habit on every same-day E/M, including ones that belong on the 090-day track as modifier 24, or omit 58 on a genuinely planned staged procedure because the documentation didn't state up front that a second session was anticipated. Both mistakes are preventable by deciding, at the time the plan is made, which track the next encounter falls on.

Medicare coverage for venous ablation is entirely MAC-specific, and getting the citation wrong undermines an otherwise solid appeal. One widely circulated competitor guide cites LCD "L33822" as Medicare's coverage policy for venous ablation — we searched CMS coverage data directly and found no LCD with that ID. Lower extremity revascularization: the 2026 territory hierarchy

This is the section every competitor guide gets least right. Search results for "37254-37299" consistently describe it as a block — the replacement for 37220–37235 — without breaking out which codes apply to which vessels, and without explaining the hierarchical rule that decides whether a second technique on the same vessel is separately billable at all. Here's the territory structure in full.

Lower extremity revascularization territories and code ranges, effective 2026.
TerritoryCode rangeVessels covered
Iliac37254–37262Common iliac, external iliac arteries
Femoropopliteal37263–37279Common femoral, superficial femoral, popliteal arteries
Tibial/peroneal37280–37295Anterior tibial, posterior tibial, peroneal arteries
Inframalleolar (new for 2026)37296–37299Below-the-ankle vessels — no dedicated code existed for this territory before 2026

Within each territory, the specific code depends on three variables together, not any one alone: which vessel was treated, what technology was used (angioplasty, atherectomy, or stent placement), and whether the lesion was straightforward stenosis or complex — including a 100%-occluded, chronic total occlusion lesion. That third variable is where the biggest documentation gap shows up: an operative note that says "stenosis treated" without characterizing occlusion percentage and lesion morphology doesn't support the complex-lesion code even when the procedure performed clearly qualified.

The hierarchical reporting rule is the piece every competitor guide skips. Within a single vessel, the code set is structured so that a higher-intensity technique's code already includes the lesser techniques performed to achieve it — angioplasty performed to prep a vessel for atherectomy, or atherectomy performed before stent placement, doesn't stack as separate line items on top of the highest code used. A claim reporting an atherectomy code and a stent add-on code on the identical vessel in the identical session is, in almost every case, reporting the same intervention twice rather than two distinct services, and it denies as bundled far more often than it pays. The one scenario where two codes on the same leg are both billable is genuinely separate vessels or separate territories treated in the same session, documented as such.

Primary-vessel-versus-additional-vessel logic follows the same territory structure: a primary code applies to the first vessel treated within a territory, and where an add-on exists for treating an additional vessel in the same territory, it's reported alongside the primary code rather than as a second primary code. Mixing this up — billing two primary-vessel codes for what was actually a primary vessel plus an additional vessel in the same territory — is a second common stacking error distinct from the atherectomy-plus-stent pattern above.

PAD endovascular coverage criteria got a dedicated, newly effective national-scope LCD in 2026: L40228, "Endovascular Management for Peripheral Arterial Disease of the Upper and Lower Extremities" (Palmetto GBA), with a comment period opening 02/26/2026 and an effective date of 04/12/2026, confirmed directly against the CMS Coverage Database. This is the policy to check before scheduling an elective endovascular PAD intervention on a Medicare patient covered by that jurisdiction, and it sits alongside the older, narrower L37774 (Palmetto GBA), "Supervised Exercise Therapy for the Treatment of Peripheral Arterial Disease with Symptomatic Lower Extremity Intermittent Claudication," which governs the conservative-therapy pathway many payers expect to see attempted, or at least offered and declined, before an elective endovascular claudication intervention is approved. ⚠️ We confirmed the LCD ID, MAC, and effective date for L40228 directly; the full text of its specific coverage indications and documentation checklist could not be retrieved from CMS's coverage-document pages during this build (access errors on every fetch attempt), so pull the current LCD text yourself before building an order-set rule against it.

Full territory-by-territory code tables, worked multi-vessel billing scenarios, and the hierarchical-reporting decision tree are in our lower extremity revascularization coding guide.

Aortic aneurysm repair: EVAR, TEVAR, and open repair

Endovascular aneurysm repair (EVAR) of the abdominal aorta is billed from the 34701–34718 family depending on the extent of aortic and iliac artery involvement and whether the repair uses a standard or fenestrated/branched endograft. Documentation has to establish aneurysm location and extent precisely enough to support the specific code, not just "AAA repaired" — the operative note needs to state which segments (infrarenal, juxtarenal, pararenal) and which iliac arteries were involved in the repair, because that's what both the code selection and the ICD-10 diagnosis (below) hinge on.

TEVAR (thoracic endovascular aortic repair) is where 2026 made its second major structural change. Catheter placement, radiologic supervision and interpretation, and proximal extensions performed at the time of the primary TEVAR procedure are now bundled into the revised base procedure codes in the 33880–33886 range, rather than billed as separate add-on components the way they were before 2026. The revised codes shift toward anatomy-based selection that accounts for left subclavian artery coverage and the use of branched or fenestrated endografts, and a new, distinct code exists for cases using a thoracic branch endoprosthesis (TBE) — the first new device category in the TEVAR family since FDA approval in 2022 finally got a dedicated code. ⚠️ The exact TBE code number and the individual descriptors across 33880–33886 could not be confirmed against a primary CPT source during this build; see the flag in the 2026 changes section above.

Open aortic aneurysm repair is coded by aortic segment — the approach and the extent of the repair (infrarenal versus suprarenal versus thoracoabdominal) determine the code family, distinct from the endovascular codes above, and carries the standard 090-day global period discussed earlier.

ICD-10 specificity for aortic aneurysm is one of the clearest examples in vascular surgery of a code choice that decides coverage outright, not just documentation quality. We verified the full I71 hierarchy directly against the FY2026 ICD-10-CM code set.

Carotid and cerebrovascular procedures

Carotid revascularization splits between open carotid endarterectomy (35301) and endovascular carotid artery stenting, which itself splits by access route — transfemoral carotid artery stenting (37215/37216, with and without cerebral protection) and transcarotid artery revascularization (TCAR) using the 37217/37218 family. Code selection isn't a style choice; it follows the access route and cerebral protection device actually used, documented in the operative note, and CEA carries the 090-day global period that governs staged and unrelated-visit billing the way the earlier global-period section describes.

Non-invasive cerebrovascular study coverage is governed by L35753 (WPS Insurance Corporation), "Non-Invasive Cerebrovascular Studies," effective 02/26/2026, confirmed directly against the CMS Coverage Database, alongside L35397 (Novitas Solutions), "Non-Invasive Cerebrovascular Arterial Studies," for practices in that jurisdiction. Diagnostic angiography of the carotid, vertebral, and subclavian territories — the invasive study, not the duplex — is governed by L35035 (Novitas Solutions), "Thoracic Aortography and Carotid, Vertebral, and Subclavian Angiography," also confirmed directly. Neither of these was correctly cited by name in the competing guides we reviewed; most either skip carotid coverage policy entirely or cite generic "Medicare coverage" without naming the article.

ICD-10 laterality on carotid occlusion and stenosis diagnoses is a hard requirement, not a nicety: I65.21 (right carotid artery), I65.22 (left), and I65.23 (bilateral) are the specific, billable options, verified live against the FY2026 code set, against I65.29 (unspecified carotid artery) as the fallback that fails a coverage check a laterality-specific code would pass. Given that CEA and CAS are both inherently laterality-specific procedures — the operative side is never ambiguous in the chart — there's rarely a legitimate reason to submit I65.29 on a carotid revascularization claim.

Dialysis access procedures

This is the gap nobody else covers, and it's one of the highest-volume, highest-denial procedure families a practicing vascular surgeon bills. Dialysis access work splits into three code families that function as a hierarchy, not an independent menu.

Dialysis access procedure code families, 36818-36909.
RangeServiceHierarchy rule
36818–36833Open AV fistula/graft creation, revision, and open thrombectomyOpen surgical family, coded by specific procedure performed (creation vs. revision vs. thrombectomy) and vessel used (autogenous vs. graft)
36901–36903Percutaneous management of a patent dialysis circuit — diagnostic imaging only, angioplasty, or angioplasty with stent, in increasing intensityHierarchical: report the single highest-intensity code performed, not each component separately
36904–36906Endovascular dialysis circuit thrombectomy, with or without angioplasty or stent, in increasing intensityHierarchical within itself, and mutually exclusive with 36901–36903 in the same session

The rule that matters most operationally: only one code from the combined 36901–36906 set is reportable per session per patient, because each code in that set is defined to already include the lesser interventions performed to reach it. A dialysis circuit session that includes a diagnostic fistulagram, angioplasty, and thrombectomy is reported with the single highest code in the hierarchy that describes what was done — not three separate line items. Billing a 3690x angioplasty code alongside a separate 3690x thrombectomy code from the same encounter is the single most common bundling denial we see in a practice with meaningful dialysis access volume, and it's a documentation-and-charge-capture problem, not a payer being unreasonable: the codes were built as a hierarchy specifically to prevent that pattern.

Maturation-period billing traps. A newly created AV fistula needs time — commonly six to twelve weeks — to mature into a usable dialysis access before cannulation, and interventions during that window (angioplasty to assist maturation) are billed differently than maintenance work on an already-mature, in-use access. Billing a maturation-assist intervention as if it were routine circuit maintenance, or vice versa, is a documentation-driven denial pattern specific to this window that a general vascular billing guide won't flag.

Medicare coverage for ongoing dialysis access maintenance is governed by L34062 (CGS Administrators), "Dialysis Access Maintenance," confirmed live against the CMS Coverage Database with an effective date of 01/22/2026. Hemodialysis frequency itself — how often sessions are covered, distinct from access maintenance — is governed by a Frequency of Hemodialysis LCD published separately by nearly every MAC, confirmed active for: Wellpoint Federal (L37475), CGS Administrators (L37575), WPS Insurance Corporation (L37537), Noridian Healthcare Solutions (L37502, current; a prior version, L37504, retired 10/16/2025), Palmetto GBA (L34575), First Coast Service Options (L37564), and Novitas Solutions (L35014). Confirm which MAC governs the claim and cite that specific article, because a Frequency of Hemodialysis denial (CO-151) is fought or corrected entirely differently than a Dialysis Access Maintenance coverage denial (CO-50).

Fistula First documentation expectations track the CMS-driven quality initiative favoring autogenous AV fistulas over grafts and catheters wherever clinically appropriate: the chart should reflect why a fistula versus a graft was chosen, particularly for a graft placement or a catheter-to-fistula conversion, because payers and quality reviewers increasingly expect that rationale documented rather than assumed. Full code family detail, maturation-window billing scenarios, and the CGS LCD's documentation checklist are in our dialysis access billing guide.

Vascular ultrasound and diagnostic studies

Non-invasive vascular studies (93880–93990) are vascular surgery's version of cardiology's imaging component-split problem: the same duplex study can legitimately be billed globally, technical-only, or professional-only depending on who owns the equipment and who performs the interpretation, using the same 26/TC modifier logic covered in the modifiers section below.

Coverage is entirely MAC-specific and split by study type, and we confirmed four separate active LCDs governing it directly against the CMS Coverage Database.

The duplicate-billing trap that shows up most: a hospital-based vascular lab performs and bills the technical component of a duplex study, and the surgeon's own practice separately bills the global fee for the same study on the same date, because the workflow never distinguished "I read a study my hospital's lab acquired" (professional component only, modifier 26) from "my practice's lab acquired and I read this" (global, no modifier). The two claims contradict each other on their face and one of them recoups, exactly as in cardiology's echo billing problem — the fix is the same: map every study to its default component split by site of service before it's billed, not after the denial arrives.

Modifiers that carry weight in vascular surgery

No specialty leans harder on laterality and distinct-vessel modifiers than vascular surgery, because so much of the code set describes a procedure that's inherently one-sided or vessel-specific.

RT / LT / 50
  • RT and LT identify which side a unilateral procedure or study was performed on; 50 reports a genuinely bilateral procedure performed in the same session.
  • Missing laterality on a vascular ultrasound or an endovascular intervention is a common cause of a claim pricing at the wrong rate rather than denying outright — nobody catches it without a specific audit.
59 / XE, XS, XP, XU
  • Distinct procedural service — only appropriate for a genuinely separate vessel, territory, or session, never as a routine way to clear a territory-hierarchy bundling edit.
  • XS (separate structure) covers most defensible vascular unbundling, since the distinction is almost always anatomic — a separate vessel or territory, not the same vessel treated twice.
26 / TC
  • 26 — professional component only, reading a vascular ultrasound acquired on equipment the practice doesn't own.
  • TC — technical component only, billed by whoever owns the equipment and employs the sonographer, regardless of who interprets it.
59 to clear a same-vessel stack
  • Appending 59 or an X-modifier to bill atherectomy and a stent add-on on the identical vessel doesn't make the pair separately payable — the territory hierarchy makes the second code non-distinct by design, not by an edit that documentation can override.

58, 78, and 79 govern staged and unplanned scenarios inside a global period, as introduced earlier: 58 for a planned staged or related procedure, 78 for an unplanned return to the operating room for a complication of the original procedure, and 79 for a genuinely unrelated procedure by the same physician during the global period. KX attests that a specific LCD's coverage criteria were met — append it only when the chart actually supports every element of the relevant LCD's checklist (CEAP class and reflux duration for ablation, conservative-therapy trial for PAD intervention), because appending KX without the supporting documentation is treated the same as billing false coverage attestation on audit, not as a harmless formality.

NCCI edits, MUE limits, and bundling traps

This is where vascular surgery billing gets decided, and it's the exact gap the cardiology pillar in this series exploited first: every competitor guide names that bundling pairs exist and says "append modifier 59 when distinct," without ever stating the pair-specific value that determines whether an override is even possible.

The territory hierarchy itself is a bundling structure. Before looking at any published NCCI pair, understand that the 2026 code set folds the majority of same-vessel bundling logic directly into code selection: a higher-intensity technique's code already includes the lesser techniques performed to reach it, which is why the atherectomy-plus-stent stacking pattern described in the LER section above denies so reliably — it isn't just an NCCI edit, it's the code definitions themselves.

Named pairs competitors mention without stating the values that matter:

  • 37236 + 37238Stent placement, first stent versus each additional stent, same vessel. Bundled when performed on the identical vessel/lesion; a modifier override requires genuinely separate lesions with separate access points, documented as such.
  • 36475 + 36478Radiofrequency ablation, first vein, versus laser ablation, first vein. A same-session pairing across two ablation modalities needs to reflect genuinely distinct veins treated with different technology, not a coding-template default.
  • 37187 + 37188Percutaneous mechanical thrombectomy, vein(s), versus the each-additional-vessel add-on. Relevant to dialysis circuit and other venous thrombectomy claims; report the add-on only for a genuinely additional vessel treated in the same session.
  • Dialysis-circuit angioplasty + thrombectomy (36901–36906)Structurally mutually exclusive within a session, as covered in the dialysis access section above — not a bypass-with-modifier situation, because only one code from the combined range describes the encounter.

⚠️ On specificity: we could not retrieve the current NCCI modifier indicator (0 versus 1) or the MUE Adjudication Indicator (MAI 1, 2, or 3) for any of the specific pairs named above directly from CMS's primary NCCI PTP edit file or MUE tables during this build — both the Medicare Coverage Database's underlying files and the NCCI Policy Manual PDF returned access errors to every automated retrieval attempt. Do not treat the pairs above as automatically overridable or automatically non-appealable without checking the current indicator yourself: an indicator of 0 means no modifier changes the outcome regardless of documentation, while an indicator of 1 means an override is possible with genuine distinctness; an MAI of 2 has no appeal path while an MAI of 3 does. Confirm both values for the specific pair and code in the CMS NCCI PTP Edits Lookup Tool before writing an appeal or building a scrubber rule, because these values are pair-specific and revise quarterly.

Payer coverage nuances, prior authorization, and getting paid

Medicare LCDs set a coverage floor for endovascular PAD intervention, venous ablation, and EVAR/TEVAR built around specific imaging findings, laterality- and stage-specific diagnoses, and a documented trial of conservative therapy — but commercial payers and Medicare Advantage plans routinely layer their own, often stricter, prior-authorization requirements on top rather than simply adopting the LCD as written. A commercial payer may require a longer supervised conservative-therapy trial before authorizing an elective claudication intervention than the Medicare LCD baseline calls for, may mandate a specific imaging modality (duplex before angiography, for instance) as a prerequisite, or may require a peer-to-peer review before authorizing an elective EVAR or venous ablation procedure that a Medicare LCD would cover on the chart alone.

The documentation payers actually request before approving, across nearly every elective vascular procedure in this guide, comes down to the same core elements: imaging that establishes severity (duplex reflux data for ablation, ankle-brachial index or angiographic severity for PAD intervention, sizing imaging for EVAR/TEVAR), and evidence of a conservative-therapy trial or a documented clinical reason one was not attempted. Building those two elements into the pre-authorization workflow — not just the clinical documentation — is what keeps an authorized procedure from being retroactively denied on medical necessity after the fact.

ABNs matter most wherever an intervention might not meet a payer's specific coverage criteria on the date of service — an ablation without a fully charted conservative-therapy trial, or a repeat intervention inside a payer's reintervention window — and should be signed and on file before the procedure, not requested after a CO-50 or CO-151 denial arrives. Full prior-authorization requirements by procedure family, payer-specific documentation checklists, and the peer-to-peer process for elective vascular procedures are in our prior authorization guide.

Frequently asked questions

What actually changed in lower extremity revascularization coding for 2026?

Codes 37220 through 37235 were deleted outright and replaced with 46 new codes, 37254 through 37299, organized into four vascular territories instead of a flat list: iliac (37254-37262), femoropopliteal (37263-37279), tibial/peroneal (37280-37295), and a newly created inframalleolar territory (37296-37299) for below-the-ankle work that had no dedicated code before. Selection within each territory depends on the specific vessel, the technology used, and whether the lesion is straightforward stenosis or a complex, 100%-occluded lesion. If your charge master or scrubber still references the deleted range, claims are denying on invalid code before medical necessity is ever evaluated.

Is LCD L33822 the right citation for Medicare venous ablation coverage?

No. We searched CMS coverage data directly and found no LCD with that ID. The five active local coverage determinations for varicose vein treatment are L33575 (Wellpoint Federal), L34209 (Noridian Healthcare Solutions), L34082 (CGS Administrators), L34536 (WPS Insurance Corporation), and L39121 (Palmetto GBA), each titled some variation of "Treatment of Varicose Veins of the Lower Extremities." Confirm which MAC processes your Part B claims before citing any LCD number in an appeal, since citing a nonexistent policy ID undermines the whole appeal regardless of how strong the clinical argument is.

Can we bill atherectomy and a stent on the same vessel in the same session?

Not as two separately payable codes on the same vessel in most cases. The 2026 territory-based code families are built as a hierarchy: within a given vessel, a single code captures the highest-intensity technique used, whether that's angioplasty, atherectomy, or stent placement, and the code descriptors are structured so that a more intensive technique's code already accounts for the less intensive ones performed to get there. Reporting atherectomy and a stent as two separate line items on the identical vessel is a stacking pattern that denies as bundled far more often than it pays, and appending a bypass modifier without a genuinely separate lesion or vessel to justify it does not fix that.

Do dialysis circuit angioplasty and thrombectomy codes bundle together?

They're built to be mutually exclusive within a session, not just edited against each other after the fact. The angioplasty-only family (36901-36903) and the thrombectomy family (36904-36906) are both internally hierarchical, and only one code from the combined 36901-36906 set is reportable per session per patient, because each code in the set is defined to include the lesser interventions performed to get to it. Add-on codes 36907-36909 layer onto whichever base code applies. A claim billing both a 3690x angioplasty code and a separate 3690x thrombectomy code the same session is reporting the same encounter twice, not two distinct services.

What ICD-10 specificity does PAD endovascular intervention need for Medicare coverage?

Laterality and clinical stage both matter, not just the presence of atherosclerosis. I70.209 (unspecified atherosclerosis of native arteries of the extremities, unspecified extremity) is valid for HIPAA transactions but carries none of the severity information a coverage determination is built to check. The specific alternatives run from I70.211-I70.213 (intermittent claudication) through I70.221-I70.223 (rest pain), I70.231-I70.249 (ulceration, coded by leg and wound site), I70.261-I70.263 (gangrene), and I70.92 (chronic total occlusion) for a vessel that's fully occluded rather than stenotic. An unspecified code on a PAD intervention claim is one of the fastest routes to a CO-50 denial that a specific, already-documented stage would have cleared.

How do we know if an NCCI edit on a vascular pair can be appealed?

Two separate CMS values decide it, and neither is a guess. The NCCI modifier indicator on the specific column-1/column-2 pair tells you whether any modifier can bypass the edit at all: an indicator of 0 means no modifier changes the outcome, full stop, while an indicator of 1 means a modifier can bypass it only with documentation showing the second service was genuinely distinct. Separately, the MUE Adjudication Indicator on a capped add-on code tells you whether exceeding the unit limit is appealable: MAI 2 is treated as clinically implausible with no appeal path, while MAI 3 can be appealed with documentation. Look up both values for the specific pair and code in the CMS NCCI Edits Lookup Tool before writing the appeal, because the values are pair-specific and change quarterly.

What's the difference between a CO-97 and a CO-151 denial in vascular surgery?

CO-97 (and its close relative CO-234) means the code is bundled into another service already paid on the claim, typically an NCCI column-2 code or a service the primary procedure's code already includes, such as a lesser technique folded into a higher-intensity territory code. CO-151 is a frequency denial, meaning the payer's records show the service was billed more often than its coverage policy allows in the relevant window, such as ablation retreatment inside the LCDs' typical 12-month reintervention interval or a hemodialysis session count exceeding a Frequency of Hemodialysis LCD. A bundling denial is fixed by confirming the modifier indicator and documenting distinctness; a frequency denial is fixed by confirming the elapsed interval and, if the claim was submitted too early, resubmitting rather than appealing.

Do all commercial payers follow the Medicare LCD baseline for angioplasty and stent prior authorization?

No, and treating a Medicare LCD as the commercial answer is one of the more expensive assumptions in vascular billing. Medicare LCDs set a coverage floor for endovascular PAD intervention, venous ablation, and EVAR/TEVAR built around specific imaging findings and a documented trial of conservative therapy, but commercial payers and Medicare Advantage plans routinely layer their own prior-authorization requirements on top, sometimes requiring a longer conservative-therapy trial, a specific imaging modality, or a peer-to-peer review before an elective procedure is authorized. Verify the specific payer's prior-auth policy for the exact CPT family before scheduling, rather than assuming Medicare's LCD criteria satisfy a commercial plan's requirements.
