Internal medicine billing and coding: the complete 2026 guide.

Internal medicine doesn't have one hard billing problem — it has five running at once on the same patient every month: E/M leveling for someone with three or four active chronic conditions, a care-management program stack (CCM, TCM, AWV, RPM, BHI, PCM) that collides with itself if nobody's tracking which code excludes which, diagnosis specificity that Medicare's HCC model actually pays on, and same-day EKG and injection bundling that trips scrubbers weekly. This guide covers the full revenue cycle for the specialty, with the concurrent care-management exclusion matrix and the CARC-to-appeal pairing that the other guides in this space skip entirely.

Reviewed by Phares A., MD · Physiotherapist, CPC (AAPC) · Updated August 2026

Key takeaways

  • CCM and TCM cannot be billed for the same patient in the same calendar month, full stop. Practices running care-management programs at volume hit this exclusion constantly and it's rarely built into the scheduling or billing workflow.
  • 99417 and G2212 trigger at different time thresholds — CPT's own rule versus Medicare's stricter one — and billing the wrong one on the wrong payer is a clean, avoidable line-item denial.
  • E11.22 (diabetes with diabetic CKD) is incomplete without an N18.x stage code. Combination codes still carry "use additional code" instructional notes, and a claim missing the stage code reads as underspecified even though the primary code looks complete.
  • Chronic care management has no MAC-specific LCD at all — we confirmed this directly against the CMS Coverage Database, which returned zero local coverage documents for CCM. It's governed nationally, and "check your MAC's LCD" is the wrong instinct for this code family.

Internal medicine billing at a glance

Four structural problems drive most of internal medicine's denial and underpayment volume: E/M leveling on multi-comorbidity patients where documentation habitually lags the actual complexity of the visit, a care-management code stack with real exclusion rules that nobody publishes as a single matrix, diagnosis specificity that both Medicare LCDs and the HCC risk-adjustment model use as hard gates rather than formalities, and routine same-day bundling collisions on EKG, spirometry, and injection codes. Four 2026 developments matter operationally for internal medicine, more than for almost any other specialty, because IM lives on E/M volume and risk-adjusted chronic-condition documentation.

E/M coding and MDM leveling for multi-chronic-condition visits (99202–99215)

The classic internal medicine visit — a patient with diabetes, hypertension, hyperlipidemia, and maybe COPD or CKD, all being actively managed at once — is exactly the case the 2021 E/M guideline revision was built around, and it's also where coders most reliably underlevel. Established-patient visits (99212–99215) are billed on either total time or medical decision making, never both together — fully satisfying either pathway justifies the code, and you pick whichever one the documentation actually supports.

Established-patient E/M time ranges (total time, face-to-face plus same-day non-face-to-face work).
CodeTime rangeNew-patient equivalentTime range

MDM leveling on problem complexity. Under the current guidelines, "number and complexity of problems addressed" counts differently depending on status: two or more stable chronic illnesses supports moderate complexity on its own. One chronic illness with exacerbation, progression, or side effects of treatment also reaches moderate — and a chronic illness with severe exacerbation, progression, or treatment side effects, or an acute/chronic condition posing a threat to life or bodily function, reaches high. This is the exact distinction coders miss: three stable, well-controlled chronic conditions is moderate MDM by problem count alone, but the same patient with one of those three genuinely decompensating — an A1c that jumped two points, a GFR that dropped a CKD stage, blood pressure that's no longer controlled on the current regimen — pushes the visit toward high complexity, and the note has to say so explicitly, not just list the diagnosis again.

Data reviewed and analyzed is scored across three categories: reviewing prior external notes/tests/orders and independent historian input (category 1), independent interpretation of a test performed by another provider (category 2), and discussion of management or test interpretation with an external physician, other qualified health professional, or appropriate source (category 3). Moderate-complexity data generally requires meeting a threshold across category 1 elements or hitting category 2 or 3 once; high-complexity data typically requires a combination reaching a higher threshold, or category 3 discussion plus independent interpretation. For a multi-chronic-condition IM visit, reviewing recent labs, an outside cardiology note, and discussing an abnormal result with the ordering specialist by phone is a realistic way to reach high-complexity data — and it's routinely under-documented because the phone discussion never makes it into the note as a discrete, dated entry.

Risk of complications, morbidity, or mortality is the third MDM element, and it's where prescription drug management usually lands: moderate risk includes prescription drug management itself (starting, stopping, or adjusting a medication) as well as a decision regarding minor surgery with identified patient/procedure risk factors. High risk requires drug therapy requiring intensive monitoring for toxicity, or a decision regarding hospitalization, or parenteral controlled substances. Adjusting insulin, titrating an anticoagulant, or starting a medication that needs lab monitoring for toxicity (methotrexate, lithium, certain diuretic-ACE inhibitor combinations in a patient with reduced renal function) genuinely reaches high risk — but only if the note documents the monitoring plan, not just the prescription itself.

Pro tip

The downcoding trap that costs IM practices real revenue: a coder sees three chronic diagnoses on the assessment and defaults to 99214 as the "safe" multi-problem code, without checking whether one condition is exacerbating (pushing problem complexity to high) or whether the data or risk elements independently clear the high-complexity bar. Run a monthly sample of 99214s against the actual note text for a documented exacerbation, an intensive-monitoring drug, or a hospitalization decision — that sample reliably finds 99215-supportable visits billed a level down.

Prolonged services fold in once the visit runs past the level-5 time range, and this is where the split between CPT and Medicare gets missed constantly. CPT's own guidance ties add-on code 99417 to the minimum time of the level-5 code (60 minutes for 99205, 40 minutes for 99215) plus 15 minutes before the first unit is reportable. Medicare uses its own code, G2212, instead of 99417, and sets a stricter threshold tied to the maximum of the level-5 range (74 minutes for 99205, 54 minutes for 99215) plus 15 minutes. ⚠️ These specific minute thresholds are the industry-standard figures reported consistently across coding references, but this build could not open CMS's own MLN or PFS Final Rule text directly to re-confirm them against the primary source (CMS.gov returned access errors to automated retrieval); verify the current thresholds in the CMS PFS Look-Up Tool or your MAC's E/M billing guidance before building a scrubber rule against them. The practical rule that doesn't change: bill 99417 on commercial payers following straight CPT time rules, bill G2212 on Medicare claims, and never mix the two on the same claim. The full time-vs-MDM decision tree, worked examples for a 3- and 4-comorbidity visit, and the complete prolonged-services logic are in our E/M coding and MDM leveling guide.

Chronic care management billing (99490, 99439, 99487, 99489, 99491, 99437)

CCM is the highest-volume care-management program in internal medicine and also the one with the most internally confusing code family, because three separate code pairs cover overlapping ground and are mutually exclusive with each other for the same patient in the same calendar month.

The CCM code family, by who performs the time and what it covers.
CodeWho performs the timeThresholdNotes
99490Clinical staff, under general supervisionFirst 20 minutes/monthStandard (non-complex) CCM; base code for the family
99439Clinical staffEach additional 20 minutes/monthAdd-on to 99490 only; typically capped at 2 units/month
99487Clinical staffFirst 60 minutes/monthComplex CCM — requires moderate- or high-complexity MDM and substantial care-plan revision, not just time
99489Clinical staffEach additional 30 minutes/monthAdd-on to 99487 only
99491Physician or NPP personally, not clinical staffFirst 30 minutes/monthMutually exclusive with 99490/99439/99487/99489 for the same patient, same month
99437Physician or NPP personallyEach additional 30 minutes/monthAdd-on to 99491 only

The exclusion rule that gets missed: you pick one track per patient per month — either the clinical-staff-time codes (99490/99439, or 99487/99489 for complex cases) or the physician/NPP-personal-time codes (99491/99437) — not a mix. A practice that has a nurse log 15 minutes of care-coordination time and then has the physician personally spend another 20 minutes the same month cannot combine those into either code family's threshold; the time has to belong entirely to one track for that patient that month.

Eligibility requires two or more chronic conditions expected to last at least 12 months or until the patient's death, each of which places the patient at significant risk of death, acute exacerbation/decompensation, or functional decline. Documentation requirements that actually decide whether a CCM claim survives audit: patient consent (written or verbal, but the verbal version has to be documented in the record with the date), a comprehensive care plan that's more than a diagnosis list — it needs goals, interventions, and the care team involved — and a contemporaneous, dated monthly time log that adds up to the threshold claimed, not a retroactive reconstruction at the end of the month.

CCM can't start from nothing. A face-to-face visit that addresses the qualifying chronic conditions — typically a comprehensive E/M, an AWV, or the IPPE — has to occur before or at CCM initiation for a patient new to the program with that practitioner; you can't enroll a patient in CCM sight-unseen off a chart review alone. "Clinical staff" performing the time under 99490/99439/99487/99489 means auxiliary personnel employed by, or contracted to, the billing practitioner and working under general supervision — a nurse or medical assistant on staff, not an outside care-management vendor billing independently unless the arrangement is structured correctly as incident-to work. The care plan itself has to be accessible to the care team at all times and shared with the patient or caregiver, typically via the patient portal, a printed copy, or fax; a care plan that exists only in the physician's private notes doesn't satisfy the requirement even if it's thorough.

  • 1Incomplete monthly time documentation. The single most common CCM denial driver. A time log that shows individual entries but doesn't clearly sum to 20 (or 60, for complex CCM) minutes for the billed month reads as unsupported, even if the total work genuinely happened.
  • 2Missing or undated consent. Verbal consent is allowed, but it has to be documented at the time it was obtained, including that the patient understood cost-sharing may apply. A consent note added after the fact, or with no date, is a fixable-in-appeal problem if the actual conversation happened — but it has to be rebuilt from real documentation, not backfilled.
  • 3No qualifying initiating visit on file. Enrolling a patient in CCM without a documented comprehensive visit that addressed the chronic conditions first is a structural gap that surfaces on audit even when every subsequent monthly claim looks clean.

A worked example of the track rule in practice: a patient with diabetes, hypertension, and stage 3 CKD has a medical assistant spend 12 minutes reviewing home glucose logs and coordinating a nephrology referral, and separately the physician personally spends 10 minutes that same month adjusting the care plan after reviewing labs. That's 22 minutes of real work, but it can't be combined into a single CCM claim — it has to be billed entirely under the clinical-staff track (99490, since the staff time alone doesn't reach 20 minutes on its own here without the physician's time counting toward it under that track's rules) or restructured so one practitioner category performs the qualifying time. Getting this distinction wrong at the workflow level, not the coding level, is why CCM revenue leaks even at practices that know the rule exists.

Because CCM is billed monthly and per-patient at real volume in a typical internal medicine panel, this is the code family where a scheduling and time-logging system pays for itself fastest. The full code-by-code billing rules, consent language templates, and care-plan documentation checklist are in our chronic care management billing guide.

Transitional care management billing (99495, 99496)

TCM covers the 30-day period immediately following a discharge from an inpatient hospital, observation stay, skilled nursing facility, or partial hospitalization, and it's built around three required elements that all have to be met, not just documented in general terms.

  • 1Interactive contact within 2 business days of discharge. Phone, video, or in-person — attempted and unsuccessful contact doesn't count; it has to actually connect with the patient or caregiver. This starts the clock on the whole episode.
  • 2Face-to-face visit within the code's window. 99495 (moderate-complexity MDM) requires the visit within 14 calendar days of discharge. 99496 (high-complexity MDM) requires it within 7 calendar days. The visit itself can't be billed as a separate E/M — it's bundled into the TCM code.
  • 3MDM complexity spanning the 30-day period, not just the face-to-face visit. The complexity level (moderate for 99495, high for 99496) reflects the overall medical decision making involved in managing the patient's transition, which the face-to-face visit anchors but doesn't have to single-handedly demonstrate.

Beyond the three required elements, the TCM service period itself covers non-face-to-face care management that has to actually happen, not just be available: medication reconciliation and management no later than the face-to-face visit, review of the discharge summary and any pending diagnostic tests or treatments, education of the patient, family, or caregiver, and coordination with community and social services the patient needs during the transition. The interactive contact doesn't have to be performed by the billing practitioner personally — clinical staff can make it under the practitioner's direction — but the face-to-face visit itself must be performed by the billing physician or qualified health professional, not delegated to staff.

Two scenarios end a TCM episode early and change what's billable. If the patient is readmitted within the 30-day period before the required face-to-face visit occurs, the TCM service for that discharge generally cannot be completed as originally opened, and a new TCM episode starts from the new discharge instead. If the patient dies during the 30-day period, TCM billing depends on whether the required elements were completed before death; work performed but never completing the full requirement set is not separately billable as TCM. Neither scenario is a documentation failure on your part — both are simply reasons the TCM claim doesn't go out, and coders who bill it anyway create a denial that then requires a correction rather than an appeal.

TCM cannot be billed the same calendar month as CCM for the same patient — if a discharge and a TCM episode fall in a month where CCM time was also being tracked, the CCM time for that patient doesn't get billed that month; either close out CCM for the prior month before discharge or resume it the month after the TCM episode ends. The two most common TCM denial patterns are billing before the required face-to-face visit has actually occurred — the claim can't go out until day 7 or day 14 has passed and the visit is documented — and downcoding 99496 to 99495 because the note doesn't clearly establish high-complexity MDM, which usually happens when the discharge diagnosis was serious but the follow-up note reads like a routine visit rather than documenting the ongoing complexity of the transition. A third, quieter pattern: the discharge date on the claim doesn't match the facility's own discharge claim, which payers cross-reference — confirm the actual discharge date against the hospital's records before opening the TCM episode, not the date your practice was notified. Full requirements, a sample interactive-contact script, and the complete denial-and-appeal pattern for TCM are in our transitional care management billing guide.

Annual wellness visit billing (G0402, G0438, G0439) and combining with problem visits

Three HCPCS codes cover Medicare's preventive visit family, and they're sequence- and calendar-gated in ways that generate a predictable denial pattern when billed out of order.

Medicare preventive visit codes and their gating rules.
CodeServiceGate
G0402Initial Preventive Physical Examination ("Welcome to Medicare" visit)One-time only, must occur within the first 12 months of Part B enrollment
G0438Initial Annual Wellness VisitOnce per lifetime; requires the patient to have had Part B coverage for at least 12 months before this visit (independent of whether G0402 was ever performed)
G0439Subsequent Annual Wellness VisitAnnual; requires at least 11 full months to have passed since the last AWV (G0438 or a prior G0439)

Both G0402 and G0438/G0439 require a health risk assessment and a personalized prevention plan — the AWV is not a physical exam in the traditional sense, and billing it as one without the HRA and prevention-plan components on file is a coverage gap even if the visit itself was thorough clinically. The two denial patterns that show up constantly: billing G0439 before G0438 has ever been performed (the subsequent-visit code requires an initial AWV on record first), and billing any AWV within 365 days of the patient's last one, measured from the actual date of service, not the calendar month.

The health risk assessment itself has defined components, and a shortened in-house version that skips one is the quiet reason an AWV fails audit even though the visit happened: demographic data, self-reported health status, psychosocial and behavioral risk factors, activities-of-daily-living and instrumental-ADL functional status, and a validated screening tool result for each of these where applicable. The personalized prevention plan built from the HRA has to include, at minimum, a screening schedule covering the next 5–10 years appropriate to the patient's age and risk factors, a list of risk factors and conditions with recommended treatment options, and a current list of the patient's other providers and suppliers of care. Starting with the initial AWV, a cognitive assessment for signs of impairment is a required component, not optional — a brief structured screen, not a full neuropsychological workup, but it has to be documented as having been performed and its result recorded.

Advance care planning (99497, and its add-on 99498 for each additional 30 minutes) can be billed on the same day as an AWV, and when it is, Medicare waives the patient's deductible and coinsurance for that specific advance-care-planning service — a detail worth building into the visit workflow, since it changes the patient's out-of-pocket exposure and is worth mentioning at scheduling. Outside that specific pairing, advance care planning billed on a different day, or alongside a problem-oriented E/M instead of the AWV, doesn't carry the same cost-sharing waiver.

Combining an AWV with a same-day problem-oriented E/M is common and billable — append modifier 25 to the E/M — but the two services have to be kept genuinely separate in the note. The AWV's HRA, risk factors, and cognitive/functional screening can't be recycled as the E/M's history, and the time or MDM elements used to justify the AWV can't also be counted toward the E/M level; if a physician manages a diabetes medication adjustment during the same visit, that management has to be documented and leveled on its own, with its own assessment-and-plan content distinct from the AWV's prevention plan. Full HRA component checklist, prevention-plan documentation requirements, and same-day E/M split logic are in our annual wellness visit billing guide.

Concurrent care-management billing: CCM, TCM, AWV, RPM, BHI, and PCM overlap rules

This is the section every competitor guide in this space skips, and it's the one that costs internal medicine practices real, recurring revenue — not because the individual codes are hard, but because a typical panel patient qualifies for three or four of these programs simultaneously, and the exclusion rules between them aren't published anywhere as a single reference.

Concurrent-billing rules across internal medicine's care-management code families, same patient, same calendar month.
PairCan they be billed together?Rule

CCM (staff-time track) + CCM (physician-time track)No99490/99439/99487/99489 and 99491/99437 are two separate tracks for the same underlying service — pick one per patient per month, never both.

The financial impact of getting this wrong runs in both directions. Billing an excluded pair (CCM + TCM, or CCM on both tracks) gets one of the two claims denied and, if it happens repeatedly, invites payer scrutiny of the whole care-management program. But the more common and more silent loss is under-billing: practices that know CCM and RPM can theoretically coexist but never actually staff and log the two time buckets separately, so RPM time never gets billed at all even though the monitoring is happening. A patient on CCM for diabetes and hypertension, discharged after a COPD exacerbation, and enrolled in RPM for home blood pressure monitoring is not a hypothetical — it's a normal IM panel patient, and getting the sequencing right across a single calendar month (TCM opens, CCM pauses, TCM closes, CCM resumes, RPM runs throughout) is the operational skill this section exists to teach.

Behavioral health integration (99484) deserves its own note because it's the pair most often billed incorrectly in either direction. BHI requires at least 20 minutes of clinical staff time per calendar month directed by the treating physician or NPP, covering an initial assessment, systematic caseload review with a psychiatric consultant where applicable, and brief behavioral interventions. A patient with diabetes, hypertension, and major depressive disorder can legitimately be on both CCM (for the physical chronic conditions) and BHI (for the depression-focused care coordination) in the same month, but the two require separate, non-overlapping time logs — a nurse can't log the same 20-minute phone call toward both codes' thresholds just because the conversation touched on medication adherence for both the metformin and the sertraline.

A full-month walk-through makes the sequencing concrete. Week 1: patient is discharged from the hospital after a COPD exacerbation — TCM opens, interactive contact happens within 2 business days, face-to-face visit is scheduled inside the required window. CCM time for this patient is not logged or billed for this calendar month at all, even though the diabetes and hypertension are still being managed in the background. Week 3: RPM device (home blood pressure cuff) continues transmitting readings throughout the month regardless of the TCM episode — RPM and TCM are not mutually exclusive, so 99453/99454/99457 billing continues on its own timeline. The following month, once the TCM 30-day period has closed, CCM resumes and that month's clinical staff time is logged and billed normally. Building this sequence into the practice management system as an automated hold-and-resume rule, rather than relying on billing staff to remember it patient by patient, is the single highest-leverage fix in this section.

ICD-10 specificity for multi-morbidity patients and HCC risk adjustment

Internal medicine's diagnosis coding gets treated by most competitor content as a category-range exercise — "code E11.x for diabetes, I10–I16 for hypertension, N18.x for CKD" — but the codes that actually decide medical necessity and risk-adjustment payment are combination codes with instructional requirements that a category range doesn't capture.

Verified against the FY2026 ICD-10-CM code set. Combination-code requirements that drive denials when missed.

The pattern across all three families: a category-level code that "sounds specific" — E11.22, I12.9, I13.10 — is still incomplete on its own where ICD-10-CM's own instructional notes require a companion code. Coders who stop at the combination code because it already mentions the second condition are the most common source of this specific denial, because the claim doesn't look obviously wrong at a glance.

Two more comorbidity families carry the same trap for a typical IM panel. COPD splits into J44.0 (with acute lower respiratory infection), J44.1 (with acute exacerbation), and J44.9 (unspecified, no acute component) — billing J44.9 for a patient actually being managed through an exacerbation understates both medical necessity for the visit's complexity and the condition's risk-adjustment weight. Depression coded alongside a chronic physical illness follows the same logic: F32.9 (single episode, unspecified) and F33.1 (recurrent, moderate) are both valid, but a patient with a documented history of prior depressive episodes coded as F32.9 repeatedly, rather than the recurrent-episode code once that history is established, is under-specified in exactly the way that erodes an HCC score over time without ever generating a denial to flag it.

Status codes (Z-codes) support medical necessity for the monitoring services built around them. Z79.4 (long-term insulin use) on a diabetic patient's chart supports the medical necessity of glucose-monitoring supply orders and A1c-frequency decisions; BMI codes in the Z68 range (for example, Z68.41, BMI 40.0–44.9 in an adult) pair with the underlying obesity diagnosis (E66.x) to support weight-management counseling codes and some payers' obesity-related coverage policies. These are easy to leave off a claim because they don't feel like "the diagnosis" for the visit, but their absence is a common, quiet reason a related service denies as not medically necessary.

HCC risk adjustment adds a second, separate specificity trap. Under the CMS-HCC model, only codes that map to a payable HCC category contribute to a patient's risk score, and unspecified codes routinely fail to map where a specific code in the same family does. E11.9 (type 2 diabetes without complications) and I50.9 (heart failure, unspecified) are both valid, billable codes, but neither carries the same risk-adjustment weight as a specific complication or type code where one is documented and supported. Chronic conditions also have to be re-documented and re-coded at least annually to remain on a patient's active risk-adjustment sweep — a condition coded once in a prior year and never re-addressed in the current year's notes effectively drops off, even though the patient still has it. ⚠️ Specific HCC category numbers, weights, and the V28-versus-V24 mapping differences for individual codes could not be confirmed against a primary CMS HCC crosswalk in this build (no live HCC-mapping connector is available and CMS's risk-adjustment model files returned access errors); confirm current-year HCC mapping for any specific code against your MA payer's or CMS's own current-year model documentation before using it in coding education materials. Modifiers internal medicine practices actually use

Four modifiers carry real weight in internal medicine, and each maps to a specific, recurring claim scenario rather than a general rule.

25
  • Significant, separately identifiable E/M on the same day as a preventive service (AWV) or a minor procedure. The highest-volume use in IM is AWV + sick visit.
  • Denial pattern: payer system flags the two claim lines as duplicative because the same diagnosis appears on both — happens when the note doesn't clearly wall off preventive content from problem-focused content.
59 / XE, XS, XP, XU
  • Distinct procedural service, used most often in IM on same-day in-office procedures — injections, EKGs, spirometry — that trip an NCCI edit against another same-day code.
  • Denial pattern: appended routinely to "make a claim pass" without documentation of a genuinely separate encounter, site, or practitioner — a reliable audit trigger regardless of whether any single claim was correct.
24 (rare in IM)
  • Unrelated E/M by the same physician during another provider's 90-day global period — genuinely uncommon in general IM since the specialty does little major surgery, but shows up when an IM physician sees a patient during a surgeon's post-op window for an unrelated chronic-condition visit.
  • Denial pattern: omitted entirely because the visit "isn't related to surgery" in the physician's mind, but the claims system still needs the modifier to separate it from the global period.
95 / 93 / FQ (telehealth)
  • 95 — synchronous audio-video telehealth. 93 — synchronous audio-only telehealth (used where audio-only is permitted for the service). FQ — identifies a service furnished using audio-only communication technology, a distinction Medicare has continued to require for certain audio-only claims.
  • Denial pattern: billing a telehealth visit with the in-person place-of-service code and no modifier, or using 95 for a visit that was actually audio-only — the modifier and the place-of-service code both have to match the actual encounter format.

The through-line across all four: every one of these modifiers exists because a single CPT code covers a scenario that, in practice, splits into meaningfully different claim situations, and the modifier is the only thing on the claim that tells the payer which situation applies.

Modifier 25's actual failure point is rarely the modifier itself — it's the "significant, separately identifiable" standard behind it. A same-day injection (an intramuscular B12 injection, a joint injection, a vaccine administration) billed with an E/M and modifier 25 is defensible when the note shows real, separate cognitive work beyond what's needed to justify the injection itself; it's indefensible when the E/M note is just a restatement of the same complaint that led to the injection, with no separate history, exam, or MDM content. Payers increasingly audit modifier 25 pairs specifically by checking whether the E/M note stands on its own as a billable service if the procedure were removed — a useful internal test before submission.

Telehealth billing also depends on the place-of-service code matching the modifier, not just the modifier alone. POS 10 (patient's home) and POS 02 (other, non-home telehealth location) carry different reimbursement implications on Medicare claims, and a claim with modifier 95 but an in-person POS code, or the reverse, is an internally inconsistent claim that denies regardless of whether the visit itself was appropriate. Confirm the POS-and-modifier pairing your specific MAC or commercial payer expects before batch-submitting telehealth claims, since this pairing has changed more than once as telehealth policy has evolved since 2020.

NCCI edits and bundling: EKG, spirometry, and in-office procedures vs. E/M

Two CMS values decide whether a same-day bundling collision in internal medicine can even be resolved with a modifier, and neither is a guess — both are pair-specific, code-specific values published by CMS and updated quarterly.

The NCCI modifier indicator attached to a Column 1/Column 2 code pair:

  • 0Never bypassable. No modifier, however well-documented, overrides it. If the pair carries a 0, the second code simply isn't separately payable.
  • 1Bypassable with documentation. An NCCI-associated modifier (59 or the specific X-modifier) can override the edit, but only where the record shows the second service was genuinely distinct — separate site, separate session, separate structure.
  • 9Edit deleted. The pair no longer applies.

The MUE Adjudication Indicator (MAI) attached to a per-code, per-day unit cap:

  • 1Claim-line edit. Units above the cap deny that line, but can sometimes be split across separate lines with the correct modifier and documentation.
  • 2Absolute, date-of-service edit. CMS treats exceeding it as clinically implausible. No appeal path exists — confirm the MAI before staff spend time writing one.
  • 3Appealable, date-of-service edit. A real path exists with documentation showing excess units reflect genuinely distinct, medically necessary services.

Applied to internal medicine's actual collisions: the EKG family (93000 global, 93005 tracing-only, 93010 interpretation-only) is mutually exclusive within itself for a single test on a single date — billing more than one of the three for the same EKG is the most common bundling error in this family, not a conflict with the same-day E/M. A genuine repeat EKG on the same date — the patient develops new chest pain mid-visit and a second tracing is medically necessary — is a modifier 76 (repeat procedure, same physician) scenario, not a 59/X-modifier scenario, since it's a true repeat of the identical service rather than a distinct one. Diagnostic EKGs and spirometry are generally separately payable from an E/M performed the same day, because they're distinct diagnostic services rather than bundled components of the visit; the actual collision points in IM are between the diagnostic test's own component codes (as above), and between minor in-office procedures — injection administration (96372 and similar), vaccine administration — and the E/M, where modifier 25 on the E/M, not modifier 59 on the injection, is usually the correct fix.

Spirometry billing (94010 for a basic spirometry, 94060 for spirometry pre- and post-bronchodilator administration) has its own internal edit logic worth knowing before appending anything: 94060 already includes the pre- and post-bronchodilator measurements as a single global service, so billing 94010 separately for the same encounter's baseline reading is a component-of-the-whole error, the same category of mistake as double-billing the EKG family. Injection administration codes (96372 for a therapeutic/prophylactic injection) carry their own MUE unit caps per date of service; a patient receiving two separate, medically distinct injections in the same visit needs the units and any required modifier applied correctly to each line, not a blanket 59 appended to the second line without documenting which specific injection it identifies.

⚠️ The specific current NCCI modifier-indicator and MAI values for individual internal medicine code pairs (EKG-family internal edits, spirometry component edits, injection-administration-versus-E/M pairs) could not be confirmed against CMS's primary NCCI PTP and MUE files during this build; CMS's NCCI Policy Manual PDFs and PTP/MUE lookup files returned access errors to automated retrieval. Confirm the specific pair's indicator and MAI in the CMS NCCI PTP Edits Lookup Tool and the MUE tables before building any of the logic above into a scrubber rule — the framework is CMS policy structure and won't change, but individual pair values are updated quarterly and this build could not re-verify the current quarter's values directly. A usable internal medicine appeal letter follows the same structure regardless of which CARC triggered it: state the CPT/HCPCS code and date of service being appealed; name the specific denial reason and the CARC/RARC code exactly as it appeared on the remittance; state the specific standard the service met (the MDM elements, the time threshold, the combination-code specificity, the consent and time-log dates); attach the supporting documentation referenced by date, not as a generic chart dump; and close with the specific action requested — reprocess at the billed code, or reprocess at a stated corrected code. Appeals that skip the "specific standard met" step and just restate that the service was medically necessary have a materially lower overturn rate than ones that cite the actual guideline or threshold by name.

Per CPT medical decision making guidelines, this combination supports [level] complexity independent of total time documented." Sample opening for a CCM time-documentation denial: "Attached is the complete clinical staff time log for [month], totaling [X] minutes across [N] dated entries, meeting the 20-minute threshold for CPT 99490. Both work because they name the specific standard met, not just the fact that the service happened.

Payer and coverage nuances: Medicare, Medicare Advantage, and commercial

Traditional Medicare, Medicare Advantage, and commercial payers diverge in ways internal medicine practices have to track code-family by code-family, not payer by payer in general.

Traditional Medicare vs. Medicare Advantage. CCM, TCM, and AWV all follow CMS's national billing rules for traditional Medicare fee-for-service. Medicare Advantage plans are required to cover the same underlying services but frequently administer their own version — particularly for the AWV, which some MA plans fold into their own branded wellness-visit program, sometimes billed under different codes or bundled with the plan's own risk-adjustment data-collection visit. Confirm the specific MA plan's coding requirements before assuming G0402/G0438/G0439 apply exactly as they do under traditional Medicare; a claim built for FFS Medicare rules submitted to an MA plan that wants its own code is a straightforward, avoidable denial.

MAC jurisdiction — a distinction worth calling out explicitly. Internal medicine content usually assumes every Medicare coverage question is a "check your MAC's LCD" question. It isn't. We searched the CMS Coverage Database directly for local coverage documents on chronic care management and found zero LCDs or billing-and-coding articles — CCM billing rules come from CMS's national program instructions (the CCM services MLN reference, MLN909188) and CPT's own code definitions, not from any MAC-specific policy. The same search returned zero MAC-specific coverage documents for annual wellness visits and transitional care management. By contrast, EKG billing genuinely is MAC-specific: we confirmed an active Noridian Healthcare Solutions LCD (L34315, "Electrocardiograms," effective 11/06/2025) with its companion billing-and-coding article (A57326, same effective date), and a CGS Administrators LCD specifically for transtelephonic spirometry (L34541, effective 10/02/2025) — note this is transtelephonic spirometry specifically, not in-office spirometry generally; our search for a general "pulmonary function" LCD returned no results, so confirm your MAC's specific coverage posture for in-office spirometry directly rather than assuming a parallel LCD exists. Know which code family you're dealing with before reflexively pulling an LCD that doesn't exist.

Commercial payer AWV-equivalent variance. Commercial plans don't use G0402/G0438/G0439 at all — most have their own preventive-visit CPT codes (the 99381–99397 preventive medicine family) with different documentation requirements and no Medicare-style HRA mandate baked into the code itself, though many plans have adopted similar risk-assessment expectations for value-based contracts. Never bill a commercial plan using the Medicare AWV HCPCS codes; map each payer's preventive-visit benefit to its own code set at enrollment, not at claim submission.

Medicaid managed care and dual-eligible patients add a third layer most competitor content skips entirely. State Medicaid programs frequently carve care-management codes in or out of coverage differently than traditional Medicare, and a patient's specific Medicaid managed-care plan may impose its own prior-authorization or frequency rule on top of the state's baseline policy. Dual-eligible patients (Medicare primary, Medicaid secondary) add a coordination-of-benefits step where a service Medicare covers in full may still generate a secondary claim to Medicaid for cost-sharing — getting the crossover claim sequencing wrong is a common source of denied secondary claims that has nothing to do with whether the primary Medicare claim was coded correctly.

Prior authorization for internal medicine referrals and imaging

Internal medicine's prior-auth exposure concentrates in three areas: advanced imaging ordered directly from the office (CT, MRI, and increasingly certain higher-cost ultrasound studies), specialty medication referrals (biologics, certain injectables, and drugs requiring step-therapy documentation before a specialist visit even happens), and durable medical equipment orders (CPAP, home oxygen, mobility equipment) that route through DME MACs with their own documentation requirements separate from the referring physician's own claim.

A complete imaging prior-auth request that clears review the first time generally needs four elements on the order: the specific clinical indication in the patient's own words or the physician's assessment, documentation that conservative or first-line treatment was tried and failed where the payer's policy expects a step-therapy history (a lumbar MRI order without documented conservative management first is a common denial even when the clinical need is real), the relevant exam and lab findings supporting the indication, and the specific CPT code being requested rather than a general description of "an MRI." Missing any one of these is the most common reason a prior-auth request gets kicked back for more information rather than approved or denied outright, which quietly extends the turnaround time far more than an outright denial would.

Some payers and states operate "gold-carding" programs that exempt high-performing practices — those with a strong track record of approved requests for a specific service — from prior authorization on that service going forward. If your practice orders a particular imaging study or DME category at volume, check whether your top payers offer a gold-carding pathway; it's underused because it requires practices to actively apply rather than being granted automatically. Where a service is likely to be non-covered and no gold-carding exemption applies, an Advance Beneficiary Notice signed before the service protects the practice's ability to bill the patient if Medicare denies it — get it signed before the study or referral, not after a denial arrives.

The 2026 context that matters operationally: CMS's broader interoperability and prior-authorization push is moving payers toward standardized electronic API-based submission and faster turnaround commitments, with a commonly cited 72-hour standard for urgent request decisions. ⚠️ This build could not confirm the exact compliance timeline by payer type against a primary CMS source (CMS.gov prior-authorization rule pages returned access errors to automated retrieval during this build); the 2027 broader-compliance horizon is reported consistently in secondary and specialty-society sources, but confirm the specific effective date applicable to each payer you bill — Medicare Advantage plans, state Medicaid programs, and CMS-regulated marketplace plans are on different implementation tracks — before building a workflow around a specific date. Until standardized electronic submission is fully in place across your payer mix, the operational fix that actually reduces denials is upstream: build the imaging or DME order with the payer's current documentation requirements attached at order entry, not after a denial forces a resubmission, and track authorization expiration dates the same way you'd track a CCM or AWV eligibility window — as a date-driven rule, not a manual check.

Frequently asked questions

Can we bill CCM and TCM for the same patient in the same calendar month?

No. CMS treats chronic care management and transitional care management as mutually exclusive for the same patient in the same month, whether the same practitioner or different practitioners bill them. If a patient is discharged mid-month and you open a TCM episode, hold any CCM time logged that same month until the 30-day TCM period closes, then resume CCM the following month. Billing both in the same month is one of the more common care-management denials we see, and it is entirely avoidable with a scheduling rule rather than a coding fix.

What's the real difference between CPT 99417 and HCPCS G2212 for prolonged visits?

Both add time to a level-5 office visit (99205 or 99215) billed on total time, but they trigger at different thresholds and go on different claims. CPT's own guidance ties 99417 to the minimum time of the level-5 code plus 15 minutes. Medicare's G2212 is stricter: CMS requires total time to reach the maximum of the level-5 code's range plus 15 minutes before the first unit is billable. Bill 99417 on commercial claims that follow straight CPT rules and G2212 on Medicare claims — using 99417 on a Medicare claim is a straightforward line-item denial.

Why did our diabetes-with-CKD claim deny for insufficient specificity?

E11.22 (type 2 diabetes with diabetic chronic kidney disease) is a combination code, but ICD-10-CM coding guidelines require an additional code from category N18 to identify the CKD stage on the same claim. A claim carrying E11.22 alone, with no N18.x code, is incomplete under the guideline's own instructional note and payers reject it as insufficiently specified. The fix is a hard stop in the EHR problem list: diabetes-with-CKD documentation isn't complete until the stage is recorded and coded alongside it.

Can we bill an Annual Wellness Visit and a problem-oriented E/M on the same day?

Yes, with modifier 25 on the E/M, but the two services have to be documented as genuinely separate work. The AWV's history-and-risk-assessment elements can't double as the E/M's history, and time or MDM used to justify the AWV can't also justify the E/M level. The most common denial pattern here isn't the modifier — it's a payer system flagging the diagnosis codes as duplicative between the two claim lines because the documentation didn't clearly separate preventive content from problem-focused content.

What fixes a CO-97 denial on an EKG billed the same day as an office visit?

Confirm which EKG code was billed first. 93000 is the global code (tracing plus interpretation) and is mutually exclusive with billing 93005 (tracing only) or 93010 (interpretation only) for the same test on the same date — billing more than one of those three for a single EKG is the actual error in most CO-97 denials on this pair, not the E/M relationship. Once you've confirmed only one EKG component was billed, the E/M itself is separately payable as a distinct service; append modifier 25 to the E/M only if payer edits are treating it as bundled, and confirm the specific pair's NCCI modifier indicator before assuming an override is even allowed.

Why do our CCM claims deny with CO-16 or remark code MA130?

CO-16 with MA130 means the claim is missing information the payer needs to adjudicate it, and for CCM the usual gaps are an incomplete monthly time log, a missing or undated patient consent, or no comprehensive care plan on file at the time of billing. Fix the claim by pulling the actual clinical-staff time entries for the calendar month, confirming they sum to at least 20 minutes, and attaching or referencing the signed consent and care plan date in the resubmission or appeal. This is almost always a documentation gap, not a coverage denial, so it's winnable if the underlying time was real and logged.

Can remote physiologic monitoring (RPM) be billed alongside CCM in the same month?

Yes. RPM (99453, 99454, 99457, 99458) and CCM (99490 and its family) are not mutually exclusive and are commonly billed together for the same patient in the same month, because they represent different clinical work — RPM is device-based physiologic data review, CCM is care coordination time. The requirement is that the time counted toward each program is tracked separately and never double-counted; a single 25-minute block of staff time can support one program's threshold, not both simultaneously.

Do internal medicine practices need to choose between CCM and principal care management (PCM)?

Effectively yes, per patient per month. CCM requires two or more chronic conditions; PCM (99424–99427) is built for a single high-risk condition being actively managed or stabilized. CMS does not allow CCM and PCM to be billed concurrently for the same patient in the same calendar month, so a patient who qualifies for full CCM under two or more conditions should be enrolled there rather than in PCM, and PCM should be reserved for patients whose care-management need is genuinely limited to one condition.
