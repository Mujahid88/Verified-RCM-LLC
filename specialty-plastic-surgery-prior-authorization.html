Plastic surgery prior authorization: panniculectomy, blepharoplasty, and reconstruction.

Plastic surgery denials rarely turn on whether the procedure was performed correctly, they turn on whether the payer believed it was cosmetic. Panniculectomy, functional blepharoplasty, reduction mammaplasty, and post-mastectomy reconstruction all sit in the same trap: a CPT code that could describe either an elective procedure or a medically necessary one, reviewed by a payer whose default assumption leans cosmetic until the chart proves otherwise. This guide covers which procedures draw the heaviest scrutiny, the documentation package that flips a reviewer's default assumption, the WHCRA nuance that trips up reconstruction authorizations specifically, the CMS-0057-F deadlines that now apply to these requests, and how to run a peer-to-peer call that actually overturns a denial.

Reviewed by Phares A., MD · Physiotherapist, CPC (AAPC) · Part of our plastic surgery billing and coding guide

Key takeaways

  • Panniculectomy and blepharoplasty sit on CMS's own cosmetic-risk list. Both have required prior authorization at the hospital outpatient department since July 1, 2020, alongside botulinum toxin injections, rhinoplasty, and vein ablation, specifically because each one can be billed as either a cosmetic or a medically necessary service.
  • WHCRA mandates the coverage decision, not the paperwork. Post-mastectomy reconstruction must be covered when a plan covers mastectomy, but the plan can still require prior authorization for the specific stage, procedure, or facility being billed, and often does.
  • The functional case needs a number, not an adjective. A taped-versus-untaped visual field measurement, a Schnur sliding-scale resection weight, or two to three dated skin-infection episodes carries a submission further than a narrative description of discomfort.
  • CMS-0057-F's 7-day/72-hour clock applies here in full. The rule's carve-out is written for drug prior authorizations; a request for a covered surgical procedure like panniculectomy or reconstruction isn't a drug request, so the standard timeline binds it.

Why these four procedures draw the heaviest scrutiny

Most surgical specialties bill procedures that are unambiguously medical. Plastic surgery's core caseload includes several that aren't, the same incision, the same CPT code, and sometimes the same surgeon can describe either a covered, medically necessary service or a self-pay cosmetic one, and the difference lives entirely in the documentation, not the procedure itself. CMS made this explicit in 2020 when it built its Hospital Outpatient Department (OPD) prior-authorization program around exactly five services it identified as having a meaningful risk of being billed as medically necessary when they were performed for cosmetic reasons: blepharoplasty, botulinum toxin injections, panniculectomy, rhinoplasty, and vein ablation, effective for dates of service on or after July 1, 2020. CMS has since extended that same logic to ambulatory surgical centers through a demonstration project covering the same five service categories in a defined set of states.

The pattern across all five rows is the same: the payer isn't disputing that the surgeon can perform the procedure competently. It's asking whether the chart, independent of the operative note, proves the indication was functional rather than aesthetic. That's a documentation problem, and it's solvable before the request is ever submitted.

The documentation package that gets each procedure approved

A generic prior-authorization submission, diagnosis, CPT code, a brief note that the patient "would benefit", is the fastest way to draw a request for additional information, and every round trip adds days the CMS-0057-F clock is already counting against. Each of these four procedures has its own specific evidence a reviewer is trained to look for.

The through-line: a scored value, a dated photograph, or a named prior treatment survives a reviewer's second look. A description of how the patient feels about the result does not, because it can't be verified against anything in the chart.

Breast reconstruction: a mandated benefit that still runs through prior authorization

Reconstruction following mastectomy is the one procedure on this list where the underlying coverage question is already answered by federal law, and it's worth being precise about exactly what that law does and doesn't reach. The Women's Health and Cancer Rights Act of 1998 requires a group health plan or health insurance issuer that covers mastectomy to also cover reconstruction of the breast on which the mastectomy was performed, surgery on the other breast to produce a symmetrical appearance, prostheses, and treatment of physical complications of the mastectomy at any stage, including lymphedema, provided in consultation with the attending physician and the patient. WHCRA does not apply to Medicare or Medicaid directly, and some self-funded government and church plans are exempt, but it reaches the overwhelming majority of commercial group coverage and, through a later amendment, individual-market plans as well.

What WHCRA settles is the coverage decision: a plan cannot legally treat reconstruction as an excluded or optional benefit once it covers mastectomy. What it does not settle is the utilization-management process sitting in front of that benefit. Nothing in the statute prohibits a plan from requiring prior authorization for the specific procedure code, technique, or facility being billed for a given stage of reconstruction, and in practice, payers continue to apply exactly that process, which is why reconstruction claims still get denied as "cosmetic" or "not medically necessary" even though the coverage question was never legitimately open. The appeal argument in that situation isn't a medical-necessity argument the way it would be for an elective procedure; it's a statutory-compliance argument. Cite WHCRA by name, attach the mastectomy operative report and pathology establishing the qualifying event, and ask the plan to confirm how its determination is consistent with a benefit it is required by federal law to cover. That reframes the review from "is this necessary", a question WHCRA already answered, to "is this plan administering a mandated benefit correctly," which is a narrower and faster fight.

This distinction matters most on later-stage and revision procedures, where the connection back to the original mastectomy is easiest for a reviewer to lose. Resubmitting a capsulectomy or symmetry revision as a freestanding request, without restating the WHCRA basis and the original mastectomy history, is the single most common reason a legitimately mandated reconstruction stage gets treated like a new cosmetic request.

How fast the payer has to decide under CMS-0057-F

CMS's Interoperability and Prior Authorization Final Rule, CMS-0057-F, sets a hard clock on these requests for the payer types it covers: a standard prior-authorization decision within 7 calendar days, and an expedited (urgent) decision within 72 hours. It also requires the payer to provide a specific reason for any denial, not a boilerplate code. These operational timeline requirements took effect January 1, 2026, for Medicare Advantage organizations, Medicaid and CHIP managed care plans, and Qualified Health Plan issuers on the ACA exchanges; state Medicaid and CHIP fee-for-service programs have until January 1, 2027 to comply.

The one exclusion worth flagging by name, because it shows up in other specialties' content on this site, is drugs: CMS-0057-F carves prior authorization for drugs out of several of its requirements. That carve-out doesn't reach plastic surgery. Panniculectomy, blepharoplasty, reduction mammaplasty, and reconstruction are all procedure requests, not drug-benefit requests, so the 7-day standard and 72-hour expedited clock apply to them the same way they apply to any other covered surgical service under an in-scope plan. A plastic surgery PA sitting past 7 calendar days with no determination, on a plan this rule covers, is a compliance gap worth escalating in writing rather than a status update to wait out.

⚠️ The rule's scope and timelines above are drawn from CMS's own CMS-0057-F fact sheet; individual payer implementation, definitions of "standard" versus "expedited" for a given procedure, and any state-specific Medicaid managed care variation should still be confirmed against the specific plan's current provider manual, since operational rollout details can differ from the federal floor.

Winning the peer-to-peer when a borderline case is denied

A plastic surgery denial almost always comes back as a medical-necessity determination, which makes it tempting to use the peer-to-peer call to re-explain the surgical plan. That's the weakest version of the call. The reviewer on the other end of the line has already read the operative plan; repeating it gives them nothing new to approve against. What actually moves a borderline denial is anchoring the conversation to the specific functional or statutory basis the original submission was missing or under-supported.

What works
  • Leading with the exact number the payer's own policy asks for, the taped/untaped visual field delta, the Schnur-scale resection weight against body surface area, the dated count of intertrigo episodes, and confirming it meets or exceeds the plan's published threshold.
  • For reconstruction, citing WHCRA by name and walking through the mastectomy history that establishes eligibility, then asking the reviewer to explain how the denial is consistent with a benefit the plan is required to cover.
  • Asking the reviewer directly which specific criterion in their own policy the submission failed to meet, rather than restating the whole case, it forces a specific answer that's either addressable on the call or names exactly what the appeal needs.
What doesn't
  • Re-describing the operative plan or the patient's distress about appearance, the reviewer isn't disputing the surgical technique, and appearance-based language reinforces the cosmetic framing the denial already assumed.
  • Sending the same submission a second time without adding the specific missing element, a repeat request with no new documentation typically reproduces the same denial.
Pro tip

Request the payer's specific written policy for the procedure before the peer-to-peer, not after. Aetna, for instance, publishes its blepharoplasty visual-field thresholds in a clinical policy bulletin; many plans publish a Schnur-scale table for reduction mammaplasty. Walking into the call already quoting the plan's own numbers back to its own reviewer is a materially different conversation than asking what the plan wants after the fact.

Worked example: panniculectomy after bariatric-surgery weight loss

A typical post-bariatric panniculectomy authorization moves through a predictable sequence, and most of the avoidable delay in it happens before the request is ever submitted.

  • 1Weight stability confirmed and documented. The surgeon's office confirms the patient's weight has been stable since the bariatric procedure and documents the stabilization date, the single most common reason a panniculectomy PA is deferred is a submission made before stability is established, since payers read an unstabilized weight as a sign further changes to the pannus are still likely.
  • 2Functional documentation assembled. Dated photographs of the pannus, a log of intertrigo or cellulitis episodes with the treatments used for each, and an exam note connecting the pannus to a specific hygiene or mobility limitation are gathered into a single packet rather than referenced individually across the chart.
  • 3Prior authorization submitted with CPT 15830 (plus 15847 if umbilical transposition or fascial plication is also planned), routed as a standard request unless a specific clinical circumstance justifies expedited review.
  • 4Payer decision within the applicable clock. For a plan covered by CMS-0057-F, that's 7 calendar days for a standard request; other payers may apply their own published timeline. A determination request submitted with a complete functional packet the first time is the version of this workflow least likely to generate a request for additional records, which is the most common source of delay past that window.
  • 5If denied, peer-to-peer within days, not weeks. The call opens with the specific documented intertrigo history and stabilized weight date, asks which element of the plan's own policy the submission didn't meet, and either resolves on the call or produces the specific gap the formal appeal needs to close.

Frequently asked questions

Does breast reconstruction still need prior authorization if WHCRA requires the plan to cover it?

Yes, in most cases. The Women's Health and Cancer Rights Act requires group health plans and insurers that cover mastectomy to also cover reconstruction of the breast on which the mastectomy was performed, surgery on the other breast for symmetry, prostheses, and treatment of physical complications including lymphedema, in consultation with the attending physician and the patient. WHCRA settles the coverage question, not the utilization-management process, it doesn't prohibit a plan from requiring prior authorization for the specific CPT code, stage, or facility being billed. Submitting a mastectomy-history-and-WHCRA-eligibility packet with every stage of reconstruction, not just the first, is what keeps a legally mandated benefit from being denied as if it were elective cosmetic surgery.

What documentation does a panniculectomy prior authorization need after bariatric-surgery weight loss?

Payers reviewing a post-bariatric panniculectomy (CPT 15830, with 15847 as an add-on when umbilical transposition or fascial plication is performed) generally want proof the pannus is a functional and medical problem, not a cosmetic one: dated photographs showing the extent of the overhanging pannus, a documented history of recurrent intertrigo, cellulitis, or skin breakdown under the fold with the specific treatments tried, confirmation that the patient's weight has been stable for a defined period following bariatric surgery, and a note connecting the physical exam findings to the hygiene or mobility limitation the pannus causes. Panniculectomy is also one of the five procedures CMS places on its hospital outpatient department prior-authorization list specifically because it can be performed as either a cosmetic or a medically necessary procedure, so the medical-necessity case has to be explicit rather than assumed.

What visual field testing does a functional blepharoplasty prior authorization require?

Most payers want a formal automated visual field test performed twice at the same visit: once with the upper eyelids in their natural, unretracted position, and once with the redundant skin taped or manually elevated. The comparison has to show meaningful superior field loss that improves once the skin is lifted, Aetna's published clinical policy bulletin on eyelid surgery, for example, specifies a superior visual field of 30 degrees or less before taping and an improvement of at least 12 degrees or at least 30% after taping. Medicare Administrative Contractors don't all apply the same mechanics: some require both taped and untaped fields, and at least one MAC (Noridian) has accepted detailed clinical exam findings in place of a formal visual field test. Confirm the specific payer's or MAC's published policy before the visit, since the test has to be ordered and performed to match it.

How does the CMS-0057-F prior authorization rule affect plastic surgery requests?

CMS-0057-F, the Interoperability and Prior Authorization Final Rule, requires Medicare Advantage organizations, Medicaid and CHIP managed care plans, and ACA exchange Qualified Health Plan issuers to decide a standard prior authorization request within 7 calendar days and an expedited request within 72 hours, effective for these operational requirements beginning January 1, 2026; state Medicaid and CHIP fee-for-service programs have until January 1, 2027. The rule also requires a specific reason for every denial. Because the timeline exclusion in this rule is written around prior authorization for drugs, not procedures, a plastic surgery request for panniculectomy, blepharoplasty, breast reduction, or reconstruction is a covered service request and the 7-day/72-hour clock applies to it in full, unlike a drug-benefit request, which this rule's timeline requirement does not reach.
