Gastroenterology Medical Billing and Coding: The Complete 2026 Guide.

One fact governs more GI reimbursement than any other: whether a colonoscopy or upper endoscopy is classified as screening, surveillance, or diagnostic decides the code, the modifier, and what the patient owes — and that classification can flip mid-procedure the moment a polyp comes into view. Every competitor guide we reviewed treats bundling edits and modifier errors as vague categories. This guide cites the actual NCCI edit logic, the specific LCDs by MAC, and the CARC codes tied to GI's highest-volume denials, because "check your bundling" isn't an instruction a biller can act on.

Reviewed by Phares A., MD · Physiotherapist, CPC (AAPC) · Updated August 2026

Key takeaways

  • Screening-to-diagnostic conversion is the single biggest source of GI denials and underpayments. Modifier PT (Medicare) or 33 (commercial) has to go on the claim the moment a polyp is removed during what started as a screening — get it wrong and the patient is billed cost-sharing they shouldn't owe, or the practice underbills what it's entitled to.
  • The Medicare coinsurance on polyp removal during a screening colonoscopy is mid-phase-down, not settled law. Patients owe 15% through 2026, dropping to 10% for 2027–2029, and 0% starting 2030 — a detail we found in only the underlying CMS source, not in any competitor guide.
  • Biopsy and polypectomy of the same lesion are genuinely bundled — different lesions are not. The NCCI edit pairing 45385 over 45380 is bypassable with modifier 59/XS only when the operative note names separate lesions; same-lesion billing of both codes doesn't become correct just because a modifier is attached.
  • Capsule endoscopy coverage is written per MAC, not nationally. Novitas, Palmetto GBA, WPS, Noridian, First Coast, and CGS each publish their own local coverage article, and the covered indications differ contractor to contractor even though the CPT codes are identical everywhere.

GI billing at a glance

Gastroenterology's denial volume traces back to one structural fact more than any bundling edit or modifier rule: the same procedure code can represent three different billing scenarios — a covered screening, a covered surveillance exam, or a diagnostic procedure with patient cost-sharing — and the determination happens based on what the endoscopist finds, not what was scheduled. Everything else in this guide sits downstream of that one classification problem.

Four structural problems compound it. First, the classification itself can flip mid-procedure: a patient scheduled for a screening colonoscopy who turns out to have a polyp becomes, on the same claim, a screening-with-therapeutic-conversion encounter, and the modifier has to reflect that even though nothing about the scheduling changed. Second, GI stacks dense bundling logic on top of that, because so many procedures in the same family share a diagnostic core — a biopsy, a polypectomy, and an EMR performed on the same lesion are one billable service, not three. Third, diagnosis specificity gates coverage directly: Medicare LCDs and commercial medical policy both publish the conditions that support a given study, and a symptom-only code frequently fails that test where a specific one would pass. Fourth, several of GI's highest-cost studies — capsule endoscopy chief among them — carry prior-authorization requirements that differ by Medicare Administrative Contractor and by commercial payer, so "the LCD says" is meaningless without naming which one. Here's the shape of it before the detail.

What changed for 2026

2026 is a lighter code-set year for GI than the sweeping interventional-cardiology overhaul happening elsewhere in CPT, but two things changed that affect real claims going out this year.

⚠️ Coding-update sources describe 2026 as otherwise stable for GI's core endoscopy families — no deletions or major revisions to the 45378–45398, 43235–43270, or 43260–43278 ranges were identified in this build's research. If your charge master shows different guidance for a specific code in these families, confirm against your AMA CPT Professional Edition or MAC bulletin directly before relying on this page alone, since CPT's own annual errata process occasionally issues corrections after January 1.

43889's practical impact is narrower than its headline suggests. It gives ESG a real Category I code with a 90-day global for the first time, which matters for scheduling and post-op billing, but it doesn't settle payer coverage — ESG remains a procedure most commercial payers and Medicare Advantage plans still treat as investigational or explicitly excluded for weight-loss indications, separate from its coverage status when performed for a distinct, medically necessary reason. Having a Category I code makes prior authorization and appeal conversations more concrete (a real code with a real global period rather than a temporary HCPCS placeholder), but it does not by itself flip a payer's coverage determination. Confirm the specific payer's bariatric and endoscopic-procedure policy before scheduling, not after the C9784-to-43889 code switch alone.

The other quiet 2026 change worth flagging to billing staff: because the coinsurance phase-down for screening-converted colonoscopies is now three years into its multi-year schedule (2023 through 2026 at 15%), any patient financial-responsibility script or estimate template built in 2023 is accurate again for 2026 claims but needs a note that the number changes again in January 2027. Practices running open-enrollment patient communications or annual wellness-visit scripts that mention colonoscopy cost-sharing should treat that figure as a value to update yearly, not a constant to hard-code once and forget.

E/M for GI: new patient workups, consults, and the MDM trap

GI E/M coding runs on the same 2021 medical-decision-making framework as every other specialty, but two GI-specific presentations get downcoded constantly because the note doesn't capture the complexity that was actually managed.

An undiagnosed GI bleed — melena, hematochezia, or a positive FOBT with no established source — is high-risk MDM almost by definition: it's an undiagnosed problem with an uncertain prognosis, and the management options carry real risk (urgent endoscopy, transfusion, admission). That supports 99215 on time or complexity alone when the note documents the risk assessment, not just the symptom. Chronic IBD flare management sits differently: it's typically moderate-to-high complexity, driven less by the number of diagnoses and more by the data reviewed (prior colonoscopy and imaging reports, labs, prior biologic trough levels) and by the risk of drug toxicity monitoring inherent to managing a patient on a biologic or immunomodulator — infliximab, adalimumab, vedolizumab, ustekinumab, azathioprine. That toxicity-monitoring risk category is exactly the kind of MDM element that's real clinically but routinely absent from the note, because the physician did the mental work without writing it down as a distinct risk consideration.

The failure pattern we see most: a GI new-patient visit involves real chart review — outside endoscopy reports, imaging, pathology — that never gets documented as data reviewed separately from the history and exam, so the visit reads as a straightforward new-patient encounter and prices at 99203 instead of 99204 or 99205. Prolonged services (99417, the add-on to the highest-level time-based E/M code) apply once total time clears the threshold for the base code and keeps going in 15-minute increments — relevant for a genuinely complex new IBD consult where chart review alone can run 20–30 minutes before the patient is even in the room, but only billable when total time is documented, not estimated after the fact.

Total time is the other pathway, and it's the one GI practices underuse relative to how much non-face-to-face work actually happens around a visit. Total time includes same-day chart review before the visit, counseling and care coordination during it, and documentation after — for a new IBD consult, reviewing outside endoscopy reports, prior imaging, and pathology before the patient is even in the room routinely runs 15–20 minutes on its own. 99417, the prolonged-services add-on, applies once total time clears the ceiling for the highest time-based code in the family (99205 for new patients, 99215 for established) and is billed in 15-minute increments beyond that — but only when the total time is actually logged, not reconstructed from memory after the note is signed. A visit that clinically ran long but wasn't timed in real time can't retroactively support 99417 no matter how complex the case was.

New-patient visits and consults get downcoded for a specific, repeatable reason in GI: the physician does real diagnostic reasoning — synthesizing an outside colonoscopy report, a CT enterography read, and a pathology result into a working diagnosis — but the note presents that synthesis as a narrative summary rather than as data reviewed under its own heading. MDM scoring counts data reviewed as a distinct element from history and exam; when it's buried in the HPI instead of itemized, the coder (or the payer's own audit software) can't credit it, and the visit prices at whatever the history and exam alone support. Templating a discrete "data reviewed" section into the GI new-patient note — prior records reviewed, each source named, each finding summarized in one line — fixes this at the documentation level before a coder ever has to make a judgment call.

Advanced practice providers see a large share of GI's follow-up and biologic-monitoring volume, and incident-to billing rules apply the same way they do elsewhere: the physician has to have initiated the plan of care and remain immediately available in the office suite for the visit to bill under the physician's NPI at 100% of the fee schedule rather than the APP's own NPI at 85%. A new problem identified during what was scheduled as a routine APP follow-up — a new symptom outside the established plan of care — generally can't bill incident-to for that portion of the visit, which is a distinction GI practices with high APP volume audit too rarely.

Prior authorization for the biologics and immunomodulators driving that risk-of-toxicity-monitoring MDM element is its own administrative track, layered with step-therapy requirements that vary payer to payer — covered in full, alongside the rest of GI E/M coding, in our E/M coding and IBD biologic prior authorization guide.

Colonoscopy codes 45378–45398: the core code family

Colonoscopy is GI's highest-volume procedure family and the one where a base-versus-add-on mistake costs the most, because every code in this range past 45378 represents what was found and how it was removed — not an additional service stacked on top of the diagnostic exam.

Core colonoscopy codes, paraphrased from standard CPT usage — confirm exact descriptors in your CPT Professional Edition.
CodeServiceBilling logic
45378Diagnostic colonoscopy, flexible, to cecumBase code — never billed in addition to any therapeutic code below when the therapeutic work was performed at the same session; it's replaced by the higher code, not added to it
45380With biopsy, single or multipleBundled into 45385 (snare) when the same lesion is both biopsied and removed — billable as a second line only for a separate lesion
45384With removal of lesion(s) by hot biopsy forceps or bipolar cauteryUse for lesions small enough for forceps/cautery removal, not snare technique
45385With removal of lesion(s) by snare techniqueThe most common therapeutic colonoscopy code; highest-valued code in a mixed-technique session generally governs which additional codes can be billed alongside it
45388With ablation of tumor(s)/polyp(s)/lesion(s) not amenable to removal by forceps, cautery, or snareReserved for lesions that can't be fully removed by the standard techniques — document why ablation was chosen over resection
45390With endoscopic mucosal resection (EMR)For larger or flat lesions requiring submucosal injection and resection; not interchangeable with 45385 — the technique has to match the code
45391 / 45392With endoscopic ultrasound, without / with FNA or biopsyEUS-specific codes; 45392 only when tissue was actually sampled during the ultrasound portion, not just imaged

The rule that trips up the most claims: never bill 45378 alongside any of the therapeutic codes above for the same session. The diagnostic exam is inherent to every therapeutic colonoscopy code — you can't remove a polyp without first advancing to and visualizing it — so 45378 is replaced, not stacked. Where practices lose money legitimately is the reverse case: a genuinely separate lesion, in a separate part of the colon, biopsied with one technique and resected with another, which is billable as two lines when the operative note names each lesion's location and technique distinctly.

Multiple polyps in the same session, removed by the same technique, are a single line at a single code — four polyps all removed by snare is one unit of 45385, not four. The code doesn't multiply by lesion count; it changes only when the technique used to remove a given lesion changes. When a session includes, say, two polyps removed by snare and one removed by hot forceps, the standard approach is to bill the highest-valued technique code for the whole session (45385) and treat the forceps removal as bundled into it, unless payer-specific guidance in your contract says otherwise — this is one of the more frequent sources of overbilling we see in chart audits, where every technique used gets its own line regardless of whether the coding rule actually supports that.

Cold snare versus hot snare (electrocautery) is a technique distinction that affects clinical risk and increasingly affects payer scrutiny of bleeding-complication claims, but it doesn't change which CPT code applies — both fall under 45385. What does matter for the record is documenting polyp size and morphology (sessile, pedunculated) for each lesion, because size and morphology are what a payer's medical-necessity review checks when a claim for a more complex code (EMR, 45390) is billed instead of standard snare polypectomy; a large sessile lesion justifies EMR, a small pedunculated one typically doesn't, and the note has to make that case rather than leaving the coder to infer it from the code chosen.

Documentation of a complete exam matters for a reason beyond the clinical record: cecal intubation (confirmed by landmarks or photo-documentation) and a validated bowel-prep quality score (commonly the Boston Bowel Preparation Scale) both affect what screening interval is appropriate going forward, and an incomplete exam or inadequate prep documented in the note is what supports billing a repeat exam sooner than the standard interval would otherwise allow — without that documentation, a repeat colonoscopy inside the normal interval reads as a frequency violation rather than a clinically justified early repeat.

Two Medicare-specific screening HCPCS codes sit alongside the CPT family rather than replacing it: G0105 (screening colonoscopy, individual at high risk — personal history of adenomatous polyps, colorectal cancer, IBD, or family history of FAP/HNPCC, covered every 24 months) and G0121 (screening colonoscopy, individual not at high risk, covered every 120 months, or 48 months after a covered flexible sigmoidoscopy). These are the codes billed for the screening encounter itself; if a polyp is found and removed, the claim converts to the CPT therapeutic code with PT or 33 appended — covered in full in the next section.

Flexible sigmoidoscopy has its own screening HCPCS code, G0104, distinct from the colonoscopy codes above — it's a limited exam of the rectum and sigmoid colon only, not the full colon, and it sits on its own screening interval rather than being interchangeable with G0105/G0121. A flexible sigmoidoscopy that finds a polyp and proceeds to full colonoscopy in the same session is billed as the colonoscopy, not both codes together; the two aren't stacked when the sigmoidoscopy was extended into a full exam rather than performed as a genuinely separate, limited procedure.

Surveillance colonoscopy is a third category that gets conflated with screening constantly, and the two are billed differently even though the patient may be equally asymptomatic. A patient with a personal history of colon polyps (Z86.010) returning for a scheduled follow-up exam isn't billed as a screening encounter under G0105/G0121 — that history code identifies the visit as surveillance, billed with the standard diagnostic CPT code (45378 or whichever therapeutic code applies) from the start, without PT or 33, and standard deductible/coinsurance applies regardless of whether anything new is found. Coding a surveillance exam as if it were a fresh screening, or vice versa, is a distinct error from the screening-to-diagnostic conversion covered in the next section, and it's worth training staff to recognize the difference at scheduling, not just at claim submission.

Through-stoma colonoscopy has its own separate code range, 44388–44408, mirroring the same diagnostic-through-therapeutic progression as 45378–45392 but performed through an existing ileostomy or colostomy rather than transanally; don't cross-bill a through-stoma code against a transanal one for the same encounter.

What every competitor guide in this space skips is the exact edit logic behind why some of these combinations pay together and some don't — that's covered mechanically, with the specific column1/column2 pairs, later in this guide's NCCI edits and bundling section.

Screening-to-diagnostic conversion: PT, 33, KX, and the coinsurance phase-down

This is the single biggest GI billing issue by claim volume and dollar impact, and it deserves to be treated mechanically rather than as a vague "watch your modifiers" warning.

  • PTMedicare only. Append to the CPT colonoscopy code (45380, 45384, 45385, etc.) when a screening colonoscopy converts to therapeutic because a polyp or lesion was found and removed. PT waives the Part B deductible on the claim while the phased-down coinsurance still applies — it does not make the visit fully no-cost the way a pure screening claim is.
  • 33Commercial, non-Medicare. The ACA preventive-services modifier. Append when a screening colonoscopy converts to therapeutic under a non-grandfathered commercial plan, signaling the payer to waive cost-sharing entirely under the preventive-services mandate. Never append both PT and 33 to the same line — one claim, one payer type, one modifier.
  • KXRequired since 1/1/2023 on the screening HCPCS code (G0105/G0121), not the CPT conversion code, whenever the colonoscopy is a follow-up to a positive Cologuard (mt-sDNA/FIT-DNA) or FIT result. This traces to the 2023 statutory expansion of the CRC screening benefit definition to include the follow-on colonoscopy after a positive covered stool-based test — confirmed directly in CMS's National Coverage Determination 210.3 revision history (Transmittal 11824, CR13017, effective 1/1/2023). Without KX, the follow-up colonoscopy risks processing as diagnostic from the start, with full deductible and coinsurance applied from a claim that should have qualified for screening treatment.

The coinsurance detail nobody else in this space publishes: patient cost-sharing on polyp removal during a screening colonoscopy is on a multi-year phase-down, not a fixed number. Under the Consolidated Appropriations Act, 2021, Section 122, the coinsurance is 15% for 2023 through 2026, drops to 10% for 2027 through 2029, and reaches 0% starting 2030 (CMS MLN Matters MM12656, the coinsurance phase-down guidance). Practices quoting patients a flat "you'll owe 15%" answer for a screening-converted-to-therapeutic colonoscopy are accurate for claims processed in 2026, but that number changes on a schedule — build the year into your patient financial counseling scripts, not just the fact of cost-sharing.

Do
  • Confirm payer type (Medicare vs. commercial) before choosing PT or 33 — it's a hard binary, not a judgment call.
  • Keep Z12.11 in the primary diagnosis position on any screening-converted claim, even when the polyp finding is clinically the more significant piece of the encounter.
  • Append KX on the screening HCPCS code itself when the colonoscopy follows a positive stool-based test, and document the triggering test's result and date in the chart.
Don't
  • Don't append both PT and 33 to the same line — they answer the same question for two different payer categories and are mutually exclusive.
  • Don't quote a flat coinsurance percentage to a Medicare patient without checking the current year against the phase-down schedule — it changes on a set calendar, not gradually.
  • Don't use KX on the CPT conversion code (45380/45385/etc.) — it belongs on the screening HCPCS code, and misplacing it doesn't produce the coverage effect intended.

The commercial-side mechanics run in parallel but aren't identical. A non-grandfathered commercial plan covering a screening colonoscopy that converts to therapeutic gets modifier 33 on the same conversion code, and under the ACA preventive-services mandate the payer is expected to waive the patient's cost-sharing on the entire claim — there is no commercial equivalent to Medicare's phased coinsurance, because the ACA mandate doesn't carry a phase-down schedule the way the Medicare statute does. That's a meaningful patient-communication difference: a Medicare patient converting from screening to therapeutic in 2026 owes 15% coinsurance on the professional/facility fee, while a commercially-insured patient under a non-grandfathered plan typically owes nothing for the same clinical scenario. Confirm the plan is non-grandfathered before promising a patient zero cost-sharing, since grandfathered plans (increasingly rare, but not extinct) aren't bound by the preventive-services mandate.

Screening frequency itself is risk-stratified, and billing the wrong interval fires a frequency denial regardless of how correctly everything else on the claim was coded.

The KX-eligible follow-up colonoscopy is worth walking through as its own scenario, because it's the newest piece of this framework and the one most guides get vague about. Before January 1, 2023, a colonoscopy performed because a stool-based screening test came back positive was billed as diagnostic from the start — the stool test was the screening; the colonoscopy that followed it was, by the letter of the older regulation, a separate diagnostic procedure with full cost-sharing. The 2023 statutory change (confirmed directly in NCD 210.3's own revision history, effective for the follow-on colonoscopy after a covered stool-based test returns positive) reclassified that follow-up colonoscopy as itself a covered screening service. KX is the modifier that tells the payer the claim qualifies under this expanded definition rather than defaulting to diagnostic treatment. Bill it on the screening HCPCS code (G0105 or G0121, whichever risk category applies), document the positive stool test result and date in the chart, and if a polyp is then found and removed during that KX-flagged colonoscopy, PT still applies on top of it for the therapeutic conversion — the two modifiers aren't mutually exclusive, because they answer different questions (KX: does this qualify as screening at all; PT: did it convert to therapeutic).

Coding the polyp finding as the primary diagnosis and dropping Z12.11 is the single most common error in this workflow — it strips the screening origin from the claim and can cause the payer to process the entire encounter as diagnostic from the start, applying full deductible where none should apply. Upper endoscopy (EGD) 43235–43270

EGD billing runs on the same base-versus-therapeutic hierarchy as colonoscopy, priced by RVU value rather than a simple list of add-ons, and the rule that governs it is the same one that governs colonoscopy: only the highest-value code performed gets billed per session.

Core EGD codes, paraphrased from standard CPT usage.
CodeServiceNote
43235Diagnostic EGD, including specimen collection by brushing/washing when performedBase code; replaced (not stacked) by any therapeutic code below performed the same session
43239With biopsy, single or multipleNever billed with 43235 same session — the biopsy code already includes the diagnostic exam
43249With balloon dilation of esophagus (<30mm diameter)Therapeutic; governs over 43235/43239 when performed same session
43251With removal of lesion(s) by snare techniqueHighest-value common EGD code in most sessions where a polyp or lesion is resected

The RVU-driven hierarchy means the coder's job isn't to list everything the physician did — it's to identify the single highest-valued code the documentation supports and bill that one. Billing 43235 and 43239 on the same claim for the same session is a bundling error, not a documentation choice; the exam and the biopsy are one service when performed together, priced under the biopsy code alone.

Unlike colonoscopy, there is no Medicare screening benefit for EGD — every EGD, from the first diagnostic look to a repeat surveillance exam in a patient with Barrett's esophagus, is billed as diagnostic, with standard deductible and coinsurance, regardless of whether the patient has symptoms at the time. That makes diagnosis coding carry the entire medical-necessity burden for EGD claims; there's no screening-modifier safety net the way colonoscopy has PT/33. The most defensible EGD diagnoses tend to fall into a handful of buckets: dysphagia with a specific character and duration, GERD refractory to an adequate trial of medical therapy (not GERD alone), iron-deficiency anemia with a negative or incomplete lower workup, or surveillance in a patient with an established diagnosis like Barrett's esophagus or a prior peptic ulcer with a defined follow-up interval. A note that documents which of these applies, with the relevant history, supports the claim considerably better than a chief complaint of "abdominal pain" coded to R10.9 alone.

Balloon dilation (43249) and hemostasis (43255) both carry documentation expectations beyond "dilation performed" or "bleeding controlled." For dilation, payers generally want the pre- and post-dilation luminal diameter or the balloon size used, and the underlying diagnosis driving the stricture (peptic, radiation, post-surgical anastomotic, eosinophilic esophagitis) documented distinctly, because the medical necessity case differs by etiology even though the CPT code doesn't. For hemostasis, the method used (clip, injection, thermal, or a combination) and the visualized bleeding source both belong in the note; a claim for 43255 without a clearly identified bleeding source reads, on audit, like an EGD that happened to include hemostasis language rather than a procedure genuinely performed for bleeding control.

Same-day EGD and colonoscopy billing is where GI's largest volume of X-modifier questions comes from. Because the two procedures sit in different endoscopy families, there typically isn't a direct NCCI procedure-to-procedure edit blocking both codes from paying on the same claim — each is billed under its own family, subject to the Medicare Physician Fee Schedule's multiple-endoscopic-procedure reduction rather than an outright bundling denial. Where modifier XS (or generic 59, for payers that haven't adopted the X-modifiers) still earns its place is making the claim self-explanatory: appending it to the second procedure states plainly that the two services were performed on separate, unrelated anatomic sites (esophagus/stomach/duodenum versus colon), rather than leaving a payer's automated same-day-duplicate-endoscopy logic to guess. CMS's own local coverage guidance on this point, published as Article A53399, "CPT Modifier 59: Gastroenterology", states plainly that modifier 59 exists to flag procedures performed at different anatomic sites or different encounters — not as a routine override for every same-day combination. ⚠️ This build could not open the full article text directly (CMS's coverage-database pages returned an access error to automated fetch attempts), so the summary above reflects the article's stated purpose as reported by secondary coding sources; pull the article's full indication list yourself before building a scrubber rule against it.

ERCP 43260–43278 and advanced procedures

ERCP follows the same "base diagnostic code is replaced, not stacked" logic as colonoscopy and EGD, layered with the added complexity of sphincterotomy and stent codes that bundle into each other depending on what was actually accomplished.

The base diagnostic code, 43260, is rarely billed on its own in practice — most ERCPs are therapeutic from the outset, and the diagnostic code is bundled into whichever therapeutic code the session supports. Sphincterotomy, when performed as an access step to reach the bile or pancreatic duct for a subsequent therapeutic maneuver, is generally not separately billable on top of the maneuver it enabled; it's billed on its own only when it is itself the definitive therapeutic act of the session. Therapeutic add-ons follow a "highest-valued code 100%, each additional code at a reduced percentage" multiple-procedure logic within the same operative session — 43274 (stent placement) and 43275 (removal of foreign body/stent) are common examples, and both require documentation specifying which duct, which stent, and why the maneuver was necessary, since payers scrutinize multi-code ERCP claims heavily for medical necessity of each individually billed step.

Fluoroscopic guidance is inherent to ERCP and generally isn't separately billable as a standalone radiology supervision-and-interpretation code the way it might be in a different procedural context — the fluoroscopy is part of what makes the ERCP code valued the way it is, not an add-on service. Site of service matters more for ERCP than for standard upper or lower endoscopy, because the fluoroscopy equipment and the sedation/anesthesia support ERCP typically requires push most practices toward a hospital outpatient or ASC setting rather than an office-based endoscopy suite; that setting choice affects facility billing and often triggers its own prior-authorization requirement distinct from the physician's professional-component authorization.

Capsule endoscopy sits in this section because it shares ERCP's prior-authorization-heavy profile, even though it's a fundamentally different technology. 91110 covers capsule endoscopy of the esophagus through the ileum, including physician interpretation and report; 91111 is limited to esophageal capsule endoscopy alone. Both split into professional and technical components exactly like an imaging study — modifier 26 for interpretation-only when the practice doesn't own the capsule system or supply the device, TC for the technical/device component, global when the practice owns the equipment and performs the read. In practice, the device itself is frequently supplied and billed by a hospital, ASC, or the capsule manufacturer's own billing arrangement even when the interpreting physician is independent, which is exactly the scenario that calls for modifier 26 rather than a global claim — billing globally when the device and its associated technical fee were already billed elsewhere creates the same duplicate-claim conflict that a mismatched 26/TC split causes in imaging. This is one of the most prior-auth-gated studies in GI: payers commonly require documented failure or nondiagnostic results from a standard EGD and colonoscopy first, plus a specific indication rather than a symptom-only code, before authorizing it. Full code detail, the professional/technical split, and the prior authorization documentation checklist are in our capsule endoscopy billing and prior authorization guide.

Moderate sedation and anesthesia billing for GI procedures

Sedation billing for GI endoscopy has its own dedicated code that most other specialties don't need to think about, and getting the substitution wrong is a guaranteed denial rather than an underpayment.

For Medicare, when the physician performing the GI endoscopy also personally provides the moderate sedation, the correct code is G0500 — a GI-specific moderate sedation code CMS created specifically to replace 99152 for this scenario. Billing 99152 in this situation for a Medicare claim denies outright; the code substitution isn't optional or payer-preference, it's a hard requirement built into how Medicare recognizes GI endoscopy sedation. Add-on code 99153 still applies on top of G0500 for additional 15-minute increments of sedation time beyond the first, the same way it would with 99152 in a non-GI context.

  • 1Medicare, physician performing both the endoscopy and the sedation. Bill G0500 for the first 15 minutes, +99153 for each additional 15-minute increment. Never 99152 in this scenario — it denies.
  • 2Commercial payers frequently haven't adopted G0500. Many still expect 99152/99153 for the identical clinical scenario. This is one of the few GI coding decisions that genuinely has to be set per payer contract rather than standardized practice-wide — check the specific payer's policy before defaulting to either code set.
  • 3Facility-setting caveat. ⚠️ Industry billing guidance describes a facility-setting restriction where an endoscopist billing for sedation cannot also have a facility separately bill a technical component for the same sedation service — consistent with the general Medicare principle that a single sedation service isn't split-billed by two different parties. This build was not able to independently confirm the exact mechanics of that restriction against a primary CMS transmittal, so verify the specific rule with your MAC or facility contract before relying on it to structure a claim.

Documentation for any moderate sedation claim — G0500 or 99152/99153 alike — needs the same core elements regardless of which code set applies: start and stop time of the sedation service (not the procedure itself), the ASA physical status classification, and confirmation that an independent trained observer, separate from the person performing the endoscopy, monitored the patient's level of consciousness and vital signs throughout. Missing the independent-observer documentation is a common, entirely preventable reason a sedation claim that was clinically appropriate still doesn't hold up on audit, because the code descriptor itself requires that role to be filled and recorded.

Anesthesia provided by a separate anesthesiologist or CRNA (rather than moderate sedation by the endoscopist) is billed entirely differently, under the anesthesia code set with time-based units and its own medical necessity documentation — that's a distinct billing track from G0500/99152/99153 and isn't interchangeable with either. This route has become more common for average-risk patients over the past several years, not just for the higher-ASA-class patients it was traditionally reserved for, and payers have correspondingly tightened medical necessity review for monitored anesthesia care (MAC) or general anesthesia during routine endoscopy. Documentation supporting the anesthesia route — a specific clinical reason moderate sedation wasn't appropriate (severe sleep apnea, chronic high-dose opioid use, prior sedation failure, anticipated prolonged or complex procedure) — increasingly determines whether the anesthesia claim itself, not just the endoscopy, gets paid without a medical-necessity denial.

NCCI edits and bundling in GI

This is where GI billing actually gets decided, and it's the section every competitor guide we reviewed — Neolytix, AnnexMed, Muni Health among them — treats as a conceptual warning ("watch for bundling errors") rather than citing the mechanics that determine whether a bundled code is billable at all.

The NCCI procedure-to-procedure modifier indicator attached to a column1/column2 code pair is what actually governs the outcome, not general caution:

  • 0Never bypassable. No modifier, however well-documented, changes the outcome. The column 2 code simply isn't separately payable when this indicator applies to the pair.
  • 1Bypassable with documentation. An NCCI-associated modifier (59 or the specific X-modifier) can override the edit, but only where the record genuinely supports a distinct site, session, or lesion — not as a routine way to get a second line paid.
  • 9Edit deleted. The pair no longer applies going forward.

GI's highest-volume real-world example: 45385 (snare polypectomy) as the column 1 code over 45380 (biopsy) as column 2. Industry coding references consistently describe this pair as carrying an indicator that permits override — billable as two lines, with modifier 59 or XS on 45380, only when the biopsy and the polypectomy were performed on genuinely separate lesions, using separate technique, with each lesion's anatomic location documented distinctly in the operative note. Same lesion, both techniques, same session is one procedure clinically and one line on the claim — attaching a modifier doesn't change that, and a payer or auditor comparing the modifier against the note will catch it. ⚠️ This build could not open CMS's primary NCCI PTP edit file directly to confirm the exact numeric modifier indicator value for the 45385/45380 pair (CMS's coverage-database and NCCI file pages returned access errors to every automated fetch attempt made during this build) — the override-permitted behavior above is reported consistently by professional coding references (AAPC) but should be confirmed against the current quarter's file in the CMS NCCI PTP Edits Lookup Tool before it's built into a scrubber rule, since these values update quarterly.

Do
  • Name each lesion's specific anatomic location (e.g. "sigmoid colon, 25cm" versus "ascending colon, 60cm") when billing biopsy and polypectomy as separate lines.
  • Confirm the modifier indicator for a specific pair before assuming a modifier will make it billable.
  • Document the technique used on each lesion distinctly when more than one technique was used in a session.
Don't
  • Don't bill a biopsy and a polypectomy of the same lesion as two lines, modifier or not — it's one procedure.
  • Don't use 59/XS as a routine way to get a second line paid without documentation showing a genuinely separate site.
  • Don't assume EGD-plus-colonoscopy needs an override modifier the way same-family colonoscopy bundling does — confirm whether a true PTP pair exists before defaulting to XS out of habit.

EGD-plus-colonoscopy in the same session is a different mechanism entirely, and conflating it with the biopsy/polypectomy bundling above is a common mistake. Because the two procedures sit in separate endoscopy families, there typically isn't a direct NCCI PTP pair between, say, 43235 and 45378 — both are billable, subject to the multiple-endoscopic-procedure fee reduction rather than a bundling denial, with an X-modifier commonly used to state plainly that the sites were separate (upper GI versus lower GI) rather than to override a true edit.

ERCP's therapeutic add-on codes carry their own internal bundling logic, distinct from the biopsy/polypectomy pattern above: a sphincterotomy performed purely as access to place a stent is bundled into the stent-placement code, not billed as a separate line, while a sphincterotomy performed as the definitive therapeutic act of the session (no subsequent stent or stone removal) is billed on its own. The distinguishing question for the coder is what the sphincterotomy accomplished clinically, not whether it happened — and that has to be traceable in the operative note, not inferred from the code list.

Routine, high-frequency use of 59 or an X-modifier to clear an edit — rather than to reflect a genuinely distinct service — is one of the most reliably audited patterns in gastroenterology specifically, because the specialty's procedure mix generates so many legitimate opportunities to use these modifiers that overuse is easy to hide inside normal volume. Payers and CMS's own program integrity contractors track append-rate outliers by provider and by practice; a GI practice whose 59/XS rate on colonoscopy claims sits well above its regional peers draws review attention even when every individual claim, examined in isolation, might be defensible. The operative-note documentation habits described throughout this section — naming each lesion's location, naming the technique used on each — are what make that review, when it happens, a non-event rather than a recoupment exposure.

Modifier 22 (unusual procedural service) belongs in this conversation even though it isn't an NCCI-override modifier, because it's frequently confused with the distinct-service modifiers covered above. 22 signals that a single procedure required substantially greater physician work than usual for that code — extensive adhesions, an unusually difficult polypectomy requiring significantly more time and skill — not that two procedures were performed. Appending 22 requires a comparison to the typical case (not just a description of what happened) and generally increases payment for the single code billed, rather than unbundling anything. Confusing 22 with 59/XS — using 22 to try to get a second procedure paid, or using 59 to claim a routine case was unusually difficult — is a pattern that draws scrutiny precisely because the two modifiers answer different questions.

MUE awareness matters most in this specialty for capsule endoscopy (a study that's clinically almost never repeated same-day, making a second unit on the same date an audit flag rather than a coding decision) and for biopsy code units, where billing more units than distinct specimens actually taken is a common data-entry error rather than a clinical one. ⚠️ Specific MUE unit-cap values for 91110/91111 and the biopsy codes were not independently confirmed against CMS's primary MUE files during this build for the same access-error reason noted above; treat any specific unit cap as unconfirmed until checked against the current MUE table. Commercial payers layer their own review on top of, and sometimes in conflict with, Medicare's framework. UnitedHealthcare, Cigna, and other major commercial payers frequently route capsule endoscopy and IBD biologic infusion authorizations through delegated review vendors (EviCore- and Carelon-style utilization management programs), with their own indication lists that don't automatically mirror any MAC's LCD. Treat a Medicare LCD as context for a commercial claim, never as the answer to what a specific commercial plan requires.

Medicare Advantage plans add a third layer that GI practices frequently underestimate: an MA plan is required to cover what Original Medicare covers, but the specific prior-authorization process, the delegated review vendor, and the documentation format it wants can all differ from both the underlying MAC's LCD and from other MA plans in the same market. A capsule endoscopy authorization approach that works for one MA plan in a given state doesn't necessarily transfer to a different MA plan covering the same MAC jurisdiction — the coverage floor is the same, the administrative path to it isn't. Practices running high MA volume are better served maintaining a payer-specific authorization checklist than assuming "Medicare coverage" is a single, uniform standard across every plan that carries the Medicare name.

ABNs matter in GI wherever a service might not meet a payer's medical necessity bar — most often a repeat advanced procedure ordered without a new qualifying diagnosis, or a screening interval billed ahead of schedule at the patient's request. An Advance Beneficiary Notice signed and on file before the service, naming the specific service and the specific reason coverage might be denied, is what allows the practice to bill the patient if Medicare declines the claim; requesting one after a CO-50 denial has already arrived doesn't protect the practice retroactively. GI practices that build ABN screening into scheduling — flagging any procedure ordered outside its standard interval or against a nonspecific diagnosis — catch this before the visit rather than after the remittance advice.

Prior authorization: capsule endoscopy, biologics, and advanced imaging

Prior auth denials in GI are almost entirely preventable, because the requirements are documented in advance — the failure is procedural, not clinical.

For capsule endoscopy, payers consistently want to see documented failure or a nondiagnostic result from a standard EGD and colonoscopy first, plus a specific clinical indication — obscure GI bleeding, suspected small-bowel Crohn's, or iron-deficiency anemia with a negative standard workup — rather than a symptom-only code like R10.9 standing alone. Scheduling the study before the authorization is confirmed, or authorizing one CPT code (91110) while the study performed maps to a different one (91111, esophagus-only), are the two most common preventable failure points.

IBD biologic and immunomodulator infusion prior authorization typically follows a step-therapy pattern: payers frequently require documented failure or intolerance of a conventional therapy (mesalamine, corticosteroids, an immunomodulator alone) before authorizing a biologic, and re-authorization at defined intervals tied to documented clinical response (often via a validated disease activity index or a repeat inflammatory marker). Missing a re-authorization renewal window is a common, entirely preventable cause of an infusion visit denying outright after a patient has already received the drug.

Advanced imaging for IBD — MR enterography and CT enterography specifically — carries its own prior-authorization track, frequently managed through the same radiology benefit management vendors that gate advanced imaging across other specialties rather than through a GI-specific process. The documentation these vendors want mirrors what a payer wants for capsule endoscopy: a specific indication (assessing disease extent or complication in an established IBD diagnosis, evaluating a symptom pattern suggesting small-bowel involvement not visible on colonoscopy) rather than a general "IBD" diagnosis alone, and, for a repeat study, a documented clinical reason the interval since the last imaging study warrants another one. Ordering advanced imaging and capsule endoscopy for the same clinical question in the same workup without documenting why both were necessary is a pattern that draws denials on whichever study is billed second.

  • 1Before scheduling capsule endoscopy: confirm the standard EGD/colonoscopy findings are documented as failed or nondiagnostic, the specific indication is coded (not just a symptom), and the authorized CPT code matches what will actually be performed.
  • 2Before an IBD biologic infusion: confirm step-therapy documentation is on file, the authorization is still within its valid window, and the units/dose billed match what the authorization actually covers.
  • 3For both: verify the specific MAC's LCD or the specific commercial payer's medical policy by name before the study is scheduled — not a generic search result.

Conclusion and next steps

GI billing is decided by classification, not complexity — whether a procedure is screening, surveillance, or diagnostic drives the code, the modifier, and the patient's cost-sharing all at once, and it can flip mid-procedure the moment a polyp comes into view. The practices that get this right build the classification logic into their workflow before the claim is coded: scheduling systems that flag risk category and screening interval up front, note templates that separate data reviewed from history and exam, and operative note templates that force lesion-by-lesion documentation rather than a summary paragraph. Every one of those is a workflow fix, not a coding-team retraining exercise, and every one of them prevents denials that would otherwise consume appeal staff time downstream.

The pattern worth carrying out of this guide: most of GI's highest-volume denials trace back to a handful of mechanical facts — which modifier belongs to which payer type, which NCCI pairs are genuinely never-bypassable versus conditionally overridable, which diagnosis code actually supports medical necessity for a given study, and which prior authorizations gate the highest-cost procedures. None of these require judgment calls once the rule is known; they require the rule being built into the workflow before the claim goes out, not discovered after the denial comes back. That's the difference between a GI billing operation that spends its time on genuinely disputed claims and one that spends its time re-litigating claims that should never have gone out wrong in the first place.

Frequently asked questions

When do we use modifier PT versus 33 versus KX on a colonoscopy claim?

PT is Medicare-only and converts a screening colonoscopy to diagnostic when a polyp or lesion is found and removed, waiving the deductible while the phased-down coinsurance still applies. Modifier 33 does the equivalent job for commercial ACA-regulated plans, signaling a preventive service so the payer waives cost-sharing entirely. KX is different: it's required on the screening HCPCS code itself (G0105/G0121) when the colonoscopy is a follow-up to a positive stool-based test, to show the visit still qualifies as a covered screening under the 2023 rule. Never stack PT and 33 on the same claim line, and never use KX on the CPT conversion code (45380/45385/etc.) — it belongs on the screening code.

Why does our EGD deny for lack of medical necessity?

Almost always because the diagnosis code on the claim is a symptom code, not the specific finding or established diagnosis the payer's coverage policy requires. R10.9 (unspecified abdominal pain) or a vague reflux code rarely clears a medical necessity edit on its own, especially for a repeat EGD. The fix is documenting the presenting symptom with duration and severity, prior treatment failures, and any relevant history (Barrett's, prior ulcer, alarm features) so the diagnosis coded reflects what actually justified the exam, not a shorthand summary.

What actually changed in GI CPT coding for 2026?

The headline addition is 43889, a new Category I code for transoral endoscopic sleeve gastroplasty, effective January 1, 2026, replacing the temporary HCPCS code C9784 and carrying a 90-day global period. It's now separately reportable by hospitals and ambulatory surgical centers as well as physicians. Beyond that, 2026 also continues the multi-year Medicare coinsurance phase-down for polyp removal during a screening colonoscopy, which is a payment-policy change rather than a code change, but it affects what the patient owes on claims processed this year.

Can we bill an EGD and a colonoscopy performed in the same session?

Generally yes — EGD and colonoscopy are different endoscopy families, so there typically isn't an NCCI procedure-to-procedure edit blocking both from paying, and each is billed with its own code. What applies instead is the multiple-endoscopic-procedure payment reduction: the highest-valued code in each family pays at 100% and additional codes in that session are reduced. Some payers still want an X-modifier (commonly XS, separate anatomic structure) appended to the second procedure to make the claim self-explanatory rather than relying on the adjudicator to infer it, so check your specific payer's edit before assuming silence means it isn't needed.

Do we need prior authorization before scheduling a capsule endoscopy?

For most Medicare Advantage and commercial plans, yes, and it's one of the most denied studies in GI when skipped. Payers typically want documentation that a standard EGD and colonoscopy already occurred and were nondiagnostic or incomplete, plus a specific indication — obscure GI bleeding, suspected Crohn's, or iron-deficiency anemia with a negative standard workup — rather than a symptom-only code like R10.9. Confirm your Medicare Administrative Contractor's specific local coverage article before scheduling, because the covered indications differ by MAC even though the CPT codes don't.

What's the difference between G0500 and 99152/99153 for endoscopy sedation?

For Medicare, G0500 is the required code when the physician performing the GI endoscopy also personally provides the moderate sedation — 99152 is denied outright in that scenario because CMS created G0500 specifically to replace it for GI endoscopic services. Add-on code 99153 still applies for additional 15-minute increments beyond the first. Many commercial payers haven't adopted G0500 and still expect 99152/99153, so this is one of the few coding decisions in GI billing that has to be set per payer, not standardized across the board.

Why does our biopsy-plus-polypectomy claim deny as bundled?

Removing tissue by biopsy forceps and then by snare on the same lesion, in the same session, is one procedure clinically, and CMS's National Correct Coding Initiative treats it that way — the biopsy code is a component of the polypectomy code when they're the same lesion, and appending a modifier to force separate payment isn't appropriate no matter how the claim is coded. It's only billable as two lines, with modifier 59 or XS on the lesser code, when the biopsy and the polypectomy were performed on genuinely different lesions or sites, and the operative note has to name each lesion's location separately to support that.

Which diagnosis goes first when a screening colonoscopy finds and removes a polyp?

Z12.11 (encounter for screening for malignant neoplasm of colon) stays in the primary position — the encounter started as a screening, and that's what determines the PT/33 modifier and cost-sharing treatment even though the procedure became therapeutic. The polyp finding (a D12.x benign neoplasm code, by specific colon segment) is coded secondary. Coding the polyp finding as primary and dropping Z12.11 is a common error that strips the screening status from the claim and can shift the patient's cost-sharing incorrectly.
