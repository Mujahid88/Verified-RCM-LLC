Physical Therapy Billing and Coding: The Complete Guide for 2026

PT billing runs on two things most competing guides treat as afterthoughts: exact unit math and diagnosis specificity. Get the 8-minute rule wrong and every claim in a batch is off by a unit; keep a retired diagnosis code on a superbill and every claim carrying it denies outright. This guide covers the full 2026 rule set — unit calculation, the high-volume code families, NCCI bundling with the actual modifier indicator (not just "be careful"), the KX threshold, coverage by LCD document ID, and the payer-by-payer prior authorization landscape — plus the specific gaps we found nobody else in this niche publishes correctly.

Reviewed by Phares A., MD · Physiotherapist, CPC (AAPC) · Updated August 2026

Key takeaways

  • M54.5 is a category header, not a billable code. Every competitor guide we reviewed still lists it as usable low back pain diagnosis. It hasn't been valid for HIPAA transactions since the FY2022 code set restructured the category — you need M54.50, M54.51 (vertebrogenic), or M54.59.
  • 97140 and 97530 bundle under a modifier indicator of 1, not a flat "can't bill together." The override is real but conditional: it requires documentation of separate 15-minute time blocks, not just a modifier appended out of habit.
  • Outpatient PT coverage and home health PT coverage are governed by different LCDs from different contractor types — Palmetto GBA's L34428 versus CGS's L33942 or Palmetto's L34564. Conflating them is a documented denial pattern no reviewed guide names specifically.
  • Dry needling is not a Medicare-covered service outside chronic low back pain — and even there, it's the acupuncture code set that's covered, not a musculoskeletal CPT code relabeled as needling. Most PT guides either skip Medicare coverage of these codes or flatten the exception away.

Executive summary: what changed for PT billing in 2026

Five things moved this year that touch nearly every PT practice's revenue cycle: the Medicare conversion factor reset, the KX threshold climbed again, a new remote therapeutic monitoring code sat alongside the established family, and prior authorization moved in two directions at once depending on which UnitedHealthcare announcement you're reading. None of these are trivia — each one changes a number on a claim or a workflow step before a claim goes out.

The conversion factor sets the dollar value of every relative value unit on every claim, but it's locality-adjusted and code-specific — there's no single per-visit dollar figure that applies across the board, so check the CMS Physician Fee Schedule Look-Up Tool for your own locality and code mix rather than relying on a flat number from any guide, this one included. The KX threshold and medical review threshold matter more operationally: they're calendar-year running totals per beneficiary, tracked across every provider that bills that patient's Part B therapy benefit, not just your practice.

The prior authorization picture is genuinely two-directional this year, and treating it as one trend in either direction is a mistake. UnitedHealthcare simultaneously expanded a Medicare Advantage prior-auth requirement to two new states in February and announced a broad rollback of prior-auth categories in May — both are real, they don't cancel each other out, and which one applies to a given claim depends on the specific plan and service. We cover the practical verification workflow this creates later in this guide.

On diagnosis coding, nothing changed structurally in 2026, but the FY2022 restructuring of the M54.5 low back pain category is still tripping up claims four years later because so much documentation and EHR tooling was built before the change and never updated. We verified the current status directly against the ICD-10-CM code set for this guide, and it's unambiguous: M54.5 is a category header, not a billable code, covered in full later in this guide.

The core coding framework: evaluation, timed, and untimed codes

PT billing sits on three code types, and mixing up their rules is a common source of both under- and over-billing. Evaluation and re-evaluation codes are untimed and selected by complexity tier, not clock minutes. Timed, constant-attendance procedure codes (the 97110/97140/97530 family and most modalities) are billed in 15-minute units under the 8-minute rule. Untimed, supervised-modality codes (hot/cold packs, mechanical traction) are billed once per date of service regardless of how long they took, because the code descriptor doesn't include a time component at all.

Initial evaluation coding (97161–97163) is tiered by complexity across three pillars — patient history, examination findings, and clinical presentation/decision-making complexity — and the rule that surprises new coders every time: the lowest-scoring pillar sets the level, not the highest. A patient with a complex history but a straightforward, single-system exam and low-complexity clinical presentation still bills as low complexity, because two of the three pillars didn't support anything higher.

The failure pattern here is almost always the opposite of upcoding: a therapist documents a genuinely complex initial visit — multiple comorbidities, an unstable presentation — but the note doesn't explicitly capture the exam breadth or clinical instability, so the chart only supports 97161 despite the visit doing 97163-level work. Because eval codes aren't time-based, defending the level on audit comes down entirely to whether the documentation independently establishes all three pillars at the level billed, not whether the visit felt complex.

Re-evaluation (97164) is billed only when the plan of care itself changes based on a documented shift in the patient's status — a new impairment, a plateau requiring a different approach, a significant functional change. Billing 97164 on a routine periodic progress note without a genuine POC revision is a common cause of denial, because the code descriptor itself requires the revised goals and changed presentation, not just a scheduled check-in.

The 8-minute rule: exact unit math and where practices get it wrong

The 8-minute rule (CMS Medicare Claims Processing Manual, Chapter 5) converts total timed-code minutes into billable units. It applies to every constant-attendance, one-on-one timed code — 97110, 97112, 97140, 97530, 97032, 97035, and the rest of that family — and it is the single most common source of small, systematic underbilling or denial-triggering overbilling in the specialty, because it's arithmetic, and arithmetic errors compound across a full caseload.

Each additional unit adds roughly 15 minutes to the range, continuing the same pattern. The rule is stated in whole minutes and the threshold to cross into the next unit is always at the "≥8 minutes into the next 15-minute block" mark — which is exactly why the ranges above open 7 minutes past each 15-minute multiple, not at the multiple itself.

The mixed-remainder rule, worked through: when a visit includes more than one timed code, do not calculate units per code independently and add them — that method routinely overcounts. Instead, total every timed minute across every timed code billed that date, convert the sum to a unit count using the table above, and then distribute those units across the codes actually performed, generally starting with whichever code has the most minutes.

Worked example: a visit includes 20 minutes of 97110, 15 minutes of 97140, and 10 minutes of 97530 — 45 total timed minutes. Per the table, 45 minutes converts to 3 units total (38–52 range), not 1+1+1=3 units calculated separately by coincidence in this case, but that coincidence is exactly the trap: change the split to 20/13/10 (43 minutes) and calculating per-code (1 unit each, since 13 minutes rounds down under some methods) risks either dropping a legitimately billable unit or, more commonly, billing 3 units per-code that the combined-minutes method wouldn't support once true remainders are accounted for. Total the minutes first, always.

CMS 8-minute rule versus the AMA/CPT "substantial portion" method: these are two different systems, and several commercial payers use the AMA method instead of CMS's, which changes the unit count at the margins. The AMA method requires each unit to represent the midpoint or more of a 15-minute increment calculated per code, not combined across codes the way CMS's mixed-remainder rule works. A practice that bills Medicare correctly under the CMS method and then applies the identical unit count to a commercial claim without checking which method that payer uses is a documented source of commercial-payer unit discrepancies. Confirm the payer's methodology in its therapy billing policy before assuming CMS math travels unchanged.

The most common documentation trap in this section isn't the math itself — it's that the total treatment time on the note doesn't match the sum of the individual timed-code minutes actually documented. If the flowsheet shows 15 minutes of 97110 and 15 minutes of 97140 but the note's total-time field says 25 minutes, that 5-minute gap is exactly what a payer's post-payment reviewer flags first, because it means the units billed can't be reconstructed from the chart as written. Every timed minute billed has to appear, itemized, somewhere in the documentation — a global total-time statement without itemization doesn't support unit-level billing on audit. The full unit-calculation walkthrough, including split-visit scenarios and co-treatment math, is in our 8-minute rule guide for physical therapy billing.

High-volume procedure family 1: therapeutic exercise and neuromuscular re-education

97110, 97112, and 97116 together account for a large share of PT line-item volume, and payers scrutinize the distinction between them because it's easy to document loosely and hard to defend loosely on audit.

Core therapeutic exercise family, paraphrased descriptions.
CodeWhat it coversClinical focus
97110Therapeutic exercise, one or more areas, each 15 minutesIsolated strength, endurance, range of motion, or flexibility work — a single system, repetitive and measurable (reps, sets, resistance, ROM in degrees)
97112Neuromuscular re-education, each 15 minutesMovement, balance, coordination, kinesthetic sense, posture, or proprioception retraining — multi-system, addressing motor control rather than isolated strength
97116Gait training, each 15 minutesIncludes stair climbing — ambulation-specific retraining, distinct from general balance work billed under 97112

The clinical line between 97110 and 97112 is the one that gets blurred most: 97110 is isolated, measurable strength or motion work — "3 sets of 10 quad sets, 2 lb resistance." 97112 is neuromuscular control work — balance on an unstable surface, proprioceptive drills, motor-pattern retraining after a neurologic event. A note that says only "exercises for LE weakness, tolerated well" doesn't establish which code applies, because it describes neither the isolated-strength framing of 97110 nor the coordination/balance framing of 97112 specifically enough to survive a payer request for records.

Documentation that survives audit
  • "97110: standing hip abduction, 3x10, yellow band, L > R weakness targeted per eval finding of 3/5 MMT."
  • "97112: single-leg stance on foam pad, 30 sec x 3, cueing for proprioceptive feedback post-CVA, progressing balance confidence scale."
  • Objective measures (reps, sets, resistance, seconds held, distance) tied to a specific impairment from the eval or the plan of care goals.
Documentation that doesn't
  • "Exercises as tolerated." No specificity on which code applies or what impairment was addressed.
  • Identical templated language copied visit to visit with no progression of resistance, difficulty, or measured outcome.
  • 97112 billed for what the objective findings describe as pure strengthening, with no balance, coordination, or proprioceptive component named anywhere in the note.

Same-day pairing with the evaluation code is routine — a new patient frequently gets 97161–97163 plus 97110 or 97112 in the same visit — but the eval and the treatment codes aren't bundled by NCCI in the way procedure-to-procedure pairs are; the relevant modifier question is whether an E/M-style significant-and-separate distinction applies at all, which for a PT evaluation billed alongside same-day treatment generally isn't the modifier 25/59 issue it would be in a physician office, since eval codes aren't E/M codes and most payers expect same-day eval-plus-treatment as standard practice. Where modifier 59 genuinely comes into play in this family is 97112 paired with 97110 on the same date at the same anatomic region without time-block separation — treat that pairing with the same NCCI logic covered in the next section, not as a given.

High-volume procedure family 2: manual therapy and therapeutic activities — the 97140/97530 decision

This is the pairing that generates more denials and more incorrectly-applied modifiers than any other in outpatient PT coding, and it's the section where vague "watch your bundling" advice costs practices real money. Here's the precise mechanism.

97140 (manual therapy techniques, one or more regions, each 15 minutes) covers hands-on techniques — joint mobilization, manual traction, myofascial release, soft tissue mobilization. 97530 (therapeutic activities, direct one-on-one, each 15 minutes) covers dynamic, functional activities that use exercise to improve functional performance — lifting, carrying, transfers, functional reaching patterns tied to a real-world task.

The NCCI relationship: 97530 is a Column 2 code bundled into 97140 as the Column 1 code, under a modifier indicator of 1. That means the edit is not absolute — unlike an indicator-0 pair, an NCCI-associated modifier can override it — but the override is conditional, not automatic. It is valid only when the documentation shows the two services were performed in genuinely separate, non-overlapping 15-minute time blocks. Bill both codes on the same date, in the same session, describing overlapping time, and the edit is doing exactly what it's designed to do: preventing a single block of hands-on treatment time from being coded and paid twice under two different code numbers.

Defensible modifier use
  • "9:00–9:15am: manual therapy, PA glides to L4-L5, grade III mobilization for hypomobility (97140). 9:15–9:30am: therapeutic activities, functional lifting task simulating job requirement, 10–20 lb, floor to waist (97530, modifier 59/XS)." Two named time blocks, two distinct techniques, no overlap.
Automatic audit flag
  • "30 min manual therapy and therapeutic activities to improve function." One time block, both codes billed with modifier 59 appended by default, no separation documented anywhere. This is the single most common post-payment review trigger in the 97140/97530 pairing.

Prefer the specific X{EPSU} modifier over generic 59 wherever it fits: XS (separate structure) is usually the right choice when manual therapy addressed one anatomic region and therapeutic activities addressed functional movement more broadly; use plain 59 only when none of XE, XS, XP, or XU describes the distinction more precisely. CMS and most MAC guidance state the X-modifiers are preferred whenever they apply, even though 59 remains valid.

MUE unit caps: both codes carry per-day Medically Unlikely Edit unit caps designed to flag clinically implausible volumes of hands-on, one-on-one time in a single visit. ⚠️ This build could not independently confirm the current numeric MUE value for either code against CMS's primary MUE file — the CMS coding and billing pages returned access errors to automated retrieval during this research pass, consistent with the access issue CMS's site presents to automated tools generally. Billing-industry secondary sources consistently describe a low single-digit unit cap for both codes; verify the current value in the CMS NCCI MUE Lookup Tool before building a scrubber rule against it, because MUE values are revised quarterly.

Codes exempted from needing a modifier: not every code pair in the 97000 series carries this same conditional bundling. Some pairings that intuitively seem related (for example, most modality-to-modality combinations at different anatomic sites) are not edited against each other at all, and appending an unnecessary modifier to a pair that was never bundled just adds noise to the claim without changing payment. Check the specific pair in the lookup tool rather than assuming every 97000-series combination needs an override modifier by default. The full bundling reference across the whole 97000 series, structured by code family, is in our NCCI edits and bundling guide for physical therapy billing.

High-volume procedure family 3: modalities — supervised vs. constant attendance

Modalities split cleanly into two billing categories, and mixing them up is a quieter but still costly error — usually a lost unit rather than a denial.

The Medicare-specific trap in this family is unattended electrical stimulation. CPT 97014 describes electrical stimulation without the constant-attendance requirement, but Medicare does not recognize 97014 for Part B claims — MACs require HCPCS G0283 instead for the identical unattended service. A practice billing 97014 to Medicare is billing an invalid code for that payer, even though the same code is perfectly valid to most commercial payers for the same modality. Keep a payer-specific crosswalk in the charge master rather than a single default code for unattended e-stim.

Bundling with same-day therapeutic procedures is common in this family because modalities are frequently used to prepare tissue before manual work or exercise — heat before manual therapy, ultrasound before stretching. Whether a specific modality/procedure pair requires a modifier depends on the same Column 1/Column 2 logic covered above; the practical pattern worth watching is billing a supervised modality (97010) at the same time a timed procedure is also documented as occurring, since the untimed code shouldn't be consuming any of the same clock minutes claimed under the timed codes on the same note.

ICD-10 specificity for PT diagnoses: where claims actually get rejected

We verified the following directly against the current ICD-10-CM code set for this guide, and the pattern is stark: several diagnosis codes still circulating in PT billing templates and competitor guides are not valid for HIPAA-covered transactions at all.

Verified live against the FY2026 ICD-10-CM code set. Category-header codes below are not billable; child codes are.
Category header (not billable)Valid billable children
M54.5 Low back pain — category header only, not valid for HIPAA transactionsM54.50 low back pain, unspecified · M54.51 vertebrogenic low back pain (a newer, more specific code tied to a documented vertebral endplate pain source) · M54.59 other low back pain

M54.51 matters clinically, not just as a coding technicality: vertebrogenic low back pain describes a specific pain generator (degenerative vertebral endplate changes, often correlating with Modic changes on imaging), and using it instead of the unspecified or "other" code when the clinical picture actually supports it is the more accurate diagnosis, not just the more specific one. Coders defaulting to M54.50 for every low back pain patient without checking whether the clinical documentation supports M54.51 are leaving diagnostic precision on the table even where the claim itself doesn't deny.

Laterality and encounter type on S-codes: sprain and strain diagnoses (the S00–S99 injury chapter) require a 7th character denoting encounter type — A for initial encounter (active treatment), D for subsequent encounter (routine healing, follow-up), S for sequela (a late effect of a prior injury) — layered on top of laterality already built into the code itself. We confirmed this structurally: a code like S93.401 (sprain of unspecified ligament, right ankle) is itself a category header and is not billable without the 7th character; S93.401A, S93.401D, and S93.401S are the three billable variants, each describing a different phase of care for the identical injury. Billing the 7th character wrong isn't usually a denial trigger by itself, but it is a documented driver of medical necessity questions on longer episodes of care — a claim still using an "A" (initial encounter, active treatment) code six weeks into a course of PT reads, on its face, as though active injury management is still underway rather than the routine healing phase "D" describes, which invites exactly the kind of scrutiny a therapy threshold review already brings.

Functional-diagnosis alternatives: not every PT referral arrives with a clean orthopedic diagnosis. R26.2 (difficulty walking, not elsewhere classified) and M62.81 (generalized muscle weakness) are both valid, billable codes that function as the primary diagnosis when no orthopedic or neurologic diagnosis has been established but a clear functional deficit drives the medical necessity of the referral — a post-hospitalization deconditioning referral with no specific orthopedic finding is a typical use case for either.

Sequencing: when both a medical diagnosis (the underlying condition, e.g., a specific fracture or a neurologic diagnosis) and a treatment diagnosis (the functional impairment PT is actually addressing, e.g., gait abnormality or muscle weakness) exist, sequencing matters to the payer's medical necessity review. Convention is to lead with the diagnosis that most directly explains why skilled PT is medically necessary for the specific services billed that visit — not necessarily the most severe underlying diagnosis on the patient's full problem list. The complete crosswalk of diagnosis codes to the highest-volume PT procedure codes, plus a full laterality and encounter-type reference, is in our ICD-10 coding guide for physical therapy billing.

Modifiers: GP/GO/GN, 59 and X{EPSU}, KX, 96/97, CQ/CO

PT billing carries more required, load-bearing modifiers per claim line than most specialties, because Medicare uses modifiers to identify which discipline furnished a service, whether the therapy threshold applies, and who actually delivered the care.

Core PT modifier reference.
ModifierFunctionNotes
GPService furnished under a physical therapy plan of careRequired on every PT line, every claim — discipline modifiers are not optional flags, they're claim-processing requirements
GOService furnished under an occupational therapy plan of careDistinguishes OT lines when both disciplines bill the same date

KX threshold mechanics: the KX modifier attaches once a Medicare beneficiary's calendar-year Part B therapy charges cross the CY2026 threshold — $2,480 combined for PT and SLP services together, and a separate $2,480 for OT services. Crossing the dollar figure is a trigger, not a justification by itself: the documentation on file has to independently support that continued therapy remains medically necessary at that point in the episode, meaning the plan of care, progress notes, and functional goals need to show the patient is still making progress toward, or at meaningful risk of losing, a specific functional outcome. Appending KX to a claim without that documentation, purely because the running total crossed the number, is exactly the pattern that draws a targeted medical review once the separate $3,000 threshold is also crossed.

96 vs. 97: rehabilitative therapy (97) restores a function the patient had and lost — post-surgical strength, post-stroke gait. Habilitative therapy (96) develops a function the patient never had to begin with — most commonly relevant in pediatric PT for a developmental delay, or adult patients with a congenital condition. Medicare itself doesn't require this distinction on most claims, but state Medicaid programs and ACA-regulated commercial plans frequently do under habilitative/rehabilitative benefit parity requirements, and the two benefit categories can carry different visit caps or cost-sharing on the same plan — billing the wrong one can misapply a benefit limit that doesn't actually govern that service.

CQ/CO and the de minimis standard: when a PTA or OTA furnishes any part of a billed service, the de minimis standard determines whether the CQ or CO modifier applies and the associated 15% payment reduction is triggered. Under the standard, if the assistant furnishes more than 10% of the total minutes of a given timed service, that unit is billed with CQ or CO and reimburses at 85% of the standard rate; a unit where the assistant's contribution stays at or under that 10% threshold isn't billed with the modifier. This is calculated per unit, not per visit — a visit can legitimately contain a mix of CQ-modified and unmodified units depending on how the minutes actually split between supervising therapist and assistant on each timed code. Full modifier decision trees, including worked CQ/CO minute-splitting examples, are in our physical therapy modifiers guide.

NCCI edits and same-day bundling beyond 97140/97530

The 97140/97530 pairing gets the attention, but it's one edit among many across the 97000 series, and reading an NCCI Procedure-to-Procedure edit table correctly — without paying for a subscription lookup tool — is a skill worth having in-house.

Every PTP edit pair publishes a Column 1 code (generally the more comprehensive or higher-value service), a Column 2 code (the component considered included in Column 1 when billed together), and a modifier indicator:

  • 0Never bypassable. No modifier, however well-documented the distinction, changes the outcome. If a pair carries a 0, the Column 2 code simply isn't separately payable alongside the Column 1 code that date.
  • 1Bypassable with documentation. An NCCI-associated modifier (59 or the specific X-modifier) can override the edit, but only where the record shows the services were genuinely separate — distinct time block, distinct anatomic region, or distinct session.
  • 9Edit deleted. The pair no longer applies going forward; the indicator itself carries no current meaning.

Eval codes bundling with same-day treatment codes is a related but distinct question from PTP procedure-to-procedure edits, because eval codes (97161–97163) aren't E/M codes and the modifier-25-versus-59 distinction that governs a physician office doesn't map directly onto PT billing the same way. Most payers expect a new-patient eval plus same-day treatment as routine, unmodified billing; where a modifier becomes relevant is specifically among the timed treatment codes billed alongside each other, governed by the PTP logic above, not the eval-to-treatment relationship.

MUE limits by code: beyond the pair-level PTP edits, individual codes carry their own per-day Medically Unlikely Edit unit caps, structured by CMS's MUE Adjudication Indicator (MAI) — MAI 1 is a claim-line edit that can sometimes be split across separate lines with documentation, MAI 2 is an absolute date-of-service cap CMS treats as clinically implausible with no appeal path, and MAI 3 is appealable with documentation showing the excess units were genuinely distinct and necessary. ⚠️ This guide could not independently confirm the current MAI value or numeric cap for the specific high-volume PT codes against CMS's primary MUE file during this research pass — the same access issue affecting other CMS.gov coding pages applied here. Confirm the current MAI and unit cap for any code in the CMS NCCI MUE Lookup Tool before relying on a specific number operationally.

Reading the table without a paid tool: the CMS NCCI Procedure-to-Procedure Edits Lookup Tool (published free on CMS.gov, organized by code range and updated quarterly) is the authoritative source for both the Column 1/Column 2 relationship and the modifier indicator for any specific pair. Search by the Column 1 code you're billing, find the Column 2 code in question in the results, and read the indicator column directly rather than relying on a scrubber's built-in logic, which can lag a quarterly update. Full bundling patterns across the entire 97000 series, structured by procedure family with the specific pairs that come up most in PT billing, are covered in our NCCI edits and bundling guide linked above.

Coverage and medical necessity: LCDs, the KX threshold, and maintenance therapy

Outpatient physical therapy coverage under Medicare Part B is governed by a specific Local Coverage Determination, not a generic "Medicare covers PT" assumption: LCD L34428, "Outpatient Physical Therapy," published by Palmetto GBA (the Part A/B MAC covering outpatient services in its jurisdiction), currently effective 05/18/2023 and last updated 05/08/2023 per the CMS Coverage Database. It defines the medical necessity standard, documentation requirements, and the covered-service framework for office, clinic, and hospital-outpatient PT.

This is not the same coverage document as home health PT, and conflating the two is a documented source of coverage-standard errors: home health physical therapy, delivered under a home health plan of care rather than an outpatient episode, falls under a completely separate LCD published by the Home Health and Hospice (HHH) MAC for that jurisdiction — CGS Administrators' L33942, "Physical Therapy – Home Health," or Palmetto GBA's L34564, "Home Health Physical Therapy," depending on which HHH MAC serves the state. Both exist independently of L34428 and neither one's coverage criteria automatically transfers to an outpatient claim, or vice versa. If your practice bills both settings, keep the correct LCD mapped to each service line by site-of-service, not by a single blanket "Medicare PT coverage" reference in your documentation templates.

Maintenance therapy and Jimmo v. Sebelius: the 2013 Jimmo settlement established, and CMS's own manual language now confirms, that skilled therapy services are covered when the skill of a therapist is required to safely and effectively perform a maintenance program — the patient does not have to be improving, or expected to improve, for the service to be covered. What has to be documented instead is why the specific skills of a licensed therapist, not a caregiver or the patient independently, are necessary to maintain the current level of function or prevent decline. A note that simply states "patient has plateaued, continuing current program" without explaining why skilled oversight specifically (rather than a home exercise program alone) remains necessary does not meet the Jimmo standard even though the legal standard itself doesn't require ongoing improvement.

Certification and recertification timing: the initial plan of care must be certified — signed and dated by the referring physician or qualified non-physician practitioner — and the standard window is within 30 days of the initial evaluation. Recertification is required at least every 90 days, or sooner if the plan of care changes materially. As of dates of service on or after January 1, 2025, CMS's PFS final rule created a specific exception for the initial certification only: a signed and dated referral or order in the medical record, combined with documented evidence that the completed plan of care was transmitted to the referring provider within 30 days of the initial evaluation, satisfies certification even without a returned signature — the referring provider's silence functions as acceptance of the submitted plan. This exception does not extend to recertification; a physician or NPP signature is still required every 90 days to recertify, specifically because CMS did not want patients receiving open-ended therapy without a physician periodically re-attesting to its necessity. The full LCD-by-setting breakdown, KX documentation language that survives review, and the certification workflow are in our KX modifier and therapy threshold guide.

Prior authorization in 2026: what's expanding, what's rolling back

Prior authorization for outpatient PT moved in two directions in 2026, from the same payer, within a few months of each other, and treating either announcement as the full picture is a mistake that costs a practice either an unnecessary denial or unnecessary administrative overhead.

Expansion: effective February 1, 2026, UnitedHealthcare extended its existing Medicare Advantage prior-authorization program for outpatient PT, OT, and speech therapy — along with traditional Medicare chiropractic services under the same MA plans — to individual and group retiree plan members in specific Arizona and California plans. The program itself had already been running in other states since September 2024; this was a geographic expansion, not a new program. Providers were able to begin submitting authorization requests for the newly-included plans starting January 1, 2026, ahead of the February 1 effective date.

Rollback: in May 2026, UnitedHealthcare separately announced it would eliminate prior authorization requirements for approximately 30% of the services still requiring it, phased in through the end of 2026, and explicitly named select outpatient therapies and chiropractic care among the categories affected, alongside select outpatient surgeries and some diagnostic imaging. UnitedHealthcare's own public statement characterized this as reducing prior-auth requirements to roughly 2% of its total medical services company-wide, with the large majority of submitted requests already approved within 24 hours.

Both of these are accurate simultaneously because they apply to different, specific service/plan combinations — a practice cannot assume either "UHC now requires more prior auth" or "UHC dropped prior auth for PT" as a blanket rule. The only reliable approach is verifying requirements per plan, per service, before the first visit of a new episode of care, not relying on last quarter's experience with that payer.

CMS's parallel mandate: separately from any individual payer's policy, CMS's Interoperability and Prior Authorization Final Rule (CMS-0057-F) put new operational requirements on impacted payers effective January 1, 2026 — specific, stated reasons for any prior-authorization denial rather than a generic rejection, and adherence to defined decision-turnaround timelines regardless of submission method. Those same payers must publicly report prior-authorization metrics — approval and denial rates, appeal outcomes, average decision time — covering calendar year 2025, with the first report due by March 31, 2026. This doesn't eliminate prior auth anywhere, but it does mean a denial arriving in 2026 without a specific stated reason is itself a compliance gap worth flagging back to the payer.

State Medicaid variation: unlike Medicare and most commercial plans, state Medicaid programs vary widely and structurally — some states impose no hard visit cap on medically necessary PT, while others cap visits per plan year (commonly in the 20-visit range) with mandatory pre-approval required before any visit beyond an initial evaluation-and-short-course allowance. There is no single "Medicaid rule" for PT prior authorization; treat each state Medicaid program as its own payer for authorization-workflow purposes.

Practical verification workflow: confirm authorization requirements as part of the same real-time eligibility check that verifies the therapy benefit itself, before visit one of any new episode — not after the eval is billed. Where a plan requires authorization, capture the approved visit count and expiration date in the scheduling system so it can flag automatically as the count approaches the limit, rather than relying on staff to remember a number from an approval letter weeks into the episode. Full payer-by-payer detail, including how to check MA plan-specific requirements and a state Medicaid quick-reference, is in our prior authorization guide for physical therapy practices.

Dry needling and acupuncture: the coverage confusion competitors get wrong

This is a narrow but consequential coverage question that most PT-focused billing guides either skip or flatten into an inaccurate blanket statement. Here is the coverage rule as CMS actually states it.

Medicare covers acupuncture under National Coverage Determination 30.3.3, and the coverage is narrow by design: only for chronic low back pain, defined specifically as pain lasting 12 weeks or longer, nonspecific (no identifiable systemic cause such as metastatic, inflammatory, or infectious disease), not associated with surgery, and not associated with pregnancy. Covered volume is up to 12 visits in a rolling 90-day period, with an additional 8 sessions available for patients demonstrating documented improvement — for a hard annual ceiling of 20 acupuncture treatments per year, and treatment must stop if the patient isn't improving or is regressing.

The critical language most guides omit entirely, quoted directly from the NCD's non-coverage section: "All types of acupuncture including dry needling for any condition other than cLBP are non-covered by Medicare." That's not us paraphrasing loosely — it's CMS naming dry needling specifically inside the acupuncture NCD and stating plainly that it, along with every other acupuncture technique, is non-covered outside the chronic low back pain indication. There is no separate Medicare coverage pathway for dry needling as a distinct musculoskeletal intervention; the acupuncture NCD is the only Medicare coverage vehicle either service can use, and it only opens for cLBP.

Two related coverage points that are easy to get wrong even within the cLBP exception:

  • 1Same-date billing exclusion. Acupuncture and dry needling are not separately billable for the same patient on the same date of service — they're treated as the same category of intervention for billing purposes even when performed with different technique names, so stacking both on one visit note doesn't create two billable services.
  • 2Practitioner qualification requirements are separate from coverage. The NCD requires acupuncture furnished by physicians under state scope-of-practice rules, or by PAs/NPs/CNSs and auxiliary personnel who additionally hold a master's or doctoral-level ACAOM-accredited acupuncture credential and a current, unrestricted state acupuncture license — a PT license alone does not meet this bar for billing the covered acupuncture codes to Medicare, regardless of state practice-act authority to perform dry needling clinically.

State and commercial variation: state practice acts govern who may legally perform dry needling as a clinical matter — many states explicitly authorize PTs to perform it within scope of practice, some restrict it, and a few prohibit it outright, which is a separate question from Medicare billing coverage entirely. Commercial payers vary independently of both the state practice-act question and Medicare's cLBP-only NCD: some commercial plans cover dry needling under a musculoskeletal procedure code with its own medical necessity criteria, others exclude it outright as experimental or investigational, and the coverage terms live in the specific plan document, not a general "most payers cover it" assumption. Confirm both the state practice-act authority and the specific payer's coverage policy before billing dry needling to any payer, Medicare or otherwise. Full code-level detail, state-by-state practice authority notes, and commercial payer coverage patterns are in our dry needling and acupuncture coverage guide.

Conclusion and next steps

Three fixes account for most of the recoverable revenue in a PT billing operation, and none of them require new software. First, unit math: total timed minutes across all timed codes before converting to units, every time, and audit a sample of charts against the flowsheet to confirm total-time documentation actually reconciles with the units billed. Second, KX documentation: build the medical-necessity language into progress notes before a patient crosses the $2,480 threshold, not as a reactive scramble once a claim denies. Third, ICD-10 specificity: purge M54.5 and any other category-header codes from EHR templates, superbills, and favorites lists, and replace them with the correct child codes before the next batch of claims goes out, not after a denial report flags the pattern.

Everything else in this guide — NCCI overrides, LCD-specific coverage standards, prior-authorization tracking, and the dry needling exception — compounds on top of those three fundamentals. A practice with clean unit math, solid KX documentation, and specific diagnosis coding is already ahead of most of the denial patterns that consume AR staff time in this specialty.

Frequently asked questions

Why is M54.5 rejecting on our claims when it worked fine before?

M54.5 is now a category header only — it was subdivided effective October 1, 2021 and is not valid for HIPAA transactions under the current ICD-10-CM set. You need one of its children: M54.50 (low back pain, unspecified), M54.51 (vertebrogenic low back pain, a newer and more specific code), or M54.59 (other low back pain). If your EHR problem list or a superbill template still has the parent code hardcoded, every claim carrying it denies for an invalid diagnosis code regardless of how sound the clinical picture is.

How do we calculate units under the 8-minute rule when we bill three timed codes in one visit?

Add the total minutes of all timed, constant-attendance codes together first, then convert the sum to units using the CMS unit table (8 to 22 minutes total = 1 unit, 23 to 37 = 2, and so on in 15-minute steps). Then assign those units to the specific codes based on which services took the most time, giving preference to the code with the most minutes when there's a remainder to allocate. Don't calculate units per code independently and then add them — that overcounts because leftover minutes under 8 for one code can combine with leftover minutes from another to form a billable unit.

Can we bill 97140 and 97530 on the same date of service?

Yes, but only with a modifier and only when the documentation shows they happened in separate 15-minute time blocks. NCCI treats 97530 as a Column 2 code bundled into 97140 with a modifier indicator of 1, meaning modifier 59 or the more specific X{EPSU} modifier (XS for separate structure is typical here) can override the edit — but only when the note proves the two services weren't performed during the same interval. Append the modifier without that separation in the documentation and you've created an audit flag, not a legitimate override.

What exactly triggers the KX modifier and what happens if we forget it?

The KX modifier attaches once a Medicare beneficiary's combined PT and SLP billed charges, or OT billed charges separately, cross the CY2026 threshold of $2,480. It has to be supported by documentation in the record showing continued therapy is medically necessary — not just appended because the dollar figure was crossed. Submit a claim above the threshold without KX and it denies outright; that's a clean, avoidable denial that shows up constantly in AR aging reports.

Is dry needling covered by Medicare?

Effectively no. Medicare's acupuncture coverage under NCD 30.3.3 applies only to chronic low back pain, capped at 20 visits a year, and CMS's own coverage language states plainly that all forms of acupuncture, including dry needling, are non-covered for any condition other than cLBP. Even for cLBP, Medicare's covered codes are the acupuncture code set — dry needling billed under a musculoskeletal CPT code isn't the same covered service, and acupuncture and dry needling cannot both be billed for the same patient on the same date.

What's the difference between the outpatient PT LCD and the home health PT LCD?

They're separate coverage documents from separate contractor types and guides that conflate them cause real coverage errors. Outpatient physical therapy — office, clinic, or hospital outpatient department — is governed by Palmetto GBA's LCD L34428. Home health PT, delivered under a home health plan of care, falls under the Home Health and Hospice MAC's own LCD instead: CGS Administrators' L33942 or Palmetto GBA's L34564 depending on jurisdiction. Applying the outpatient LCD's coverage logic to a home health claim, or vice versa, is a documented source of medical necessity denials.

Do we still need to report functional limitation G-codes to Medicare?

No — functional limitation reporting, including the nonpayable G-codes and severity modifiers, was discontinued by CMS for Medicare Part B therapy claims with dates of service on or after January 1, 2019. If your scrubber, superbill, or EHR template still generates those codes, they're dead weight at best and a source of confusion at worst. Some Medicare Advantage plans and commercial payers separately adopted similar functional reporting — check payer-specific requirements rather than assuming FLR is gone everywhere.

What changed with UnitedHealthcare's prior authorization requirements for PT in 2026?

Two things moved in opposite directions the same year. Effective February 1, 2026, UnitedHealthcare expanded its existing Medicare Advantage outpatient therapy prior-auth program to plan members in Arizona and California, with providers able to submit requests starting January 1, 2026. Then in May 2026, UnitedHealthcare announced it would eliminate prior authorization for roughly 30% of remaining requirements by the end of the year, naming select outpatient therapies among the categories affected. Verify current requirements per plan before every new episode of care rather than assuming last quarter's rule still applies.
