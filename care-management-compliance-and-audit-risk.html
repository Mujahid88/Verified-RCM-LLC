Our complete care management guide

Where care management audits look, and what the OIG has already found.

Chronic care management and remote patient monitoring have both been audited by the HHS Office of Inspector General, and the findings repeat: duplicate billers, patients with no history at the practice, thin oversight and time that does not match the notes. This page sets out what the OIG found, how each finding maps onto a care management program, and what to do about it before an auditor asks.

Key takeaways

  • The OIG found $1.9 million in CCM overpayments across 50,192 claims in its 2021 audit, including more than 17,000 claims where more than one practitioner billed the same patient in the same month.
  • A new OIG audit of CCM is active. It looks at payments that may not meet the requirement of multiple chronic conditions.
  • For remote monitoring, the OIG flagged patients with no prior relationship with the practice, enrollment spikes and months with no treatment management.
  • Routine waiver of cost sharing can violate the law. The exceptions are narrow, and a program should never be marketed as covered by insurance.
  • An outside vendor is not a compliance shortcut. CMS says a service with little oversight is not furnished by the practice, and the vendor agreement is an anti-kickback question for your attorney.

Both remote monitoring reports concern remote physiologic monitoring, which is a different service from RTM, but they are the template for how auditors will look at RTM. CMS cited the OIG’s no-prior-relationship finding when it proposed limiting RTM to established patients in 2027.

Cost sharing and marketing

Medicare patients owe the Part B deductible and 20% coinsurance on each billed code, and CMS has no authority to waive it. The OIG’s long-standing position is that routinely waiving copayments or deductibles causes false claims, because the charge is misstated, and is a potential anti-kickback problem. The statute on beneficiary inducement bars offering remuneration likely to influence a patient’s choice of provider. A waiver is outside that definition only if all three conditions hold: it is not advertised or solicited, it is not routine, and it follows a good-faith determination of financial need or reasonable collection efforts that failed.

The practical rule for a care management program: tell the patient the cost sharing in the consent, collect it, handle genuine hardship case by case with documentation, and keep the word “free” and the phrase “covered by insurance” out of every flyer, script and web page. Many patients have supplemental coverage that pays the cost sharing, which is different from the practice waiving it.

Outside vendors

A program that uses an outside team has three separate questions to answer, and the first two are Medicare billing rules and the third is a contract question for an attorney.

  • Is the staff eligible today? Clinical staff under general supervision who are employees, leased employees or contractors can count, if they meet state requirements, are not excluded from federal programs and work from the United States.
  • Will the arrangement survive 2027? For RTM and RPM, the proposal would require direct employees of the billing practice. Contracts that bill RTM on outside staff need a plan for January 1, 2027.
  • Is the compensation safe? Arrangements where the vendor is paid per patient or a share of collections attract scrutiny. A flat, fair-market-value fee set in advance and unrelated to referrals is the safer structure. Your attorney should confirm.

Preparing for an audit request

  1. Keep the documents togetherFor each patient: the initiating visit note, consent, the practitioner enrollment note, the care plan with approvals, the time log, call notes, the billing file row and the payer benefits check.
  2. Keep the program documents togetherThe staff roster with licenses and exclusion checks, signed protocols, training records, the RTM intended use statement and regulatory opinion, and the vendor agreement.
  3. Run your own audit firstThe monthly scorecard in the SOP page is built to find what an auditor would. Fix critical failures before billing.
  4. Know who answersName the person who responds to a records request and the attorney who reviews the response.

Frequently asked questions

Is chronic care management being audited?

Yes. The OIG’s 2021 audit found about $1.9 million in overpayments, mostly duplicate billing for the same patient in the same period, and the OIG announced a new audit of CCM payments in March 2026 that looks at the multiple chronic conditions requirement.

Can we waive the patient’s share if they cannot pay?

Only case by case. A waiver is not treated as remuneration only if it is not advertised, not routine and follows a good-faith determination of financial need or failed reasonable collection efforts. Routine waivers are the problem. Talk to your attorney about a written hardship policy.

Why does the OIG care about patients with no history at the practice?

Because remote services billed for patients the practice has never seen suggest enrollment driven by a billing company, not by the practitioner’s clinical judgment. In its remote monitoring snapshot, the OIG found practices with no prior relationship with most of their patients.

Does using an outside vendor increase audit risk?

It adds questions rather than risk by itself: whether the staff are eligible, whether the practitioner supervises in fact, and whether the payment arrangement is safe. The 2027 proposal would also end contracted-staff RTM under Medicare if finalized as proposed.

What should we do if our own audit finds a critical failure on a paid claim?

Stop billing the pattern and ask your compliance officer or healthcare attorney what the overpayment report-and-return rules require. Do not decide it yourself, and do not wait.
