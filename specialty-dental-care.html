Dental billing and coding: the complete guide.

Dental billing runs two code systems that rarely agree with each other. CDT is the ADA's HIPAA-mandated D-code set for dental payers; CPT and ICD-10-CM are what medical payers demand the instant a service stops being routine dental care and becomes medically necessary — trauma, pathology, a transplant workup, a cancer-treatment prerequisite. Most guides in this niche name four or five crossover codes and stop there. This guide builds the actual code tables: tooth-loss class I–IV by cause in K08.4x, laterality and encounter type in S02.6x mandible fractures, and the NCCI bundling risk on the medical side of a crossover claim that nobody else covers.

Reviewed by Phares A., MD · Physiotherapist, CPC (AAPC) · Updated August 2026

Key takeaways

  • CDT and CPT/ICD-10 are not interchangeable. Dental payers reject CPT-only claims; medical payers reject dental-only diagnosis codes outright. Cross-coding is translation, not substitution — and getting that distinction wrong is the single biggest structural error in this niche.
  • CDT 2026 added 31 codes and deleted 6, including D1352 and D9248, effective January 1, 2026. Billing a deleted code on a 2026 date of service denies on code validity before anything else is checked.
  • Medicare's dental exclusion has a real, verified exception. CMS's 2023 final rule covers dental work that's "inextricably linked" to a covered medical service — a pre-transplant exam, pre-radiation extractions, jaw fracture fixation — and it reaches further than most practices realize.
  • Extraction + same-day anesthesia + same-day E/M is dental's highest-dollar bundling risk on a medical crossover claim, and it's the gap every competitor guide we reviewed skips entirely.

Dental billing at a glance

Three code systems govern a dental practice's revenue, and confusing them is where most claims go wrong before a single procedure code is even selected. CDT is the dental-specific system; CPT and ICD-10-CM are the medical system that only enters the picture once a service crosses out of routine dental care. Practices that treat this as one continuous coding decision — rather than two separate systems that occasionally need to talk to each other — lose revenue on the medical side and generate denials on the dental side.

The three code systems in dental billing, and where they actually intersect.
Code setMaintained byRequired byWhere it intersects
CDT (D-codes)American Dental Association (ADA)Dental insurance plans, dental Medicaid/CHIP, DHMO/DPPO carriers — HIPAA-mandated for dental transactionsNever billed directly to a medical payer; crosses over only via translation to CPT
CPTAmerican Medical Association (AMA)Medical insurance plans — commercial medical, Medicare Part B, medical MedicaidThe procedure code medical payers require the moment a dental-adjacent service becomes medically necessary
ICD-10-CMCDC's National Center for Health Statistics, under the WHO frameworkMedical payers, required alongside CPT on every medical claimMost dental payers don't require a diagnosis code at all; medical payers treat ICD-10 specificity as a coverage gate, not a formality

The practical consequence: a claim built entirely in CDT logic — procedure code, tooth number, no diagnosis — is structurally incapable of satisfying a medical payer, and a claim built entirely in CPT/ICD-10 logic without the corresponding D-code documentation loses the dental benefit that may still apply to the same visit. The rest of this guide treats that distinction as the organizing principle, not a footnote, because it's the one competitor guides in this space consistently gloss over — naming a handful of crossover codes without ever explaining why the two systems exist separately or when a claim legitimately needs both.

Which side of the practice generates crossover volume tracks specialty fairly predictably. A general dentist's crossover claims are mostly trauma-driven and occasional — an avulsed tooth from a fall, a fractured jaw referred out. Oral and maxillofacial surgery practices live in crossover territory by default, because impacted-tooth extraction, biopsy, and fracture-reduction work sits on the medical/dental boundary as a matter of routine, not exception. Pediatric dentistry crosses over around trauma and, increasingly, sleep-appliance and airway work. Knowing which pattern your practice fits changes how much workflow investment in dual-system verification and predetermination actually pays for itself — a general practice billing one crossover claim a quarter needs a checklist, not a dedicated biller; an OMS practice needs the reverse.

What changed for 2026

CDT 2026 carries 60 total changes, effective January 1, 2026: 31 new codes, 14 revised, 6 deleted, and 9 editorial-only edits with no coding impact. Four changes matter operationally for a practice billing today.

The practical fallout mirrors every other specialty's annual code-set transition: if a practice management system's fee schedule or a payer's claim edits still reference D1352 or D9248 on a 2026 date of service, that line denies on invalid code before medical or dental necessity is ever evaluated. Audit the charge master and any claim-scrubber rules against this table before the next batch of claims goes out, not after the first round of denials comes back — the same lesson every specialty's annual code update teaches, every year.

D0426 and D0461 are worth flagging specifically because they're new diagnostic categories, not just new billing codes for existing chairside work, and payer adjudication behavior for genuinely new diagnostic codes in their first year is inconsistent by nature — some plans process them against an existing diagnostic-services benefit bucket, others deny them outright as non-covered until a policy update catches up. Verify coverage for D0426 and D0461 with a practice's top payers directly rather than assuming either processes cleanly just because the code exists in the 2026 set; the same caution applies to D9224/D9225 given that anesthesia-with-advanced-airway is a narrower clinical scenario than general anesthesia billing generally, and a payer's existing anesthesia policy may not yet distinguish the two.

Two code systems, one mouth: CDT vs. CPT/ICD-10

CDT exists because dental insurance is structurally different from medical insurance — it's built around annual maximums, missing-tooth clauses, and frequency limitations rather than medical necessity review, and the D-code set reflects that: procedure-first, diagnosis-optional. HIPAA named CDT as the mandated code set for dental transactions the same way it named CPT for medical transactions, so a dental payer processing a claim expects a D-code, a tooth or quadrant designation, and usually nothing else. Submit a CPT code to a dental plan and it typically gets rejected as an invalid procedure code outright, not just paid incorrectly.

Medical payers run the opposite logic. A CPT code alone, without a supporting ICD-10-CM diagnosis that establishes medical necessity, doesn't get adjudicated as a routine claim — it gets denied or pended for records. And critically, a dental-only diagnosis (a CDT tooth-surface or periodontal classification) means nothing to a medical claims processor; medical payers require an ICD-10-CM code that describes the underlying medical condition — a fracture, a diagnosed lesion, an infection with systemic risk — not a description of the tooth. Submitting "dental caries" as a medical diagnosis without the trauma, pathology, or systemic component that actually justifies medical billing is one of the most common reasons a crossover claim denies before it ever reaches a medical-necessity review.

Cross-coding is the practice of describing the same clinical event in both languages when the event genuinely straddles both systems — not substituting one code set for the other. A tooth avulsed in a motor vehicle accident is documented with a CDT extraction code for the dental record and, on the medical claim, a CPT procedure code paired with an ICD-10 fracture-of-tooth or facial-trauma code that establishes why a medical payer should pay for it. Get the direction backwards — billing CPT to the dental plan, or D-codes to the medical plan — and the claim doesn't deny for lack of documentation, it denies because the payer's system can't even parse it as a valid transaction type.

Dual claims, where both a dental and a medical claim are filed for the same visit, are sometimes correct and sometimes fraud, and the difference is what each claim actually bills for. It's legitimate when the dental claim covers a distinct, unrelated routine service performed the same day (a cleaning, alongside a trauma-related extraction billed to medical) or when coordination-of-benefits rules on the specific plans genuinely allow both to adjudicate the same procedure as primary and secondary. It is never legitimate to bill the identical procedure to both payers as if each were the sole primary claim; that's duplicate billing regardless of how the two code sets differ, and it reads that way to a payer's coordination-of-benefits system even when no one intended it as fraud. This translation logic, procedure by procedure, along with the bundling risk that shows up only once a crossover claim reaches the medical side, is covered in full later in this guide.

Exam and evaluation codes — dental's E/M equivalent

Dental's exam codes function like E/M leveling does in medicine: get the wrong level and either the claim underpays or, more often, it gets downcoded on review because the documentation didn't support what was billed.

Core exam and evaluation codes and the frequency traps payers actually enforce.
CodeServiceCommon frequency trap
D0120Periodic oral evaluation, established patientTypically limited to twice per benefit year by most plans — a third visit in the same year denies as frequency, not as a coding error
D0140Limited, problem-focused oral evaluationUsed for a specific complaint, not as a substitute for a periodic or comprehensive exam that's simply hit its frequency cap
D0150Comprehensive oral evaluation, new or established patientMost payers limit this to once per provider, sometimes once per lifetime per payer — billing it again for a returning patient the payer has on file, even years later, downcodes to limited
D0180Comprehensive periodontal evaluation2026 descriptor now requires the exam be tied to documented signs, symptoms, systemic conditions, or social risk factors, with probing depths on the chart — a claim without that documentation downcodes
D0170Re-evaluation, limited, problem-focused, established patientNot billable as a routine post-operative follow-up; reserved for a new or changed problem since the last visit
D9110Palliative (emergency) treatment of dental pain, minor procedureCovers the emergency visit itself, not a substitute for billing the definitive procedure once diagnosed — and it doesn't stack with a same-day comprehensive exam on most plans

The new-versus-established distinction in dentistry tracks the payer relationship, not just the practice: a patient new to your office but with a D0150 already on file with the same insurance carrier from a different provider often still trips the once-per-lifetime-per-payer limit, because the frequency edit runs against the payer's claims history, not the practice's. Verify benefit history at the payer level before billing D0150, not just the practice's own records.

Do
  • Verify the payer's D0150 history before billing a "new patient" comprehensive exam — the payer's system, not the practice's chart, decides whether it's actually first.
  • Chart the specific signs, symptoms, or risk factors driving a D0180 — the 2026 descriptor makes this a claim requirement, not just good documentation practice.
Don't
  • Don't bill D0170 as a routine post-treatment check-in — it requires a new or changed problem, not a scheduled follow-up.
  • Don't stack D9110 with a same-day comprehensive exam and expect both to pay on most plans — the emergency visit and the full evaluation frequently bundle.

Core procedure family: preventive and restorative

Preventive and restorative work generates the highest claim volume in a general dental practice, and it's also where frequency limitations and alternate-benefit downcoding do the most quiet damage — damage that doesn't show up as a denial, just as a lower payment nobody flags.

Prophylaxis and direct restoration codes, by surface count and material.
CodeServiceBilling note
D1110Prophylaxis, adultMost plans limit to twice per benefit year; periodontal maintenance (D4910) is a separate code family, not a substitute when the patient has active perio disease
D1120Prophylaxis, childAge cutoff for adult vs. child prophylaxis varies by payer — commonly age 14, but confirm per plan rather than assuming
D2140–D2161Amalgam restorations, one through four-or-more surfaces, primary or permanentCoded strictly by surface count actually restored, not by cavity size or chair time
D2330–D2335Resin-based composite, anterior teeth, one through four-or-more surfacesSurface count follows the same rule as amalgam — count what's actually restored
D2391–D2394Resin-based composite, posterior teeth, one through four-or-more surfacesThe code most exposed to alternate-benefit downcoding — see below

Alternate benefit provisions are the single most common source of silent underpayment in restorative billing: a plan pays a posterior composite (D2391–D2394) at the equivalent amalgam rate, on the theory that amalgam is the "basic" material and composite is an elective upgrade, even where the tooth itself has no clinical reason to prefer one material over the other. The claim doesn't deny — it processes and pays, just at a lower rate than the code billed, which means it never lands on a denials work queue for anyone to catch. The fix is upstream: know which plans in your payer mix carry an alternate benefit clause for posterior composite before the claim goes out, and where clinically supportable, document the specific reason composite was necessary (isthmus width, cusp involvement, an esthetic zone that extends further posterior than the plan assumes) so an appeal has something to point to.

Two adjacent code families feed directly into restorative volume and carry their own limitations worth building into a fee schedule review: sealants (D1351) are typically age- and tooth-restricted — most plans cover them only on permanent molars and premolars for patients under a set age, commonly 14 to 16, and deny them outright on teeth already carrying a restoration — and topical fluoride (D1206 varnish, D1208 other topical application) is frequency-limited the same way prophylaxis is, usually to twice per benefit year, with some plans extending a third application only for documented high-caries-risk patients. Both are routinely billed alongside a periodic exam and prophylaxis on the same visit, and all three share the same benefit-year clock, so a practice's own internal scheduling can inadvertently trigger a frequency denial on one of the three even when the other two are within limits.

Core procedure family: oral surgery and extractions (medical crossover)

Extractions are where dental and medical billing collide most often, because the same clinical event — a tooth coming out — can legitimately be billed as routine dental care, as a medical crossover claim, or occasionally as both, depending entirely on why the extraction happened.

Extraction tiers by complexity, from simple to impacted.

Bill medical instead of, or alongside, dental whenever the extraction is driven by trauma, pathology, or a documented medical-necessity prerequisite rather than routine dental disease: a tooth fractured or avulsed in an accident, an extraction required to access and biopsy a suspicious lesion, or extractions performed to prepare the jaw for head-and-neck radiation — one of CMS's own named examples of a dental service "inextricably linked" to a covered medical service, covered in full in the payer nuances section below. Full oral surgery and tooth extraction billing guide walks through worked scenarios for each tier, including flap-and-bone documentation requirements that hold up on audit.

There is no CPT code that maps one-to-one onto a dental extraction, because extraction itself is fundamentally a dental procedure; medical crossover billing generally uses 41899 (unlisted procedure, dentoalveolar structures) with a detailed operative narrative attached, since no more specific listed CPT code exists for a straightforward extraction performed for medical reasons. Where the surgical work goes beyond extraction — excising the lesion itself, or reducing an associated fracture — a specific CPT code does apply: 41825–41827 for excision of a lesion or tumor of the dentoalveolar structures (paraphrased; specific code depends on size and whether repair is performed), and 21461/21462 for open treatment of a mandibular fracture, without or with interdental fixation respectively.

Pair the CPT code with the ICD-10-CM diagnosis that actually establishes medical necessity, not a dental diagnosis: S02.5XXA for a traumatic tooth fracture (initial encounter, closed fracture) rather than a caries or periodontal code, and the site-and-laterality-specific mandible fracture codes in the S02.6 family, covered in full detail in the ICD-10 specificity section below. ⚠️ Dollar amounts for 41899 and similar unlisted-procedure crossover claims are contractor-priced and vary by locality, payer, and the complexity documented in the operative narrative; this build did not attempt to state a reimbursement figure for it — check the CMS Physician Fee Schedule Look-Up Tool by locality and year, or the specific payer's unlisted-code pricing policy, before quoting a number to a provider or patient.

Anesthesia and sedation billed alongside extraction work carries its own CDT code family — general anesthesia and IV sedation are reported in time-based increments (the new D9224/D9225 pair covers general anesthesia with advanced airway specifically; older codes cover standard general anesthesia and IV moderate sedation on the same first-increment/subsequent-increment structure) — and both the anesthesia record and the surgical note need to independently support the time billed, since anesthesia time and surgical time are documented and audited separately even when they're part of the same visit. A surgical note that supports a 45-minute extraction paired with an anesthesia record billing 90 minutes invites exactly the kind of documentation-mismatch scrutiny that slows down an otherwise clean claim.

Bone grafting placed at the time of extraction (D7953 socket preservation, or the broader ridge-augmentation family) deserves a note here because it sits at the seam between the extraction and implant sections of this guide: when a graft is placed specifically to preserve ridge volume for a planned future implant, that clinical intent belongs in the chart at the time of extraction, not reconstructed later when the implant claim is submitted — a graft claim with no stated purpose reads as a routine extraction add-on, while the same graft documented as implant-site preparation supports the staged-treatment narrative implant claims often need for predetermination. The full implant timeline, including how socket preservation grafts connect to the surgical-to-restorative sequence, continues in the next section.

Core procedure family: implants and prosthodontics

Implant billing spans the longest timeline of any dental procedure family — surgical placement, a healing period measured in months, then restorative crown placement — and each stage is billed separately, on its own claim, often to different benefit categories within the same plan.

Core implant and abutment codes across the surgical-to-restorative timeline.
CodeServiceStage
D6010Surgical placement of implant body, endosteal implant (full-size)Surgical
D6013Surgical placement of mini implantSurgical
D6056Prefabricated abutmentRestorative, after osseointegration
D6057Custom fabricated abutmentRestorative, after osseointegration
D6058–D6067Abutment- and implant-supported crowns, spanning porcelain/ceramic, porcelain-fused-to-metal, and full-cast-metal options by metal typeRestorative, final

Medical crossover applies to implants placed for reconstruction after trauma, after oncologic resection of jaw structures, or for a congenital absence of teeth or alveolar bone — not for elective replacement of a tooth lost to ordinary decay or periodontal disease. On the medical side, this typically falls under 21248/21249 (reconstruction of the mandible or maxilla with an endosteal implant, partial vs. complete arch, paraphrased), paired with an ICD-10 diagnosis that establishes the trauma, resection, or congenital basis for the reconstruction — a routine tooth-loss code from the K08.4x family covered below will not, by itself, support a medical crossover claim for an implant.

Predetermination is close to mandatory for implant cases, not optional best practice, because most dental plans carry either an outright implant exclusion, a missing-tooth clause (denying coverage for a tooth missing before the policy's effective date), or a waiting period that a same-day treatment plan can blow through without anyone noticing until the claim denies. Submit predetermination before the surgical stage, not after, and re-verify it's still active before the restorative stage begins months later — plan years change, and a predetermination issued under last year's benefit structure doesn't automatically carry forward.

Bone augmentation ahead of placement is common enough to plan for as its own billing stage rather than an exception: sinus augmentation (the D7951/D7952 family) and ridge/site-specific alveoloplasty or grafting (D7953, and the broader D6104 bone graft at time of implant placement) are billed separately from the implant body itself, on their own timeline, and a plan's implant exclusion doesn't automatically extend to the augmentation procedure — verify each stage's benefit separately rather than assuming a single predetermination covers the whole arc from graft to crown. The typical sequence runs graft (if needed) → healing, commonly four to six months for a sinus lift → implant body placement → osseointegration healing, commonly three to six months depending on site and bone quality → abutment → final crown, and a claim for any stage submitted while eligibility, plan year, or predetermination status has lapsed from the prior stage restarts the same verification work the first stage should have already settled. Full staged-billing sequencing, denial patterns specific to each stage, and the medical-crossover documentation bundle are in our dental implant billing guide.

Class I–IV describes the extent and pattern of the edentulous arch (a standard prosthodontic classification), and it's the axis every competitor guide we reviewed skips — they cite K08.1 or K08.4 as a category header, which isn't even billable on its own, without ever reaching the class-and-cause-specific codes that actually determine coverage for a prosthetic or implant claim.

Mandible fractures (S02.6 family) carry the same depth: site (unspecified body, condylar process, subcondylar process, coronoid process, ramus, angle, symphysis, or alveolus), laterality (right, left, or unspecified side, where applicable), and a mandatory 7th-character encounter type — A initial encounter for closed fracture, B initial encounter for open fracture, D subsequent encounter with routine healing, G subsequent encounter with delayed healing, K subsequent encounter with nonunion, and S for sequela. A traumatic tooth fracture uses the related but distinct S02.5XX family with the same encounter-type suffixes. Billing a follow-up visit with an initial-encounter suffix, or vice versa, is a specificity error that reads as a coding mistake to a payer regardless of how clinically obvious the visit's actual stage was.

Malocclusion and dentofacial anomaly codes (M26 family) matter most on the orthodontic side of the practice: M26.211, M26.212, and M26.213 report Angle's Class I, II, and III malocclusion specifically, versus the far less useful M26.219 (unspecified class) or M26.4 (malocclusion, unspecified) that most orthodontic medical-necessity denials trace back to. Two further codes round out the crossover set: G47.33 (obstructive sleep apnea, adult or pediatric) supports medical billing for an oral appliance fabricated to manage OSA — a growing crossover category as more practices add sleep-appliance work — and Z46.3 (encounter for fitting and adjustment of a dental prosthetic device) covers a prosthesis adjustment visit rather than the original fabrication. Modifiers and tooth/quadrant/surface designators

CDT doesn't use CPT-style modifiers at all — the disambiguation system is built into the claim differently, through tooth number, quadrant, and surface designation, and confusing the two systems' logic is a fast way to build a claims scrubber that doesn't actually catch anything.

Tooth numbering
  • Universal numbering 1–32 for permanent teeth; letters A–T for primary (deciduous) teeth — the system CDT claims are built around.
  • Required on virtually every restorative, endodontic, and surgical CDT claim; omitting it or using the wrong system (Palmer notation, FDI) for a US dental payer is a routine rejection reason.
Quadrant and surface
  • Quadrant designators (UL, UR, LL, LR) apply to full-quadrant procedures like scaling and root planing or full-mouth debridement.
  • Surface letters — mesial (M), occlusal (O), distal (D), buccal (B), lingual (L), incisal (I) — determine restorative code selection by count, not just location.
Applying CPT modifiers to a CDT claim
  • Modifiers 25, 59, 50, and 26/TC belong to the CPT/HCPCS system and mean nothing to a dental payer's CDT adjudication logic — they only apply once the claim has actually crossed to the medical side.

On the medical side of a crossover claim, standard CPT modifiers do apply, and four carry most of the weight: 25, appended to an E/M or oral evaluation billed the same day as a minor procedure, when the documentation shows a significant, separately identifiable service beyond the pre-procedure workup; 59 and the more specific X-modifiers (XE, XP, XS, XU), used to bypass an NCCI bundling edit only where the record genuinely supports a distinct procedural service, covered in depth in the NCCI section below; 50, for a bilateral procedure performed on both sides in the same session, relevant when bilateral extractions or bilateral implant placement are billed under a single CPT code that doesn't already account for bilaterality; and 26/TC, the professional/technical component split, which shows up on crossover claims mainly around imaging — panoramic and CBCT imaging billed medically splits the same way diagnostic imaging does anywhere else in medicine. Modifier 22 (increased procedural services) is worth knowing too: an oral surgeon documenting a genuinely unusual complication on a crossover extraction or fracture-reduction claim — aberrant root or nerve anatomy, extensive additional dissection — can append it with a comparison narrative, though payers scrutinize 22 heavily and it only survives review when the note quantifies what made the case unusual rather than simply asserting difficulty.

One further wrinkle in tooth numbering worth flagging: supernumerary (extra) permanent teeth are numbered 51–82, continuing sequentially from the standard 1–32 system rather than restarting, and a claim that omits this or mislabels a supernumerary tooth with a standard number creates a mismatch against the patient's existing dental record that can stall claim processing even when the procedure code itself is correct. The full tooth-numbering system, surface-code combinations by restoration type, and the medical-modifier decision tree are in our dental billing modifiers, tooth numbers, and surface codes guide.

NCCI edits and bundling on the medical side

This is the section every competitor guide we reviewed skips entirely, and it's where the highest dollar risk sits in dental-to-medical crossover billing, because a bundled or capped code isn't a documentation problem — it's a structural one that no amount of narrative fixes after the fact.

  • 1Extraction + same-day anesthesia. General anesthesia or deep sedation billed alongside a surgical extraction CPT/unlisted-code claim is a common NCCI Procedure-to-Procedure pairing point. Whether it's separately payable depends on the pair's modifier indicator, which is code-specific and changes quarterly — never assume last quarter's answer still holds.
  • 2Extraction + same-day E/M. An oral evaluation or E/M billed the same date as a surgical extraction bundles into the procedure's global work unless modifier 25 is appended and the note documents a significant, separately identifiable service — not just the pre-procedure assessment that's inherent to any surgical visit.
  • 3Multiple-procedure reduction. Multiple extractions billed as CPT/unlisted-code lines on the same date, rather than as CDT lines to a dental plan, are subject to standard multiple-surgery reduction logic on the medical side — a different payment mechanic than dental's per-tooth fee schedule, and one that catches practices used to dental-only billing off guard the first time they bill a multi-tooth trauma case to medical.

MUE awareness matters specifically for quadrant-based and per-tooth medical crossover codes: a code capped at a low per-day unit count denies any excess units outright regardless of how many teeth were genuinely, medically involved, unless the claim is split across lines with supporting documentation the way any MUE-capped code would be handled elsewhere in medicine. This is exactly the scenario a multi-tooth trauma case creates: four teeth avulsed in a single accident, billed as four lines of the same unlisted or excision CPT code, can trip a per-day unit cap that was never designed with a mass-casualty dental trauma pattern in mind — documenting each tooth's specific site and clinical finding on its own line, rather than one combined narrative covering all four, is what makes a line-split appeal defensible if the cap fires.

⚠️ This build could not open CMS's primary NCCI Procedure-to-Procedure edit files or the NCCI Policy Manual directly to confirm the specific modifier-indicator values for extraction-plus-anesthesia and extraction-plus-E/M pairs against the source — CMS's site returned an access error to automated fetch attempts made while researching this page, the same pattern seen across CMS's coding and billing pages generally. Treat the bundling relationships described above as reported-but-unconfirmed at the specific-indicator level, and verify the current pair value in the CMS NCCI PTP Edits Lookup Tool before building any of it into a scrubber rule. Payer and coverage nuances: Medicare, Medicaid, and dual coverage

"Medicare doesn't cover dental" is the answer every competitor guide gives, and it's close enough to true to be dangerous, because the actual exception is narrow, specific, and verifiable — and missing it means leaving a legitimately billable service unbilled.

The exclusion itself is statutory: §1862(a)(12) of the Social Security Act excludes items and services connected to the care, treatment, filling, removal, or replacement of teeth or structures directly supporting them. We verified the foundational exception directly against CMS's own coverage database: NCD 260.6, "Dental Examination Prior to Kidney Transplantation", confirms that a dental or oral examination performed on an inpatient basis as part of a comprehensive pre-transplant workup is covered — not because the exclusion doesn't apply, but because the purpose of the exam isn't dental care itself, it's identifying infection risk before a complex surgery. The NCD is explicit on how it's billed: under Part A if performed by a dentist on the hospital's staff, or under Part B if performed by a physician (a dentist performing the exam is not recognized as a "physician" under §1861(r) for this purpose).

CMS extended that same reasoning considerably further in a 2023 final rule, codifying coverage for dental services "inextricably linked to, and substantially related and integral to the clinical success of," another covered medical service, regardless of inpatient or outpatient setting. The rule names specific examples beyond the kidney-transplant NCD: dental ridge reconstruction performed at the same time as surgical tumor removal, wiring or immobilization of teeth as part of reducing a jaw fracture, extraction of teeth to prepare the jaw for head-and-neck radiation therapy, dental splints used in conjunction with medically necessary treatment of a medical condition, and a dental or oral exam before or alongside covered dialysis for end-stage renal disease. CMS was explicit that this list of examples is illustrative, not exhaustive — other pre-surgical or pre-treatment dental clearance scenarios with a comparably direct clinical link can qualify using the same reasoning, evaluated case by case. Medicare Administrative Contractors implement this through billing-and-coding guidance — Palmetto GBA's Article A59449, "Billing and Coding: Dental Services" (effective 06/08/2023, confirmed via the CMS Coverage Database), is one such article, and per secondary reporting these claims commonly require the KX modifier to attest the inextricable-link criteria are met. ⚠️ This build confirmed the article's identifier, contractor, and effective date directly through the CMS Coverage MCP connector, but could not open the article's full text directly (CMS's coverage-database article pages returned an access error to automated retrieval) — verify the complete code list and KX-modifier documentation requirements against the current article text, or your own MAC's equivalent article, before billing against this exception.

Medicare Advantage plans layer a separate dental benefit on top of this narrow traditional-Medicare exception, and the two shouldn't be confused: most MA plans now include some dental rider, ranging from preventive-only coverage to comprehensive benefits with an annual dollar cap, but that coverage exists under the MA plan's own supplemental-benefit design, not under traditional Medicare's statutory framework — a service covered under an MA dental rider tells you nothing about whether it would also qualify under the §1862(a)(12) exception, and the two shouldn't be cited interchangeably when explaining a denial to a patient.

Medicaid splits sharply by age. For anyone under 21, dental coverage is mandatory in every state under the Early and Periodic Screening, Diagnostic and Treatment (EPSDT) benefit, codified at 42 CFR §441.56: states must cover pain relief, infection treatment, tooth restoration, and maintenance of dental health, at intervals meeting reasonable dental-practice standards, and coverage cannot be limited to emergency-only services for this population. For adults 21 and older, dental coverage under Medicaid is a state option, not a federal mandate — some states offer comprehensive adult dental benefits, others offer emergency-only coverage, and others offer none at all, so verify the specific state program's covered CDT codes and any annual benefit cap before assuming coverage exists. Full detail on both programs, including state-by-state EPSDT documentation requirements and MA dental-rider verification workflow, is in our Medicare, Medicaid, and dental insurance coverage rules guide.

Prior authorization and predetermination

These two get treated as synonyms constantly, and they aren't: predetermination is a voluntary, non-binding cost estimate a dental plan issues before treatment; prior authorization is a binding requirement, most relevant on the medical side of a crossover claim, that has to be satisfied before the service is performed or the claim denies outright regardless of medical necessity.

  • 1Almost always warrants predetermination. Implants, comprehensive orthodontic treatment, surgical extractions, and crown-and-bridge work — not because it's contractually mandatory the way medical prior auth is, but because elective-exclusion clauses, missing-tooth clauses, and waiting periods make an unverified treatment plan a real financial risk for both the practice and the patient.
  • 2Typical turnaround. Most dental plans return a predetermination response in two to four weeks; medical prior authorization on a crossover claim's medical-necessity components can run faster or slower depending on the payer and whether the request is routine or expedited — verify the specific payer's stated turnaround before promising a patient a timeline.
  • 3Starting treatment before it returns. Predetermination isn't a guarantee — it's an estimate based on the information submitted, and a plan can still deny or reduce payment on the actual claim if the treatment performed differs from what was predetermined, or if the patient's eligibility changed between submission and treatment. Treatment started before a predetermination response is on file shifts financial risk squarely onto the practice and patient, which is exactly the scenario predetermination exists to prevent.

The practical workflow: submit predetermination as early as the treatment plan is finalized, confirm it's still active immediately before treatment (not just at the original submission date), and treat any procedure crossing to a medical claim as requiring the medical payer's own prior-authorization process on top of, not instead of, dental predetermination — the two systems don't share a review, even when they're evaluating clinically related work.

Comprehensive orthodontic treatment adds a layer neither implants nor surgical extractions have: staged authorization across a multi-year treatment course. A single predetermination covering the full course of banding, adjustment visits, and retention doesn't guarantee each individual claim submitted over the following 18 to 30 months processes without incident — a patient's coverage can lapse, a plan year can change the annual maximum, or a plan can be replaced entirely mid-treatment when an employer switches carriers. Practices billing ortho routinely build a mid-treatment eligibility re-check into their workflow specifically because the original predetermination's assumptions about coverage don't reliably hold for the full length of a multi-year banding-and-retention case.

Frequently asked questions

What's the difference between CDT codes and CPT codes for dental claims?

CDT (D-codes) is the ADA's HIPAA-mandated code set for dental payers — dental insurance, dental Medicaid, DHMO/DPPO plans — and most dental payers don't require a diagnosis code at all. CPT and ICD-10-CM are what medical payers require the moment a service becomes medically necessary rather than routine dental care: trauma, pathology, a pre-transplant workup, a cancer-treatment prerequisite. A dental-only diagnosis or a D-code on a medical claim gets rejected outright; cross-coding is translating the same clinical event into the second system's language, not swapping one code for another.

What changed in CDT for 2026?

CDT 2026 carries 60 total changes effective January 1, 2026: 31 new codes, 14 revised, 6 deleted, and 9 editorial-only edits. The additions billers will actually use include D0426 (point-of-care saliva sample collection and analysis), D0461 (cracked tooth testing), and D9224/D9225 (general anesthesia with advanced airway, first and subsequent 15-minute increments). D1352 (preventive resin restoration) and D9248 (non-intravenous conscious sedation) were deleted. D0180's descriptor was revised to require explicit signs, symptoms, or documented risk factors. Billing any of the deleted codes on a 2026 date of service denies on code validity before anything else is evaluated.

Does Medicare ever cover dental work?

Rarely, but not never. The Social Security Act's §1862(a)(12) dental exclusion has a real exception: CMS's 2023 final rule covers dental services that are "inextricably linked to, and substantially related and integral to the clinical success of" an otherwise-covered medical service. The named examples are a dental exam before a renal transplant (NCD 260.6), dental ridge reconstruction done at the same time as tumor removal, wiring or immobilizing teeth as part of jaw fracture reduction, tooth extraction to prepare the jaw for head-and-neck radiation, and a dental exam before or alongside covered dialysis. The service is billed under Part A if a hospital-staff dentist performs it, or Part B if a physician does.

Does Medicaid cover dental care for adults?

For anyone under 21, yes — mandatorily, in every state, under the EPSDT benefit (42 CFR §441.56). States must cover pain relief, infection treatment, restoration, and maintenance, and cannot limit pediatric coverage to emergencies only. For adults 21 and older, dental coverage under Medicaid is a state option, not a federal requirement, so benefits and even the existence of coverage vary sharply by state — verify eligibility and covered CDT codes against the specific state Medicaid dental fee schedule before assuming a service is covered.

When do we bill a medical crossover claim instead of, or alongside, a dental claim?

Bill medical whenever the service is driven by trauma, pathology, or a documented medical-necessity prerequisite rather than routine dental care — a fractured jaw, a biopsy of a suspicious lesion, an extraction required before radiation therapy. In some coordination-of-benefits scenarios both claims are legitimate for the same visit: a trauma-related extraction billed to medical for the surgical work, with a separate dental claim for any unrelated routine care performed the same day. What's never legitimate is billing the identical service to both payers as if each were the primary claim — that reads as duplicate billing regardless of intent.

Why did our comprehensive oral evaluation get downcoded to a limited exam?

Most commonly because D0150 (comprehensive) is benefit-limited to once per provider or once per lifetime per payer, and the claim was billed for a patient who already had one on file with that payer — even from a different office years earlier. The 2026 D0180 descriptor revision adds a second trap: comprehensive periodontal evaluation now has to be tied explicitly to documented signs, symptoms, systemic conditions, or social risk factors, and probing depths have to accompany the claim. A chart that just says "comprehensive exam performed" without periodontal charting or a stated clinical reason downcodes to a limited evaluation almost automatically.

Do extractions bundle with anesthesia and same-day exams on medical crossover claims?

This is the highest-dollar-risk area in dental-medical crossover and the one competitor guides skip entirely. An E/M or oral evaluation billed the same day as a surgical extraction needs modifier 25 to be separately payable, and only when the documentation shows a significant, separately identifiable service beyond the pre-procedure workup. Anesthesia and sedation codes billed alongside the surgical CPT code are a common NCCI Procedure-to-Procedure edit pair. The specific modifier-indicator values for these pairs change quarterly and have to be looked up per pair in CMS's NCCI PTP Edits Lookup Tool — don't assume a pairing is bypassable because it was last quarter.

What's the difference between predetermination and prior authorization in dental billing?

Predetermination is a voluntary, non-binding estimate of what a dental plan will pay for a proposed treatment plan, submitted before treatment starts; prior authorization is a payer's binding approval requirement that must be obtained before certain procedures are performed, most often on the medical side of a crossover claim. Implants, comprehensive orthodontic treatment, surgical extractions, and crown and bridge work almost always warrant predetermination given how common elective-exclusion and missing-tooth clauses are in dental plans, even though it isn't contractually required the way medical prior auth is.
