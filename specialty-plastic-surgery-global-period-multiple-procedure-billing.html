Our complete plastic surgery guide

Global periods and multiple procedure billing for plastic surgery reconstruction.

A multi-stage breast reconstruction can run three or four operative sessions across a year, and every one of them lands inside, or right up against, the global period of the one before it. Getting paid for all of them — without an audit flag for unbundling or a denial for a service the payer thinks is already included — comes down to four modifiers and one payment rule. This guide covers global period assignments for the common reconstructive procedures, modifier 58 for planned staged surgery, modifier 78 for an unplanned return to the OR, modifier 79 for genuinely unrelated procedures, and the Multiple Procedure Payment Reduction that applies whenever two billable surgeries happen in the same session.

Reviewed by Phares A., MD · Physiotherapist, CPC (AAPC) · Part of our plastic surgery billing and coding guide

Key takeaways

  • Most reconstructive breast and flap procedures carry a 90-day global period, not 10 or 0 — that includes tissue expander placement (19357), implant exchange (11970), and the flap codes (19361, 19364, 19367–19369). Every follow-up visit inside that window is bundled unless a modifier says otherwise.
  • Modifier 58 requires the plan to be on paper before the second surgery happens. The original operative note has to state the staged approach — expander now, implant exchange later — or the second claim is a weaker argument for separate payment.
  • Modifier 78 pays less on purpose. An unplanned return to the OR for a complication of the original surgery is reimbursed at the intraoperative-services percentage on the fee schedule, not the full allowed amount, and it does not restart the global clock.
  • Two surgical codes in the same session almost always trigger the 50% multiple-procedure reduction on the lower-valued one — a symmetry procedure billed alongside a reconstruction stage is the most common place this gets missed or mis-sequenced.

Global periods for reconstructive procedures: what's bundled, what isn't

The global surgical package is a single bundled payment covering the procedure itself plus a defined window of related pre- and postoperative care. CMS assigns every CPT code one of three global period lengths — 000 days (minor procedure, no meaningful post-op period), 010 days (minor procedure, short post-op period), or 090 days (major procedure) — and that assignment, not the operative report, decides what's already been paid for. Reconstructive plastic surgery procedures are almost uniformly 090-day codes, which means the day of surgery, one day of preoperative care, and 90 days of postoperative care that follows are all included in the single payment for the procedure.

What's included in that 90-day bundle is routine, expected post-op care tied to the reconstruction itself: incision checks, suture or drain removal, uncomplicated wound management, and — specific to 19357 — every in-office saline expansion visit that happens before the global period closes. That last point trips up practices that bill each fill session as its own E/M visit; if the fill happens inside the 90-day window that 19357 opened, it's bundled, and billing it separately is unbundling, not an oversight a payer will wave off.

What's not included is anything genuinely unrelated to the reconstruction (modifier 24 territory for an E/M visit, modifier 79 for a procedure), anything that was planned as a separate stage from the start (modifier 58), and any complication serious enough to require a return to the operating or procedure room (modifier 78). The rest of this guide is about drawing that line correctly and documenting it so the claim survives review.

Modifier 58: billing a planned, staged breast reconstruction

Modifier 58 exists for exactly the situation that defines most implant-based breast reconstruction: a sequence of procedures planned from the outset, where each stage builds on the last. CMS describes three situations modifier 58 covers — a procedure planned prospectively at the time of the original surgery, a procedure that is more extensive than the original, and therapy following a diagnostic surgical procedure. The expander-to-implant sequence fits the first category directly: the surgeon places the tissue expander with the documented intent to exchange it for a permanent implant once expansion is complete.

The documentation standard is specific and it matters: the original operative note — the one for the tissue expander placement (19357) — should state that a staged approach is planned. A note that says only "tissue expander placed" and is silent on what comes next leaves the second claim to argue its case from standard-of-care assumptions rather than the patient's own record. A note that says "expander placed with planned exchange for permanent implant following completion of tissue expansion" gives the coder, and the payer's reviewer, exactly what modifier 58 is supposed to point to.

  • 1State the staged intent in the first operative note, not just in a later chart note or the patient's after-visit summary. The strongest documentation trail starts at the first surgery, not the second.
  • 2Append modifier 58 to the second procedure's code — the implant exchange (11970), not the original expander placement. The modifier marks the staged procedure as it's billed, which is the later one.
  • 3Expect a new global period to open. Modifier 58 procedures start their own 90-day postoperative window on the date of that surgery — unlike modifier 78, this is a fresh global period, not a continuation of the first.
  • 4Diagnosis coding should reflect the reconstruction stage, not just the original mastectomy diagnosis carried forward unchanged — a personal history code (Z90.1-) alongside the encounter reason keeps the clinical picture accurate at each stage.

This same logic covers other planned sequences in reconstructive plastic surgery: a delayed nipple-areola reconstruction following a flap or implant procedure, a planned revision of a reconstructed breast, or a second-stage contour revision after a free flap. In every case the test is the same — was this stage's necessity established at the time of the first surgery, or did it come up later because of how the patient healed? The first is modifier 58. The second is modifier 78.

Modifier 78: the unplanned return to the operating room

Modifier 78 covers the opposite scenario — a return to the operating or procedure room that nobody planned, because a complication of the original surgery made it necessary. A hematoma requiring evacuation, a surgical site infection requiring washout and debridement, or a flap showing signs of vascular compromise requiring urgent take-back are the textbook cases in reconstructive plastic surgery. The defining feature of modifier 78 is that the second procedure is related to the first — it's treating a problem the first surgery caused or left behind — but it wasn't part of the original plan.

Payment for a modifier 78 procedure is deliberately lower than a full, unrelated surgery would be, and it's lower for a specific reason: the payer isn't paying for a fresh pre-operative workup or a new 90-day package of follow-up care, because the patient is already inside the original procedure's global period and that follow-up care is still owed under the first payment. What modifier 78 buys is reimbursement for the intraoperative work itself — the percentage of the fee schedule allowance assigned to the intraoperative-services portion of that specific CPT code in the Medicare Physician Fee Schedule Relative Value File. That percentage is commonly reported in the 70–80% range in payer reimbursement policies, but it is set per code, not as a flat industry-wide number, so the exact figure has to be pulled from the RVU file for the code actually being billed rather than assumed.

Modifier 78 fits when…
  • The return to the OR is treating a complication of the original procedure — hematoma, infection, wound dehiscence, vascular compromise — and nothing about it was documented as planned in advance.
  • The original procedure's global period is still open, and no new global period should start from this encounter.
Modifier 78 doesn't fit when…
  • The second surgery was documented as part of the original plan — that's modifier 58, and it pays the full allowed amount with its own new global period.
  • The second surgery addresses a problem with no clinical connection to the first — that's modifier 79, also paid in full with its own global period.

Because modifier 78 does not open a new global period, the clock from the original 90-day window keeps running underneath it. A hematoma evacuation on postoperative day 6 of a 90-day global period doesn't reset the count — the original procedure's global period still closes on its original schedule, and any further related care in that window remains bundled into the original payment unless it independently qualifies for another modifier.

Modifier 79 and the Multiple Procedure Payment Reduction

Modifier 79 is the cleanest of the three in concept: it marks a procedure performed by the same physician during another procedure's global period that has no clinical relationship to the original surgery at all. A patient recovering from a DIEP flap breast reconstruction who separately needs an unrelated procedure — say, excision of an unrelated skin lesion diagnosed on a different visit — would have that second procedure billed with modifier 79. Because the two are unrelated, the second procedure is paid at the full allowed amount and starts its own new global period, exactly as if the original surgery's global period didn't exist.

Where reconstructive plastic surgery billing gets more consistently complicated is when two related, billable surgical procedures happen in the same operative session — most commonly, a reconstruction stage combined with a contralateral symmetry procedure such as a mastopexy or reduction performed at the same sitting to match the reconstructed side. That scenario isn't governed by modifiers 58, 78, or 79 at all; it's governed by the standard Medicare multiple-surgery payment rule.

Under that rule, when the same physician performs more than one surgical procedure on the same patient in the same session, the procedures are ranked by their Medicare Physician Fee Schedule allowed amount. The single highest-valued procedure is paid at 100% of its fee schedule amount. Every additional procedure subject to the rule is paid at 50% of its own fee schedule amount, regardless of the order the codes appear on the claim. Modifier 51 is the standard way to flag the lesser procedure(s) for this ranking, though many payers apply the reduction automatically from the RVU data on the claim rather than relying on the modifier alone.

Two details matter for getting this right on a reconstruction claim. First, ranking is by fee schedule dollar value, not by which procedure the surgeon or the patient considers primary — a lower-cost symmetry procedure never gets bumped up to 100% just because it's billed first on the claim. Second, the multiple-surgery reduction and the global-period modifiers are separate systems that can both apply to the same claim: a modifier 58 staged procedure billed alongside an unrelated same-session surgery still goes through fee schedule ranking for the multiple-procedure reduction, on top of whatever global-period modifier applies to each code individually.

Pro tip

Before submitting a claim with two surgical codes from the same session, pull the current-year non-facility or facility RVU total for each code and rank them yourself rather than trusting code order on the operative report. Practice management systems that auto-sequence by RVU catch this correctly; ones that bill in the order the codes were dictated sometimes don't, and a claim that ranks the lower-valued procedure as primary either overpays and invites a post-payment recoupment, or underpays and leaves money on the table that a corrected claim has to chase down later.

Worked example: staged exchange, then an unplanned return for a hematoma

A patient underwent mastectomy with immediate tissue expander placement (19357). The operative note documented the plan clearly: expander now, serial in-office saline fills, then surgical exchange for a permanent implant once expansion was complete. Ten weeks later, expansion finished, the surgeon performed the implant exchange. Twelve days after the exchange, the patient returned with a rapidly enlarging, painful breast; the surgeon took her back to the OR the same day to evacuate a postoperative hematoma.

Three things make this sequence bill cleanly. First, the staged intent was on the record from the very first operative note, which is what makes modifier 58 a documented fact rather than an assertion made after the fact. Second, the hematoma evacuation is unambiguously related to the implant exchange — it's a complication of that specific surgery, occurring inside its global period — which is exactly what modifier 78 is for, not modifier 58 (nothing about a hematoma return is planned) and not modifier 79 (it isn't unrelated). Third, the hematoma return doesn't get its own new global period; the implant exchange's original 90-day window keeps running underneath it, and any further related postoperative care in that window remains part of that original package unless it independently qualifies for its own modifier.

Frequently asked questions

Can modifier 58 be used for a planned expander-to-implant exchange?

Yes, provided the original tissue-expander operative note documents the staged plan — that the expander was placed with the intent to exchange it for a permanent implant once expansion was complete. Modifier 58 tells the payer the second surgery was anticipated and related, not a new, unrelated problem, and it opens a new global period of its own starting on the exchange date. Without that prospective documentation in the first operative note, some payers treat the exchange as staged anyway based on standard surgical practice, but an explicit statement is the stronger and faster-adjudicating claim.

Does modifier 78 pay the same as a new global period?

No. Modifier 78 pays only the intraoperative portion of the fee schedule allowance for the return procedure — the percentage listed in the intraoperative-services column of the Medicare Physician Fee Schedule Relative Value File for that specific code, commonly in the neighborhood of 70–80% of the full allowed amount, though the exact figure is code-specific and must be checked per CPT code rather than assumed. It also does not start a new global period; the remainder of the original procedure's postoperative window continues to run.

How does the Multiple Procedure Payment Reduction apply when a symmetry procedure is billed the same session as a reconstruction stage?

Under the standard Medicare multiple-surgery rule, when the same physician performs more than one surgical procedure on the same patient in the same operative session, the procedure with the highest Medicare Physician Fee Schedule allowed amount is paid at 100%, and each additional procedure subject to the rule is paid at 50% of its own fee schedule amount. So if a contralateral symmetry procedure such as a mastopexy or reduction is performed at the same session as an implant exchange, whichever of the two has the higher RVU value is ranked first and paid in full, and the other is reduced to 50% — modifier 51 is typically appended to the lesser procedure(s) to trigger this ranking, though many payers apply it automatically from claim-line RVU data.

What global period applies to tissue expander placement, CPT 19357?

19357 (breast reconstruction with tissue expander, including subsequent expansion sessions) carries a 90-day global period, consistent with other major breast-reconstruction codes in the 19340–19369 range. All office-based saline fill visits that fall inside that 90-day window are bundled into the original 19357 payment and are not separately billable; fills that occur after the global period closes may be billed with the appropriate E/M code.
