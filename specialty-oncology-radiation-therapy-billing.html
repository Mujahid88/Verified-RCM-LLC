Radiation therapy billing: technical vs. professional component coding.

A course of radiation therapy generates more distinct billable steps than almost anything else in oncology — a clinical plan, a dosimetric plan, a delivery code for every fraction, an image-guidance code, and a weekly management code — and every one of them can split into a technical half and a professional half depending on who owns the linear accelerator. Then, effective January 1, 2026, CMS and the AMA rebuilt the entire delivery-code family from the ground up. This guide covers the 26/TC split by site of service, the planning codes, what actually happened to delivery coding in 2026, and the treatment-management frequency rule that generates one of the most common denials in the specialty.

Reviewed by Phares A., MD · Physiotherapist, CPC (AAPC) · Part of our oncology billing and coding guide

Key takeaways

  • Who bills what turns on ownership, not technique. A freestanding center that owns its own linear accelerator and employs its physicians bills globally. A hospital-based department splits the claim: the facility bills the technical component with modifier TC, the radiation oncologist bills the professional component with modifier 26.
  • Radiation treatment delivery coding was rebuilt on January 1, 2026. CPT 77385 and 77386 (IMRT delivery) and CPT 77014 were deleted, along with the seventeen Medicare G-codes (G6001-G6017) that had stood in for conventional delivery since 2015. Delivery is now three complexity tiers — 77402, 77407, 77412 — regardless of whether the technique is 2D, 3D-CRT, IMRT, or VMAT.
  • Image guidance now lives in one code, 77387, with its technical value folded into the delivery codes' payment — a hospital-based radiation oncologist bills 77387 with modifier 26 only; there's no separate technical charge to append to it anymore.
  • CPT 77427 covers five delivered fractions per unit, not one calendar week — billing it on a weekly cadence regardless of how many fractions were actually given that week is one of the most common denial triggers in radiation oncology billing.

Who bills what: the technical/professional split by site of service

Radiation therapy is one of the clearest examples in all of medical billing of a service with a genuine, separately valued technical component (the equipment, the physics support, the therapists, the facility overhead) and professional component (the physician's planning, supervision, and interpretation). Whether that split shows up on a claim at all depends entirely on who owns the equipment and who employs the treating physician — not on the diagnosis, the technique, or the payer.

How technical and professional components are billed by practice arrangement.
ArrangementWho bills the technical componentWho bills the professional component
Freestanding radiation center, physicians employed by the same entity that owns the linacSame entity — billed globally, no PC/TC modifierSame entity — billed globally, no PC/TC modifier
Hospital-based radiation oncology department, physicians independently billingHospital, modifier TCRadiation oncology group, modifier 26
Physician group contracts to treat at a hospital- or health-system-owned vaultFacility that owns the equipment, modifier TCPhysician group, modifier 26
Physician-owned equipment inside a physician-owned freestanding suite (IDTF-style)Same entity — billed globallySame entity — billed globally

The trap is the third row. A radiation oncology group that staffs a hospital's vault under a professional-services agreement doesn't suddenly get to bill globally just because its physicians are the only clinical staff on site — the hospital still owns the linear accelerator, the maintenance contract, and the therapists' facility-side employment, so the hospital bills TC and the physician group bills 26. Billing the global, unmodified code from the physician side in that arrangement submits a claim for a technical component the group didn't actually incur, and it duplicates whatever the hospital separately billed for the same date of service — a pattern payers' post-payment audit teams specifically watch for in radiation oncology because the dollar amounts per claim are large enough to make the audit worth running.

This split applies uniformly across every code family below — planning, delivery, image guidance, and treatment management can each carry their own TC/26 designation, and a hospital-based radiation oncology group typically appends modifier 26 to nearly every line on its claim, while the hospital's chargemaster carries the TC-modified equivalent for the same date of service.

Treatment planning: the clinical plan and the dosimetric plan are two different things

Radiation oncology has two planning steps that get confused constantly, including inside billing offices: clinical treatment planning (CPT 77261-77263), which is the physician's professional work of integrating the diagnosis, staging, and overall treatment intent into a plan of therapy, and the dosimetric plan (CPT 77295 for 3D-CRT, CPT 77301 for IMRT), which is the technical/physics work of actually calculating the beam arrangement, isodose distribution, and dose-volume histogram. They represent different steps in the process of care and are billed separately when both are genuinely performed.

Clinical treatment planning complexity levels (CPT 77261-77263), paraphrased from the CPT descriptor structure — confirm exact language against your CPT Professional edition.
CodeComplexityWhat typically drives this level
77261SimpleA single treatment area, encompassed in a single port or simple parallel-opposed ports, with simple or no blocking
77262IntermediateThree or more converging ports, two separate treatment areas, multiple blocks, or special time-dose constraints
77263ComplexHighly complex blocking, custom shielding, tangential ports, special wedges or compensators, three or more separate treatment areas, rotational or special beam considerations, or combined modalities

Clinical treatment planning is professional-only and billed once per course of therapy, regardless of how many fractions follow. A course treated with IMRT almost always supports 77263, since IMRT planning inherently involves multiple beam angles, dose constraints to nearby organs at risk, and (usually) more than one treatment area consideration — but the level is justified by the documented planning complexity, not by the fact that the technique is IMRT.

The dosimetric plan is a separate line: CPT 77295 (3-dimensional radiotherapy plan, including dose-volume histograms) applies to a 3D-CRT course; CPT 77301 (IMRT plan, including dose-volume histograms for target and critical-structure dose specifications) applies when the course is planned as IMRT or VMAT. Only one of the two is billed per plan — a course doesn't get both a 3D-CRT plan and an IMRT plan for the same target volume. CPT 77300 (basic radiation dosimetry calculation) is billed separately, as many times as the calculation is actually performed during the course — typically one to six times depending on complexity, not once per fraction.

Simulation-aided field setting: why it rarely shows up as its own line anymore

Simulation used to be billed as its own step under CPT 77280 (simple), 77285 (intermediate), and 77290 (complex) — imaging and marking the treatment fields before the beam data is finalized. Those codes still exist in CPT, but for the majority of modern courses they never appear on the claim as a separate line, because CMS treats them as inclusive to CPT 77301 (IMRT plan) whenever simulation is performed as part of developing that IMRT plan — whether it happens on the same date of service or a different one. Local coverage guidance from multiple Medicare Administrative Contractors applies this bundling within a defined window around the IMRT plan date (commonly 30 days before or after) for the same group and the same patient (Billing and Coding Guidelines for Radiation Oncology Including IMRT, L34652, and consistent with payer-published bundling notices such as Blue Cross NC's July 2021 radiation oncology bundling update).

The practical effect: on an IMRT course, simulation work is baked into the value of 77301, not billed separately, and a billing office that tries to add 77285 or 77290 alongside 77301 for the same planning episode is submitting a line that's going to deny as bundled far more often than it pays. Simulation codes remain separately billable mainly on conventional 3D-CRT courses where no IMRT plan is being developed — and even then, check the applicable LCD, since coverage and bundling policy is MAC-specific.

How radiation treatment delivery was rebuilt for 2026

This is the change that affects every radiation oncology claim written in 2026, and it's worth understanding as a redesign rather than a routine annual code update. Through 2025, delivery coding ran on two parallel tracks: IMRT delivery under CPT 77385 (simple) and 77386 (complex), and conventional (3D-CRT, 2D, electron) delivery under a set of Medicare-specific G-codes (G6001-G6017) that CMS had issued back in 2015 specifically because Medicare declined to adopt that year's CPT restructuring of 77401/77402/77407/77412. Image guidance during delivery was billed separately as CPT 77387, a companion add-on code.

Effective January 1, 2026, the AMA CPT Editorial Panel collapsed all of that into a single, technique-neutral, three-tier structure. CPT 77385, 77386, and 77014 were deleted outright, and the entire G6001-G6017 series was retired along with them.

Radiation treatment delivery codes, before and after the January 1, 2026 CPT restructuring.
Through 2025Effective 2026What decides the tier now
G6003-G6015 (conventional, simple, by MeV) / 77402 (rarely used by Medicare)77402 — Level 1Simple 2D or electron beam delivery, including a single en face electron field or multiple non-abutting electron fields
77385 (IMRT, simple) / mid-tier G-codes (intermediate conventional)77407 — Level 23D-CRT or IMRT/VMAT delivered to a single isocenter, no active motion management
77386 (IMRT, complex) / highest-tier G-codes (complex conventional)77412 — Level 3Multiple isocenters (any technique), active motion management (e.g., gating, tracking), or mixed photon-electron/total skin electron therapy — any one criterion alone qualifies
77401 (superficial/orthovoltage)77436-77439 (new Surface Radiation Therapy section)Superficial and orthovoltage delivery moved to its own code family, used mainly for non-melanoma skin cancer
77387 (IGRT, companion code, billed with modifier as needed)77387 — redefinedNow the professional component of all forms of image guidance (not including motion management); the technical value is folded into the payment for 77402/77407/77412

Two consequences follow directly from this table. First, the delivery tier no longer tracks the technique name a practice is used to charging by — a VMAT plan delivered to a single isocenter with no gating is a 77407, the same tier as a comparably simple 3D-CRT plan, even though for a decade those were billed under entirely different code families with different values. Second, 77387 changed shape: before 2026 it was billed as its own line with whatever modifier matched the setting; from 2026 forward, the code represents professional work only, so a hospital-based radiation oncologist appends modifier 26 to 77387 and the hospital does not separately bill a technical component for image guidance at all — that value now sits inside the practice-expense payment for the delivery code itself.

⚠️ The scale of the reimbursement impact is still being litigated in the trade press rather than settled in a single CMS document: ASTRO's own member survey (reported via ASTROnews and covered by outlets including AJMC and Oncology News Central through 2026) found a majority of responding physicians reporting double-digit percentage cuts, and industry reporting describes payers being slow to reprice commercial contracts to the new three-tier structure, which some sources describe as producing higher denial and downcode rates on Level 3 (77412) claims specifically. Those figures come from survey and trade-press reporting, not a CMS claims database this build queried directly — treat the direction (higher scrutiny on 77412) as well corroborated and the specific percentages as reported-but-not-independently-verified.

Special procedures, image-guided add-ons, and the treatment-management clock

Two code families round out a typical course: special treatment procedures for cases that don't fit routine external-beam delivery, and the weekly management code that pays the radiation oncologist for overseeing the course as it runs.

Special radiation treatment procedure codes.
CodeWhat it covers
77469Management of the complete course of intraoperative radiation treatment (IORT) — radiation delivered directly to an exposed tumor bed during surgery. Covers only the IORT management itself, not any separate E/M furnished outside that session.
77470Special treatment procedure — additional physician work and management effort for situations such as hyperfractionation, total body irradiation, per-oral or endocavitary treatment, intraoperative cone use, or courses combining external beam with brachytherapy or stereotactic radiosurgery under a single management plan.

⚠️ This build's search of current CPT-restructuring coverage did not surface any indication that 77469 or 77470 were part of the January 2026 delivery-code overhaul — the changes described above concentrated on 77385/77386/77401/77402/77407/77412/77014/77387 and the G6001-G6017 series. Current secondary sources (AAPC/Codify code pages) describe 77469 and 77470 with the same scope outlined here, but this wasn't independently confirmed against a CMS-published 2026 CPT crosswalk; verify current status and valuation for these two codes in your CPT Professional edition and current Physician Fee Schedule before billing.

Treatment management is where most of the physician's ongoing billing during a course actually lives. CPT 77427 (radiation treatment management) is reported once per five delivered fractions, regardless of how many calendar days or weeks that takes, and requires at least one face-to-face visit with the radiation oncologist within that block of five. A final week landing on three or four leftover fractions still supports one additional unit of 77427; a course that ends with only one or two leftover fractions generally doesn't support a separate management unit for that fragment by itself. A short course consisting of only one or two fractions in total is a different code, 77431, not a fractional unit of 77427.

Worked example: a 25-fraction IMRT course with weekly management

A prostate IMRT course, planned to a single isocenter with no active motion management, delivered daily with image guidance, billed by a hospital-based radiation oncology group (technical component billed separately by the hospital):

Code sequence for a 25-fraction IMRT course, professional-component billing shown.
StepCodeFrequency
Clinical treatment planning (complex)77263-26Once, at course start
IMRT dosimetric plan77301-26Once (simulation-aided field setting bundled in)
Basic dosimetry calculation77300-26As performed, typically 1-6 times across the course
Treatment delivery, single isocenter, no motion management77407-26Once per fraction × 25

If the same course had used respiratory gating or a fiducial-tracking system for the isocenter, delivery would move to 77412 instead of 77407 for every fraction affected, because active motion management alone is sufficient to qualify for the top tier under the 2026 structure — it doesn't need multiple isocenters as well.

Bills clean
  • Physician group appends modifier 26 to every professional line (planning, delivery, guidance, management) when treating at equipment it doesn't own; the facility separately bills the same codes with modifier TC.
  • 77427 is tallied against delivered fractions in blocks of five, with a documented face-to-face visit inside each block, not against calendar weeks.
Denies or gets recouped
  • Physician group bills the global (unmodified) delivery and guidance codes when the linac and technical staff belong to the hospital — this is billing for a technical component that was never incurred, and it duplicates the hospital's own claim for the same date of service.
  • 77427 billed once per calendar week regardless of fraction count, or a 2026 claim still carrying 77385, 77386, or a G6001-G6017 code — both patterns are now routine, predictable denials rather than edge cases.

Frequently asked questions

How did the January 2026 CPT overhaul change radiation treatment delivery billing?

Effective January 1, 2026, the AMA CPT Editorial Panel collapsed the old technology-specific delivery codes into three complexity-based tiers reported regardless of technique or site of service: 77402 (simple 2D or electron delivery), 77407 (3D-CRT or IMRT/VMAT to a single isocenter, no motion management), and 77412 (multiple isocenters, active motion management, or mixed photon-electron/total skin electron therapy). CPT 77385 and 77386, the prior IMRT-specific delivery codes, were deleted, as was CPT 77014 and the block of Medicare G-codes (G6001-G6017) that had stood in for conventional delivery since 2015. Superficial and orthovoltage delivery moved out of this family entirely into a new Surface Radiation Therapy section (77436-77439).

Who bills the technical component and who bills the professional component for radiation therapy?

It depends on who owns the equipment and employs the treating radiation oncologist, not on the diagnosis or the technique. A freestanding center that owns its linear accelerator and employs its physicians bills globally, with no PC/TC modifier, because one entity performed both halves of the service. A hospital-based department typically owns the equipment and bills the technical component with modifier TC for the facility, equipment, and staff, while the radiation oncologist or their group separately bills the professional component with modifier 26 for the physician's planning, supervision, and interpretation work. The same TC/26 split applies whenever a physician group treats at a site it doesn't own or staff with its own technical personnel, including many physician-owned equipment arrangements housed inside a hospital-contracted space.

How many treatment sessions does one unit of radiation treatment management (77427) cover?

One unit of CPT 77427 covers five delivered fractions, reported once per five treatment sessions regardless of how many calendar days or weeks it took to deliver them, and it requires at least one face-to-face visit with the radiation oncologist within that five-fraction block. A final week that includes three or four fractions still supports one additional unit of 77427; a course that ends with only one or two leftover fractions generally does not support a separate management unit for that fragment on its own. Billing one unit of 77427 for every calendar week regardless of how many fractions were actually delivered that week is a common way this code gets denied or recouped.

Can CPT 77385 and 77386 still be billed for a 2026 date of service?

No. Both codes were deleted from CPT effective January 1, 2026, along with CPT 77014 and the HCPCS G6001-G6017 series that Medicare had used for conventional delivery since 2015. Any claim line for a 2026 date of service still using 77385 or 77386 will not process; the equivalent service now falls under 77407 (single isocenter, no motion management) or 77412 (multiple isocenters or active motion management), depending on how the treatment was actually delivered. Practices and billing systems that hard-coded the old IMRT delivery codes into charge masters or superbills need those updated before submitting 2026 claims.
