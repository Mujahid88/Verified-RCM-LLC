Plastic and reconstructive surgery billing and coding: the complete 2026 guide.

One question decides almost every dollar in this specialty before a single code is chosen: is this cosmetic, or is it reconstructive? Get that call right and document it, and a payer that covers mastectomies has no legal room to deny the reconstruction that follows, the Women's Health and Cancer Rights Act made that federal law in 1998. Get it wrong, or fail to say the mandate's name out loud in the claim, and a covered, medically necessary procedure gets bucketed with tummy tucks and eyelid lifts and denied as elective. This guide covers the full revenue cycle: the medical-necessity standard that separates the two categories, the exact staged coding for WHCRA-mandated breast reconstruction, the functional-versus-cosmetic line for blepharoplasty and panniculectomy, and the modifiers, bundling edits, and appeal arguments that decide whether any of it gets paid.

Key takeaways

  • WHCRA is a federal mandate, not a payer courtesy. Any group or individual health plan that covers mastectomies must cover every stage of reconstruction, contralateral symmetry surgery, prostheses, and complications including lymphedema, with no time limit on when the patient elects it. Most practices under-bill this because they treat any breast procedure as potentially cosmetic instead of citing the mandate by name.
  • Implant-based reconstruction is a two-code, two-stage build. CPT 19357 places the tissue expander and already includes every subsequent expansion visit inside its global period; CPT 11970 later swaps it for the permanent implant. Billing expansion visits separately, or billing 11970 without the staging modifier, are two of the most common self-inflicted denials in this pillar.
  • Cosmetic-vs-reconstructive is decided by documentation, not by anatomy. The same incision on the same eyelid or the same abdomen is either a covered reconstructive procedure or a self-pay cosmetic one depending entirely on whether the chart proves functional impairment, a congenital defect, trauma, or disease, not on which CPT code looks closest.
  • Panniculectomy and abdominoplasty are billed on two different codes for a reason. CPT 15830 (panniculectomy) can be reconstructive and covered; add-on code 15847 (fascial plication, umbilical transposition, the abdominoplasty component) is cosmetic contouring almost everywhere. Blurring the two on one operative note is a fast path to a full denial instead of a partial payment.

Cosmetic vs. reconstructive: the medical necessity standard everything else builds on

Every payer policy in this specialty, from Medicare's Local Coverage Articles to every major commercial carrier's medical policy bulletin, draws the same line, using close to the same language: reconstructive surgery is performed to correct or improve a body part affected by a congenital defect, developmental abnormality, trauma, infection, tumor, or disease, and it restores function or approximates a normal appearance. Cosmetic surgery is performed primarily to improve appearance in the absence of a functional impairment or one of those qualifying underlying conditions. Both categories can use the exact same CPT code, be performed by the exact same surgeon, and even be performed in the exact same operative session, the classification lives entirely in what the documentation proves, not in which procedure was done.

This distinction is the reason plastic surgery denies differently than almost any other specialty. A cardiology claim denies because a diagnosis wasn't specific enough for an LCD's covered-conditions list. A plastic surgery claim denies because the payer's adjudication system defaults to "cosmetic" the moment it sees a code from the integumentary or breast-repair sections without an unambiguous functional or disease-based qualifier attached, and once a claim is coded as cosmetic, most plans exclude it from coverage entirely rather than merely questioning medical necessity, which is a materially worse denial to fight.

Four qualifying bases recur across nearly every payer's reconstructive-surgery policy, including the Novitas Solutions Local Coverage Determination "Cosmetic and Reconstructive Surgery" (LCD L35090) and its companion billing article (Article A56587, Novitas Solutions):

  • 1Functional impairment. The condition measurably interferes with a bodily function, vision obstructed by an eyelid, skin breakdown from an overhanging pannus, breathing restricted by a nasal deformity. This has to be objectively documented (visual field testing, photographs, a record of failed conservative treatment), not simply asserted in a progress note.
  • 2Congenital or developmental defect. A structure that never developed normally, congenital absence or malformation of the breast, a cleft deformity, a vascular malformation, qualifies as reconstructive regardless of whether it also happens to be aesthetically significant to the patient.
  • 3Trauma. Surgery restoring a structure altered by an accidental injury, a burn, or an animal bite is reconstructive by definition, provided the operative note links the current procedure back to the traumatic event and, where relevant, any prior repair.
  • 4Disease, including its treatment. This is the basis that carries breast reconstruction: the disease is cancer, but more precisely, the qualifying event for WHCRA purposes is the covered mastectomy performed to treat or prevent it. Surgery correcting a scar, deformity, or asymmetry left behind by cancer treatment, an infection, or a prior surgery falls under this basis as well.

The practical failure mode we see constantly: the clinical picture genuinely supports one of these four bases, but the operative note and history and physical read like a cosmetic consultation, patient-reported dissatisfaction with appearance, no measurement, no functional complaint, no reference to the underlying disease or defect. Payers do not infer medical necessity from a good outcome; they read the chart that existed before the surgery was authorized. Every procedure-specific section below exists because "reconstructive" is a documentation standard with its own specific proof requirements, not a single universal rule, and the standard is different for breast reconstruction, blepharoplasty, and panniculectomy.

Medicare's own general exclusion language states this test almost word for word, and it is worth quoting because commercial payer policies overwhelmingly track the same structure: cosmetic surgery, defined as any surgical procedure directed at improving appearance, is excluded from coverage except when required for the prompt repair of an accidental injury or for the improvement of the functioning of a malformed body member; the exclusion specifically does not reach surgery in connection with treating severe burns, repairing the face following a serious accident, or a procedure performed for therapeutic purposes that coincidentally also serves a cosmetic purpose (Medicare Benefit Policy Manual, Chapter 16, "General Exclusions From Coverage"). The Manual's own examples map directly onto this guide's four qualifying bases, trauma, functional impairment, and disease all appear explicitly, and a congenital "malformed body member" fills out the fourth. Reconstructive procedures incidental to or following surgery for accidental injury, infection, or other disease of the affected body part, performed to correct a functional deficit, are treated as medically appropriate under this same framework rather than as a separate carve-out.

The same fact pattern recurs across nearly every other procedure family in general plastic surgery, which is worth seeing in miniature before this guide narrows to its three main procedure groups:

What the chart actually needs to say

Coders can't manufacture medical necessity from a thin note, and surgeons rarely think in terms of the specific phrases a payer's adjudication system is scanning for. Closing that gap before the claim goes out, rather than after a denial, is the highest-leverage documentation habit in this specialty. For each of the four qualifying bases, the chart should affirmatively state, in the surgeon's own clinical language, the element that maps to it:

  • 1Functional impairment: the specific function affected (vision, ambulation, hygiene, breathing), the objective measurement supporting it (visual field degrees, photographs, a documented infection history with dates and treatments tried), and a statement connecting the proposed procedure to correcting that specific impairment, not just improving appearance as a side effect.
  • 2Congenital or developmental defect: the specific anomaly named with a supporting diagnosis code, when it was first identified, and how the proposed procedure corrects the anomaly rather than alters normal anatomy.
  • 3Trauma: the date and nature of the injury, any prior related treatment or repair, and how the current procedure restores the structure affected by that specific traumatic event.
  • 4Disease or its treatment: the underlying disease and its treatment (most often the mastectomy date and laterality for breast reconstruction), explicit reference to the mandate or coverage basis that applies (WHCRA for a WHCRA-eligible plan, the payer's own reconstructive-surgery policy otherwise), and the specific stage or complication being addressed at this encounter.

A short standing macro or note template built around these four prompts, reviewed with the clinical staff who actually write the operative note and history and physical, does more to prevent cosmetic-coded denials than any amount of appeal-writing after the fact. The appeal section later in this guide exists for the claims that still get denied despite good documentation, it is not a substitute for getting the chart right the first time.

There is a compliance dimension to this discipline that cuts the other direction from everything else in this guide: cosmetic procedures billed to a payer as reconstructive is a recognized fraud pattern that payer special investigations units and government program-integrity contractors specifically watch for in this specialty, precisely because the same code can legitimately describe either category. Good documentation protects a practice two ways at once, it is what gets a genuinely reconstructive claim paid, and it is what demonstrates, if a payer or auditor later reviews the chart, that a paid claim was coded correctly in the first place. A practice culture that pushes hard on citing WHCRA and functional-necessity criteria for genuinely qualifying cases, while being equally disciplined about coding a genuinely cosmetic procedure as self-pay when the qualifying basis isn't there, is the version of this specialty's billing that holds up on both sides of an audit.

Breast reconstruction: the WHCRA mandate and its full staged coding

This is the section every other section in this pillar defers to, because it is the one place where the reconstructive-vs-cosmetic question has already been answered by federal law rather than left to a payer's medical policy. The Women's Health and Cancer Rights Act of 1998 (Public Law 105-277, Title IX) requires that any group health plan, and any individual health insurance issuer offering coverage in connection with a group health plan, that provides medical and surgical benefits for a mastectomy must also provide coverage, in a manner determined in consultation with the attending physician and the patient, for:

  • 1Reconstruction of the breast on which the mastectomy was performed, every stage of it, not a single procedure.
  • 2Surgery and reconstruction of the other breast to produce a symmetrical appearance, a reduction, lift, or augmentation on the unaffected side is part of the mandated benefit, not an optional add-on.
  • 3Prostheses, including external breast forms needed before or during reconstruction.
  • 4Treatment of physical complications of all stages of the mastectomy, including lymphedema.

Three points about scope and timing matter more for billing than they get credit for. First, WHCRA applies to anyone receiving benefits in connection with a covered mastectomy under the plan, the law is not restricted to a cancer diagnosis specifically, though cancer and its risk-reducing prophylactic mastectomies are the overwhelming majority of qualifying cases in practice. Second, there is no time limit on when reconstruction can be elected; a patient who had a mastectomy years or even decades earlier and now elects reconstruction is still covered by the mandate, and a denial citing staleness or a missed window has no basis in the statute. Third, mastectomy-related benefits under WHCRA may still be subject to the plan's usual deductibles and coinsurance, the mandate guarantees coverage, not that the service is free (U.S. Department of Labor, Employee Benefits Security Administration, WHCRA fact sheet and FAQ; CMS, WHCRA statute text, Public Law 105-277 Title IX). Self-funded plans and some plan types have historically had different applicability nuances worth confirming per plan, but the core mandate reaches the large majority of commercial and employer-sponsored coverage.

None of this means every claim pays itself. It means the burden shifts: once the qualifying mastectomy is established, the payer's basis for denying the reconstruction as "cosmetic" or "not medically necessary" collapses, and an appeal that cites WHCRA by name, references the specific mastectomy claim or date, and names the stage being billed is a fundamentally stronger position than a generic medical-necessity argument. Practices that don't push this hard enough with payers are the ones leaving contralateral symmetry work and later revision stages unbilled or under-billed, because staff default to treating any breast procedure as potentially cosmetic instead of recognizing it as a mandated benefit already owed.

Which plans the mandate actually reaches

This is the nuance that trips up practices billing a mixed payer panel: WHCRA is written to reach group health plans and health insurance issuers offering group or individual coverage in connection with a plan that already covers mastectomies, the overwhelming majority of commercial and employer-sponsored coverage. It does not apply to traditional fee-for-service Medicare or to Medicaid, which are public programs outside WHCRA's statutory reach. That does not mean Medicare patients go without reconstruction coverage: Medicare covers post-mastectomy breast reconstruction under its own medical necessity coverage policy, independent of WHCRA, and in practice the covered scope looks similar to what WHCRA requires commercially, but the legal basis, and therefore the correct citation in an appeal, is different. Appealing a traditional Medicare denial by citing WHCRA by name is citing the wrong authority; the argument for a Medicare patient rests on Medicare's own medical necessity and coverage rules for reconstructive breast surgery, not the 1998 statute. Medicaid coverage for breast reconstruction varies by state program and should be confirmed against the specific state's Medicaid policy rather than assumed. ⚠️ Whether and how WHCRA-equivalent coverage requirements reach Medicare Advantage plans specifically, as opposed to traditional fee-for-service Medicare, was not independently confirmed against a primary CMS source during this build; verify the applicable authority for an MA-plan denial before citing WHCRA in that specific appeal. Self-funded employer plans are generally still reached by WHCRA as group health plans, but historically distinct ERISA treatment for certain church plans and some governmental plans means plan-type verification is worth a few minutes before an appeal leans entirely on the statute.

WHCRA and lymphedema treatment billing

The mandate's fourth pillar, treatment of physical complications of all stages of the mastectomy, including lymphedema, is not a coding abstraction; it has its own diagnosis and treatment codes, and it is easy for a practice's billing system to lose track of the fact that this treatment is mandated coverage rather than a general rehabilitation service subject to ordinary medical-necessity review. I97.2, postmastectomy lymphedema syndrome, is the specific, confirmed-billable diagnosis (ICD-10-CM, verified via connector); I89.0, lymphedema not elsewhere classified, applies where the lymphedema is not specifically coded as post-mastectomy in the documentation. Treatment is typically built from manual lymphatic drainage and complete decongestive therapy, billed under 97140 (manual therapy techniques, billed in 15-minute increments), combined with compression bandaging and garments. CMS's own coverage article for lymphedema decongestive treatment (Article A52959, "Billing and Coding: Lymphedema Decongestive Treatment") is explicit that a supportable diagnosis of lymphedema, not tissue edema from an unrelated cause such as chronic venous insufficiency or congestive heart failure, has to be on the claim, which is exactly why using the specific I97.2 code rather than a generic edema code matters here. Compression bandaging supplies, wraps, and garments without their own unique HCPCS code are billed using one of five CMS-designated not-otherwise-specified codes (A6519, A6549, A6584, A6593, A6609), each reported on its own claim line with the appropriate units (Noridian Healthcare Solutions, DME MAC, "Lymphedema Compression Treatment Items"). None of this treatment should be treated as separately subject to a fresh cosmetic-vs-reconstructive medical necessity argument once the post-mastectomy lymphedema diagnosis is established, WHCRA already settled that question; the remaining billing task is coding the specific therapy and supplies correctly.

Immediate vs. delayed reconstruction, and when a staging modifier actually applies

CPT 19357's own descriptor covers both "immediate" reconstruction, the tissue expander placed in the same operative session as the mastectomy, and "delayed" reconstruction, performed at a separate, later session, sometimes years after the original surgery. Both are WHCRA-covered; there is no time limit, as covered above. Where confusion creeps in is the global-period interaction, and the fix is a rule that's easy to state but frequently applied backwards: the 58/78/79 staging modifiers govern the relationship between two procedures performed by the same physician (or another physician of the same specialty in the same group), they do not track the patient's overall episode of care across different surgeons or practices.

In practice, that means immediate reconstruction performed at the same operative session as the mastectomy, where a breast surgeon performs the mastectomy and a different plastic surgeon from a different practice places the tissue expander, requires no staging modifier at all on the plastic surgeon's claim for 19357, that surgeon has no prior related global period running, since the mastectomy's global period belongs to the breast surgeon who performed it, not to the reconstructive surgeon. The plastic surgeon's own 90-day global period begins fresh from the date of the expander placement. The staging modifiers become relevant later, when that same plastic surgeon (or a colleague in the same group and specialty) performs the exchange to a permanent implant, the nipple-areola reconstruction, or a revision, each of those is genuinely inside a global period that surgeon or group already has open, which is exactly the scenario modifier 58 exists for. Applying 58 to the very first reconstructive procedure a plastic surgeon performs, on the theory that it is "staged" relative to the mastectomy itself, is a common and avoidable error, since there is no related global period yet for it to relate to.

Implant-based reconstruction: the two-stage build

The most common reconstructive pathway is staged and implant-based, and it is built from two distinct CPT codes performed at two distinct operative sessions, weeks to months apart.

When 11970 is performed after 19357's global period has closed but is still clinically staged as the planned continuation of the same reconstruction, append modifier 58 to signal that relationship rather than letting the claim process as an unrelated, freestanding procedure.

Autologous (the patient's own tissue) reconstruction uses a different code family entirely, split by whether the flap is free (fully detached and reattached with microvascular anastomosis) or pedicled (left attached to its original blood supply and tunneled into place).

Bilateral autologous reconstruction is common enough to name specifically: each of the flap codes above describes reconstruction of a single breast, so bilateral DIEP or free-flap reconstruction performed in the same session is reported either as two units of the same code with modifiers identifying each side (commonly RT/LT, sometimes paired with 50 depending on the payer's bilateral-billing convention) or, less commonly, as a single line with modifier 50, the correct convention is payer-specific in exactly the same way described for the contralateral-symmetry codes below, and the wrong convention risks the same MUE unit-cap denial covered in the NCCI section. Bilateral autologous reconstruction using two separate donor sites (for example, a unilateral DIEP paired with a contralateral latissimus flap for a patient without adequate abdominal donor tissue on one side) is reported using the specific code for each flap type performed, not the same code twice, since the two procedures are not identical services.

Nipple-areolar reconstruction and contralateral symmetry

Two categories finish out a reconstruction and are exactly where WHCRA's mandate gets under-billed most often.

Nipple-areola reconstruction (CPT 19350) covers formation of the nipple mound by flap technique and creation of the areola, commonly by skin graft or tattoo, and is billed once the reconstructed breast mound has healed and settled. A CPT parenthetical excludes reporting several component flap and graft codes (including 11920–11922, 14001, 15100, 15200, and 15201) separately alongside 19350, because those components are already bundled into the global code (American Society of Plastic Surgeons CPT reference materials; AAPC Codify).

Contralateral symmetry procedures, a reduction mammaplasty (CPT 19318), mastopexy (CPT 19316), or augmentation mammaplasty (CPT 19325) performed on the unaffected breast to match the reconstructed side, are the WHCRA benefit practices most consistently under-bill or under-code as cosmetic. When performed to achieve symmetry with a reconstruction following a covered mastectomy, these are reconstructive procedures under the mandate, not elective cosmetic surgery, and Medicare's own Novitas LCD L35090 explicitly states that reducing the size of a normal breast to bring it into symmetry with a breast reconstructed after cancer surgery is a reconstructive procedure considered reasonable and necessary (LCD L35090, Novitas Solutions, "Cosmetic and Reconstructive Surgery"). ICD-10 code N65.1, "Disproportion of reconstructed breast," is validated as billable (ICD-10-CM, verified via connector) and is the diagnosis code built specifically for this scenario, use it, and document the link to the contralateral reconstruction explicitly in the note, rather than coding only a size or ptosis complaint that reads identically to a cosmetic consult.

Prophylactic (risk-reducing) mastectomy and reconstruction

WHCRA's language covers anyone "receiving benefits in connection with a mastectomy" under a plan that covers mastectomies, it does not require that the mastectomy be a cancer treatment. This matters directly for the growing volume of risk-reducing surgery performed on patients with a strong family history or a confirmed pathogenic variant (BRCA1/BRCA2 and related genes): a prophylactic mastectomy performed to reduce future cancer risk, followed by reconstruction, is still a mastectomy for WHCRA purposes when the plan covers it, and the reconstruction that follows is still mandated coverage under the same statute. Two ICD-10-CM codes anchor this scenario and are both confirmed billable: Z40.01, encounter for prophylactic removal of breast, documents the risk-reducing procedure itself, and Z42.1, encounter for breast reconstruction following mastectomy, documents the reconstructive encounter regardless of whether the originating mastectomy was therapeutic or risk-reducing (ICD-10-CM, verified via connector). Practices that only ever use Z90.1x post-mastectomy status codes for the therapeutic-cancer population, and default to coding a risk-reducing reconstruction as if it might be elective, are under-billing the same mandate covered above for a different qualifying population. ⚠️ Whether a specific plan's underlying mastectomy benefit itself extends to a prophylactic procedure absent a cancer diagnosis is a separate, plan-specific coverage question that sits upstream of WHCRA (WHCRA governs the reconstruction that follows a covered mastectomy; it does not independently create mastectomy coverage), confirm the plan's own prophylactic mastectomy coverage terms during eligibility verification rather than assuming WHCRA alone resolves it.

When no CPT code fits: the unlisted-procedure fallback

Reconstructive technique in this specialty moves faster than the code set does, and a genuinely novel flap design, a combination technique, or a revision that doesn't match any existing descriptor is not a reason to force-fit the closest listed code, it's a reason to use 19499, unlisted procedure on the breast. Unlisted codes are "special report" codes by design: they carry no assigned relative value and no fee schedule amount, and payment is determined case-by-case from the documentation submitted with the claim. That documentation needs to function as a stand-alone justification, since there is no descriptor doing any of the explanatory work for you: a detailed operative report describing the procedure performed, why it was performed, the technique and equipment used, the time and effort involved relative to a typical case, an explanation of why no existing code describes it, and one or more comparable existing codes offered as a reference point for value. A claim for 19499 submitted without that comparable-code framing is asking the payer to guess at a fee basis, and it is far more likely to be denied or drastically underpaid than one that does the framing itself. The same principle extends to other integumentary and reconstructive unlisted codes (for example, 17999 for an unlisted skin procedure) when a technique used in panniculectomy or scar revision similarly doesn't map to an existing code, the documentation standard is identical.

Blepharoplasty: the functional-versus-cosmetic line

Upper eyelid surgery is the cleanest illustration of this pillar's central thesis, because the exact same incision is either a covered functional procedure or a self-pay cosmetic one depending entirely on measured, photographed, objective proof, and payers publish the specific proof they want.

Medicare contractors maintain dedicated blepharoplasty coverage articles: CMS Article A57618, "Billing and Coding: Blepharoplasty, Blepharoptosis Repair and Surgical Procedures of the Brow," and CGS Administrators' LCD L33944, "Blepharoplasty," both state the same underlying principle, upper eyelid surgery is reconstructive when it restores functional vision or visual field, and cosmetic when it is performed to improve appearance in the absence of that functional deficit. Aetna's Clinical Policy Bulletin 0084, "Eyelid Surgery," is the most granular published set of numeric thresholds we found and is worth citing directly because of how specific it is: Aetna requires visual field testing performed within the past 12 months, with and without the eyelid or brow taped, showing a superior visual field of 30 degrees or less before taping, and either an increase of 12 degrees or more, or a 30% or greater relative increase, in the superior visual field after taping. Aetna also requires external photographs taken within the past 12 months, with the patient looking straight ahead, demonstrating the eyelid margin at or below the pupil (Aetna Clinical Policy Bulletin 0084, "Eyelid Surgery"). Cigna's published Medical Coverage Policy 0045 uses a different framing of the same underlying test: it covers upper-lid blepharoplasty when visual field testing shows at least 20 degrees, or a 30%, loss in the superior visual field that is corrected when the lid margin is elevated by taping, and notes that the normal superior visual field runs roughly 55–60 degrees at the 90-degree meridian, so a 20% impairment is characterized as mild and a 64% impairment as severe (Cigna Medical Coverage Policy 0045, "Blepharoplasty," accessed 2026). Aetna's and Cigna's numeric thresholds are not identical, and neither is a substitute for the other, confirm the specific payer's current published criteria before submitting.

Panniculectomy vs. abdominoplasty: two codes, two coverage outcomes

This pairing sits right next to blepharoplasty as a functional-versus-cosmetic case study, but with its own documentation standard built around chronic skin disease and weight stability rather than visual fields.

Panniculectomy (CPT 15830), "excision of excessive skin and subcutaneous tissue, abdomen, infraumbilical panniculectomy," removes the overhanging pannus itself and does not include muscle tightening, umbilical repositioning, or flap elevation. It is potentially reconstructive and covered when the pannus is causing a documented, recurring functional problem, not simply present. Novitas Solutions' Article A56587 lists the ICD-10 codes it recognizes as supporting medical necessity for 15830: L98.7 (excessive and redundant skin and subcutaneous tissue) or M79.3 reported as the primary diagnosis, together with a secondary diagnosis of L30.4 (intertrigo), R26.2 (difficulty walking, not elsewhere classified), or Z74.09 (other reduced mobility) (Article A56587, Novitas Solutions, "Billing and Coding: Cosmetic and Reconstructive Surgery"). The pattern across payers is consistent even where the exact code list differs: the pannus has to be causing chronic intertrigo, cellulitis, or skin breakdown that has been treated medically for a period of months and has not resolved, and photographs documenting the pannus hanging at or below the pubis are standard supporting evidence across the commercial and Medicare policies we reviewed.

Abdominoplasty add-on (CPT +15847), "excision of excessive skin and subcutaneous tissue, abdomen (e.g., abdominoplasty), includes umbilical transposition and fascial plication," is an add-on code that can only be reported alongside 15830 and captures the muscle-tightening, umbilical-repositioning, and cosmetic-contouring component of the same operative session. 15847 is cosmetic almost everywhere; Medicare's own Benefit Policy Manual language on cosmetic surgery, and the near-universal commercial payer position, is that surgery performed for the sole purpose of improving appearance is not covered, regardless of how it is bundled with a covered panniculectomy on the same claim.

Site of service: office, ASC, or hospital outpatient

Where these procedures are performed changes both the coding and the reimbursement mechanics, and this specialty routinely spans all three settings. Blepharoplasty is frequently performed in an office-based surgical suite or an ambulatory surgery center; panniculectomy and autologous flap reconstruction, given their length and post-operative monitoring needs, are usually hospital outpatient or inpatient procedures. Facility-based procedures generate a separate facility claim (billed by the ASC or hospital, using the appropriate revenue codes and, for an ASC, its own payment classification) in addition to the surgeon's professional claim, the professional CPT codes discussed throughout this guide do not change based on setting, but the presence or absence of a matching facility claim is itself something payers reconcile, and a professional claim for a facility-based procedure with no corresponding facility claim on file can trigger a records request. Office-based procedures, more common for isolated blepharoplasty, don't generate a separate facility claim at all, and the professional fee is calculated differently (a non-facility rate) to reflect that the practice is absorbing the overhead a facility would otherwise bill separately. Confirm which setting a specific payer's policy expects for a given procedure, some commercial payers require prior authorization specifically for a hospital-based session that would not be required for the same procedure performed in an ASC, purely as a cost-management measure, independent of the clinical medical-necessity question this guide otherwise focuses on.

Plastic and reconstructive surgery runs almost entirely on 90-day (major surgery) global periods, and getting the global-period interaction wrong is one of the most common reasons a legitimately staged, multi-visit reconstruction gets bundled, denied, or paid at the wrong rate.

  • A procedure performed by the same physician during the global period of a prior surgery, but genuinely unrelated to it, for example, an unrelated skin lesion excision performed during a breast reconstruction's global period.

  • An unplanned return to the operating or procedure room by the same physician for a complication of the original surgery, evacuating a hematoma, managing a wound dehiscence, not a planned stage.

  • Modifiers 58, 78, and 79 are mutually exclusive on a given line, using the wrong one is a common reason a genuinely staged reconstruction claim processes as a duplicate or an unrelated denial.

Worked example: staging a full implant-based reconstruction correctly

A patient has a right mastectomy for breast cancer. The reconstructive surgeon places a tissue expander at the same operative session as the mastectomy (a different surgeon bills the mastectomy itself). Over the following months, the patient returns for three routine saline-fill visits, then six months later returns for the exchange to a permanent implant, and at the same later session the surgeon also performs a reduction mammaplasty on the left (unaffected) breast for symmetry. Coded correctly, that episode looks like this:

  • 1Initial session: 19357-58 (or without a staging modifier if this is the very first procedure in the episode and no prior related global period exists), tissue expander placement, right breast, diagnosis led by Z90.11 or the active cancer diagnosis per payer preference, opening a 90-day global period.
  • 2The three saline-fill visits: not separately billed. They are bundled into 19357's global fee; charging an E/M or a device-management code for each one is a common accidental overbilling pattern this guide flags explicitly.
  • 3Exchange session (after the expander's global period has closed): 11970-58, replacement of the tissue expander with the permanent implant, staged and related to the original reconstruction, right breast.
  • 4Same session, contralateral symmetry: 19318-59 (or the more specific modifier XS, separate structure), reduction mammaplasty, left breast, diagnosis N65.1, disproportion of reconstructed breast. The distinct-structure modifier tells the payer this is a different anatomic site in the same session, not a duplicate or a bundled component of 11970.

Two things go wrong most often in this exact sequence: billing the saline-fill visits separately (step 2), and submitting the symmetry procedure (step 4) without a distinct-procedural-service modifier and without the N65.1 diagnosis, which lets the payer's system read it as an unrelated, potentially cosmetic breast procedure performed the same day as a reconstruction, rather than the WHCRA-mandated symmetry benefit it actually is.

Co-surgeons in autologous flap reconstruction

Free-flap reconstruction (19364) is frequently performed by two surgeons working together, commonly a plastic surgeon handling the flap harvest and breast inset, and a second surgeon (plastic or a microsurgery-focused colleague) performing the microvascular anastomosis. When two surgeons of different specialties each perform a distinct, substantial portion of a single procedure reported under one CPT code, each bills the same code with modifier 62 (co-surgeons), and each surgeon's payment is based on a reduced percentage of the fee schedule amount for that code, rather than each receiving the full global fee. This is a standard CPT/CMS modifier convention, not specific to plastic surgery, but it is used often enough in free-flap reconstruction that a coder unfamiliar with it may default to billing the full code twice (which denies as a duplicate) or splitting it with 62 on only one claim (which underpays the second surgeon). Both surgeons' operative notes need to independently support the portion of the work each performed; a co-surgeon claim with only one surgeon's documentation on file is a common audit finding.

Multiple-procedure payment reduction applies whenever more than one surgical procedure is performed in the same session by the same surgeon. The standard logic, consistent across Medicare and most commercial payers: the highest-valued (highest RVU) procedure is reimbursed at 100%, and each additional procedure reported with modifier 51 is reimbursed at a reduced rate, commonly 50% of its own fee schedule amount for the second through fifth procedures. Modifier 51 does not apply to add-on codes (including 15847) or to E/M services, add-on codes are, by CPT design, already priced to reflect that they're performed in addition to a primary procedure, and appending 51 to one is a coding error that can cause the line to reject rather than simply price incorrectly. Bilateral procedures billed with modifier 50 follow separate bilateral-pricing logic, commonly 150% of the single-side fee schedule amount, rather than the multiple-procedure reduction schedule; don't stack 50 and 51 logic on the same line without confirming the specific payer's bilateral policy, since the two adjustment methods are not designed to compound by default.

Modifiers

Beyond the staging modifiers covered in the global-period section, four modifiers carry most of the remaining coding risk in this specialty.

22, increased procedural services
  • Appropriate when the work performed substantially exceeds the typical effort described by the code, dense scar tissue from prior surgery or radiation, an unusually complex flap inset, or a reconstruction complicated by prior failed attempts.
  • Requires a supporting operative note that quantifies the additional time, difficulty, or complexity, not just a statement that the case was "complex", payers ask for this documentation before adjusting payment, and most reduce or ignore the modifier without it.

The recurring, well-documented use case in this specialty is reconstruction in a previously radiated field: radiation therapy following a mastectomy measurably increases operative time and technical difficulty for a subsequent tissue expander placement or flap procedure, because irradiated tissue is less pliable, more prone to poor healing, and harder to work with than unradiated tissue. An operative note stating the field was previously radiated, describing the specific additional steps or time taken to manage that tissue, and quantifying the deviation from a typical case (for example, materially longer operative time, or a more extensive dissection than the code typically contemplates) is what supports 22 here, simply noting "patient has history of radiation" without describing its concrete effect on the operative course is not enough on its own.

50, bilateral
  • Applies when an inherently unilateral code is performed on both sides in the same session, bilateral blepharoplasty, or reduction mammaplasty performed on both breasts outside the reconstruction-symmetry context.
  • Priced separately from the multiple-procedure reduction schedule; confirm the specific payer's bilateral pricing logic rather than assuming a flat 200%.
59 / XE, XS, XP, XU
  • Distinct procedural service, a genuinely separate session, structure, or non-overlapping service, not the same reconstructive stage reported twice under two codes.
  • Prefer the specific X-modifier where it applies; XS (separate structure) is frequently the defensible choice in this specialty, since a bundling conflict often comes down to two different anatomic sites (e.g., contralateral breast vs. affected breast) in the same session.
51 on an add-on code
  • Modifier 51 (multiple procedures) should never be appended to an add-on code like +15847, since add-on codes are already priced to reflect that they're reported alongside a primary procedure. Appending 51 to an add-on code is a common scrubber-rule gap that causes the line to reject rather than simply price wrong.

Two less frequently used modifiers still come up often enough in this specialty to name specifically. Modifier 52 (reduced services) applies when a procedure is partially reduced or eliminated at the physician's discretion, for example, a planned bilateral procedure where only one side is ultimately addressed for a clinical reason discovered intraoperatively. Modifier 53 (discontinued procedure) applies when a procedure is started but terminated before completion due to extenuating circumstances threatening the patient's well-being, such as a hemodynamic event during a lengthy flap reconstruction. Both require an operative note that clearly documents what was planned, what was actually done, and why the two diverged; without that contrast, a payer has no way to distinguish a legitimately reduced or discontinued service from an undercoded one.

The single most consequential modifier decision in the specialty, though, remains the 58/78/79 choice covered above, because it decides whether a legitimately staged, multi-visit reconstruction is recognized as one connected episode of care or fragmented into claims that look like duplicates or unrelated services to an automated payer system.

NCCI edits and MUE bundling

The National Correct Coding Initiative applies the same two governing values here as in every other specialty, and they decide whether a bundled or capped code is billable at all.

The NCCI modifier indicator attached to a Column 1/Column 2 code pair:

  • 0Never bypassable. No modifier overrides it; the second code is not separately payable regardless of documentation.
  • 1Bypassable with documentation. An NCCI-associated modifier (59 or a specific X-modifier) can override the edit, but only where the record shows the second service was genuinely distinct, separate site, separate session, separate structure.
  • 9Edit deleted. The pair no longer applies.

The MUE Adjudication Indicator (MAI) attached to a per-code, per-day unit cap:

  • 1Claim-line edit. Units above the cap deny that line but can often be split across lines with modifiers and documentation.
  • 2Absolute, date-of-service edit. CMS treats exceeding it as clinically implausible, no appeal path exists.
  • 3Appealable, date-of-service edit. A real appeal path exists with documentation that the excess units reflect genuinely distinct, medically necessary services.

Where bundling shows up most in this specialty: 19371 (complete capsulectomy) is not reported together with 19370 (partial capsulectomy/capsulorrhaphy) on the same breast in the same session, since the complete procedure already includes the lesser one, 19342 (implant replacement) can still be reported separately when a new implant is placed in the same session as the capsulectomy, per breast-procedure coding guidance summarizing AMA CPT Assistant instruction. A mastectomy performed in the same session as immediate tissue expander placement (19357) is a distinct-surgeon, distinct-procedure pairing that virtually always requires modifier 58 or 59 depending on whether one or two surgeons are billing, since the two procedures share the same operative field and date of service. Panniculectomy performed at the same session as an unrelated procedure in the same anatomic region, such as a ventral hernia repair, is a frequent Column 1/Column 2 pairing that payers scrutinize closely for genuinely separate medical necessity for each component. Blepharoplasty performed alongside a brow ptosis repair (the 67900 series) is another recurring pairing, since a droopy brow and dermatochalasis of the upper lid can present together and are billed on different, non-integumentary-chapter codes.

MUE unit caps and bilateral breast procedures

Bilateral procedures create a specific, recurring MUE question in this specialty: is a bilateral mastopexy, reduction, or augmentation billed as two units of the same code, or as one unit with modifier 50? The answer is payer-specific and directly interacts with the code's MUE value, a code with an MUE of 1 unit per day billed as two plain units (no modifier 50) will trip the unit cap and deny the second unit outright, even though the same bilateral work billed correctly as one line with modifier 50 (or, for some payers, two lines each with modifier 50, LT, and RT) would have paid. This is a coding-convention question a scrubber rule should enforce automatically once the correct convention for a given payer is confirmed, rather than something coders decide claim-by-claim from memory. ⚠️ The specific MUE value and MAI for the breast-repair and reconstruction code family were not confirmed against CMS's primary MUE table during this build for the reason noted below; confirm both the current MUE unit cap and the specific payer's preferred bilateral-billing convention before assuming either is safe to bill on autopilot.

Worked example: panniculectomy performed with a ventral hernia repair

This pairing deserves its own walk-through because it is one of the most common real-world combined operative sessions in this specialty, and one of the most likely to get flagged for bundling review. A post-bariatric patient has both a symptomatic incisional hernia (K43.2, incisional hernia without obstruction or gangrene, confirmed billable, ICD-10-CM) and a pannus meeting the functional-impairment standard covered above. The general surgeon or plastic surgeon repairs the hernia in the same session as the panniculectomy, since operating through the same incision once is safer and more efficient than two separate surgeries. Each component still has to independently support its own medical necessity on the claim: the hernia repair is justified by the hernia diagnosis and its own clinical findings (bulge, pain, incarceration risk), and the panniculectomy is justified by the pannus-specific functional documentation (chronic intertrigo, recurrent infection) covered in the panniculectomy section above, one procedure's medical necessity does not transfer to the other. Payers reviewing this combination look specifically for whether the operative note actually describes two distinct procedures with two distinct indications, or whether the panniculectomy language reads as an afterthought to the hernia repair (or vice versa), which is the pattern most likely to trigger a bundling denial or a request for records before either line is paid. Sequence and modifier logic follow the multiple-procedure rules covered in the global-period section: the higher-valued procedure is listed first, and modifier 59 or the appropriate X-modifier is appended to the panniculectomy code to signal it as a distinct procedure addressing a distinct problem, not a component of the hernia repair.

Payers process an enormous volume of these denials on autopilot; an appeal that immediately establishes the mandate applies is far more likely to be escalated past the first-level reviewer than one that argues clinical necessity in isolation.

That reporting view is what turns this guide's documentation guidance into a standing process improvement rather than a one-time read: track the specific missing element behind each cosmetic-coded denial for a quarter, and the fix is usually one added field or one added prompt in the intake or documentation workflow, not a wholesale process overhaul.

A WHCRA-based appeal converts fastest when it follows a consistent structure rather than restating the clinical note in prose. In order: (1) state plainly that the mandate applies, name the statute, confirm the plan is a group health plan or individual policy covered by it, and confirm the plan already covers mastectomy benefits; (2) identify the qualifying mastectomy, date of service, claim number if available, and diagnosis; (3) name the specific stage or benefit category being appealed (initial reconstruction, contralateral symmetry, prosthesis, or complication treatment) and tie it explicitly to one of the four mandated categories in the statute; (4) attach the operative note and any supporting diagnosis codes (Z90.1x, N65.1, I97.2, as applicable); and (5) request reprocessing under the plan's mastectomy benefit rather than its cosmetic-surgery exclusion specifically, since the two are frequently adjudicated by different internal review teams and a misrouted appeal can sit unprocessed even after approval in principle. For blepharoplasty and panniculectomy appeals, the same five-part structure applies with the payer's own published functional-necessity criteria substituted for the mandate citation in steps 1 and 3.

Prior authorization

Prior authorization touches a meaningful share of this specialty's higher-cost and functionally-justified procedures, and a missing authorization is one of the few denials in this pillar that is almost never worth appealing after the fact, it has to be prevented upstream.

Services that commonly require prior authorization across the payers we reviewed: panniculectomy, given its close proximity to cosmetic abdominoplasty and the weight-stability and chronic-skin-condition documentation payers want to review before approving it; blepharoplasty, given the same cosmetic-adjacency and the visual-field/photographic documentation standard; and certain stages of breast reconstruction, particularly contralateral symmetry procedures and later revision stages, where a payer's system may not automatically recognize the WHCRA linkage without the prior-authorization request spelling it out explicitly. Some payers also require authorization for autologous flap reconstruction given the surgical complexity and cost involved (payer prior-authorization policy summaries reviewed for this build; specific requirements vary by plan and should be confirmed directly with the payer before scheduling).

The documentation package that supports approval mirrors the medical-necessity standard covered throughout this guide, assembled before the request is submitted rather than gathered reactively after a denial:

  • 1For breast reconstruction: the qualifying mastectomy date and diagnosis, an explicit statement that WHCRA applies, and the specific stage being requested.
  • 2For blepharoplasty: visual field test results with and without taping, external photographs within the payer's specified window (commonly the past 12 months), and a documented functional complaint (difficulty reading, driving, or performing daily tasks).
  • 3For panniculectomy: photographs of the pannus at current weight, a chart record of recurrent intertrigo/cellulitis/skin breakdown treated over a period of months without resolution, and documented weight stability with height, starting weight, and current weight.

Beginning in 2026, CMS's own prior-authorization initiatives (including expanded service categories under Medicare demonstration models) put additional emphasis on payers providing specific, itemized reasons for a prior-authorization denial rather than a generic rejection, use that specificity when it's provided; a denial that names the exact missing element (a photograph date, a visual field threshold, a duration of treatment) tells you precisely what to resubmit rather than requiring a guess.

When a prior-authorization request is denied outright rather than returned for missing information, a peer-to-peer review, a direct conversation between the treating surgeon and the payer's medical director, is frequently the fastest path to reversal, faster than a written appeal in most commercial payer workflows, precisely because the functional-necessity and WHCRA arguments in this guide are clinical arguments a surgeon can make directly and specifically to another physician. Practices that route every prior-authorization denial straight into a written appeal queue without first requesting the peer-to-peer option are giving up the fastest lever available. Have the specific documentation, the visual field results, the photographs, the mastectomy date, the weight-stability record, in front of the surgeon before the call, not assembled afterward.

Multi-stage reconstruction creates a scheduling wrinkle prior-authorization workflows have to account for explicitly: a request approved for "breast reconstruction" as a general concept does not necessarily authorize every future stage automatically. Confirm whether the payer's authorization covers the full staged plan (tissue expander placement through implant exchange through nipple-areola reconstruction and symmetry surgery) as a single authorized episode, or whether each stage requires its own separate authorization request as the patient progresses, payer practice on this varies, and assuming the broader interpretation without confirming it in writing is a common source of a later-stage denial that arrives well after the authorization window a practice assumed it had.

Advance Beneficiary Notices for borderline Medicare cases

For a traditional Medicare patient where the functional documentation for blepharoplasty or panniculectomy is genuinely borderline, a visual field result close to but not clearly past the contractor's threshold, or a pannus with a shorter treatment history than the LCD article typically expects, an Advance Beneficiary Notice of Noncoverage (ABN) should be discussed with the patient and signed before the service is performed, not requested after a denial arrives. The ABN shifts financial responsibility to the patient if Medicare determines the service was not medically necessary, and its absence on a claim that is later denied on medical-necessity grounds typically means the practice, not the patient, absorbs the cost. This is a routine practice in most specialties with a functional-necessity coverage test; plastic surgery's version of it is simply blepharoplasty and panniculectomy in place of, for example, cardiology's repeat advanced imaging. An ABN is not a substitute for good documentation, it is a financial safeguard for the specific cases where documentation, however good, may still not clear a specific contractor's published threshold.

Coordination of benefits when Medicare is secondary

A meaningful share of the post-mastectomy reconstruction population is age 65 or older, meaning a patient may carry both an employer group health plan (primary, and WHCRA-reached) and Medicare (secondary, and not WHCRA-reached). In that scenario, WHCRA's mandate still governs the primary employer plan's obligation to cover the reconstruction, and the claim should be filed to the primary plan first citing the mandate as usual; Medicare, as secondary payer, then processes its portion under its own separate medical necessity and coordination-of-benefits rules once the primary payer has adjudicated. Filing to Medicare first in this situation, or assuming Medicare's coverage rules govern the claim because the patient is Medicare-eligible, misapplies both payers' rules at once and is a preventable source of a coordination-of-benefits denial that then has to be unwound and refiled in the correct order.

Timely filing and reconstruction that spans plan years

A staged reconstruction can easily span a calendar year boundary, tissue expander placement in November, exchange to the permanent implant the following spring, contralateral symmetry and nipple reconstruction later still. Three practical consequences follow, and none of them are optional to plan for once a reconstruction is underway:

  • 1Deductibles and out-of-pocket maximums reset. WHCRA guarantees coverage, not that the patient's cost-sharing carries over; a later stage falling in a new plan year is subject to that year's deductible and coinsurance structure even though it is part of the same mandated episode of care.
  • 2A change of employer or plan does not extinguish the mandate, if the patient moves to a new group health plan that also covers mastectomies, WHCRA's requirement to cover reconstruction attaches to the new plan as well, though the new plan will not have the original mastectomy claim in its own system, making it even more important to have the original mastectomy date, diagnosis, and operative report ready to submit alongside the later-stage claim.
  • 3Timely filing clocks reset per claim, not per episode. Each stage is its own claim with its own timely filing deadline measured from its own date of service; a practice that treats a multi-stage reconstruction as administratively "one claim" risks missing the filing deadline for a later stage while still waiting on adjudication of an earlier one.

Track staged reconstructions as a linked case in the practice management system, with the original mastectomy date, diagnosis, and plan information attached to every subsequent stage's claim record, rather than relying on staff memory or a note in the chart that doesn't travel with the billing workflow.

Frequently asked questions

Does insurance ever have to cover breast reconstruction after a mastectomy?

Yes, and it is not discretionary. The Women's Health and Cancer Rights Act of 1998 requires any group or individual health plan that covers mastectomies to also cover reconstruction of the affected breast, surgery on the other breast for symmetry, prostheses, and treatment of physical complications including lymphedema, at every stage, with no time limit on when the patient elects it. A plan cannot deny a reconstruction claim as cosmetic when WHCRA applies; the fix is citing the mandate by name in the claim and appeal, not just describing the clinical picture.

What is the WHCRA and does it apply to every health plan?

WHCRA is federal law (Public Law 105-277, Title IX) that applies to group health plans and individual health insurance coverage that already provides mastectomy benefits; it is not limited to cancer patients specifically, but to anyone receiving mastectomy coverage under the plan. It does not apply to traditional fee-for-service Medicare or to Medicaid, which cover reconstruction under their own separate medical necessity rules rather than under WHCRA, so citing WHCRA in a Medicare appeal is citing the wrong authority. It does not independently force a plan to cover mastectomies in the first place, and self-funded church plans have historically had different applicability rules, so confirm plan type before assuming the mandate applies, though it covers the overwhelming majority of commercial and employer-sponsored plans.

How do we bill the two stages of implant-based breast reconstruction?

Stage one is CPT 19357, tissue expander placement, which already includes every subsequent saline-fill expansion visit inside its 90-day global period, so those visits are not separately billable. Stage two, typically performed after the global period closes, is CPT 11970, replacement of the tissue expander with the permanent implant, reported once per breast. Append modifier 58 to 11970 when it falls within the global period of an earlier related procedure, since it is a planned, staged continuation of the same reconstruction, not a separate unrelated service.

Can we bill for surgery on the other, healthy breast for symmetry?

Yes, and WHCRA specifically requires it when the patient had a mastectomy covered by the plan and elects reconstruction. A reduction mammaplasty (19318), mastopexy (19316), or augmentation (19325) performed on the unaffected breast to match the reconstructed side is reconstructive, not cosmetic, and should be coded to reflect that intent, commonly supported by ICD-10 N65.1, disproportion of reconstructed breast. Documentation has to tie the procedure explicitly to the contralateral reconstruction, not just describe a size or shape complaint in isolation.

What has to be documented for a blepharoplasty to be covered instead of cosmetic?

Payers look for objective proof that excess eyelid skin or fat is obstructing vision, not just aesthetic dissatisfaction. That typically means visual field testing performed with and without the lid taped up, external photographs taken within roughly the prior 12 months showing the lid margin at or below the pupil, and a documented functional complaint such as difficulty reading or driving. Exact numeric thresholds are payer-specific; Aetna's published policy, for example, looks for a pre-taping superior visual field of 30 degrees or less with a defined minimum improvement after taping, and Medicare contractors publish their own separate blepharoplasty coverage articles.

What's the real difference between panniculectomy and abdominoplasty for billing purposes?

Panniculectomy (CPT 15830) removes the overhanging pannus itself and is potentially reconstructive when it is causing a documented functional problem such as recurrent intertrigo, cellulitis, or skin breakdown that has not resolved with medical treatment. Abdominoplasty adds fascial plication and often umbilical transposition for cosmetic contouring, reported with add-on code 15847, and is not separately covered by Medicare or most commercial payers on its own. Billing 15847 without a covered 15830 on the same claim is one of the fastest ways to convert a partially payable claim into a fully denied one.

When do we use modifier 58 versus 78 versus 79 in a staged reconstruction?

Modifier 58 marks a planned, staged, or more extensive procedure performed by the same physician during the global period of a prior related procedure, which describes most multi-stage breast reconstruction by design. Modifier 78 is for an unplanned return to the operating room for a complication of the original procedure, such as evacuating a hematoma. Modifier 79 marks a procedure that is unrelated to the original surgery even though it falls inside its global period. The three are mutually exclusive on a given line, and picking the wrong one is a common reason a legitimately staged reconstruction claim gets bundled or denied as a duplicate.

Why do reconstructive claims keep getting denied as cosmetic, and how do we fight it?

Most practices default to treating any breast, eyelid, or body-contouring procedure as potentially cosmetic and under-document the functional or mandate-based basis for coverage, so the payer's system flags it automatically. The fix is upstream and in the appeal: for breast reconstruction, cite WHCRA by name and reference the qualifying mastectomy claim; for blepharoplasty and panniculectomy, attach the objective functional documentation the payer's own policy asks for, not a general medical-necessity statement. A denial that never named the mandate or the specific coverage criterion is usually a documentation gap, not a genuinely uncovered service.
