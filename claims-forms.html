CMS-1500 vs UB-04: the two claim forms, and the fields that actually decide payment.

Every medical claim in the United States is filed on one of two forms, and which one applies depends on the type of provider billing, not the type of service delivered. Getting the wrong form, or the right form with the wrong field, is one of the most basic and most common ways a clean encounter turns into a rejected claim. This guide walks through both forms field by field, their electronic equivalents, and the specific errors that cause each one to bounce.

CMS-1500 fields
33 numbered boxes

The professional claim form. Governed by NUCC's 1500 Claim Form instructions — used by physicians, non-physician practitioners and most outpatient practices. The full field-by-field reference lives in NUCC's own reference manual, not reproduced here.

UB-04 field locators
81 form locators (FL 1–81)

The institutional claim form, also called the CMS-1450. Governed by NUBC — used by hospitals, SNFs, home health and other facility-type providers. Full instructions sit in the Medicare Claims Processing Manual, Pub. 100-04, Chapter 25.

Electronic equivalents
837P & 837I

The HIPAA-mandated electronic transaction sets almost every claim actually travels as. 837P mirrors the CMS-1500, 837I mirrors the UB-04.

Key takeaways

  • The form follows the provider type. CMS-1500 for professional services, UB-04 for institutional. A hospital-owned clinic visit can generate both.
  • Electronic is the default. The 837P and 837I transactions are what almost everyone actually submits; the paper layout still defines the fields.
  • UB-04's Type of Bill code decides how the whole claim is read. Three digits, and getting one wrong routes or rejects the entire claim.
  • CMS-1500's diagnosis pointer is a linking field, not a coding field. It connects each service line to a diagnosis letter, and a broken link rejects the line.
  • UB-04 carries structural information the CMS-1500 doesn't need. Condition, occurrence and value codes describe an institutional episode of care, not a single office visit.
  • One visit can legitimately produce two claims. A hospital-based encounter can generate a UB-04 for the facility and a CMS-1500 for the physician — two providers billing two different things, not duplicate billing.

Professional vs. institutional: which form applies

The CMS-1500 is the professional claim form used by physicians, non-physician practitioners and most outpatient practices to bill for the services they personally perform: the office visit, the procedure, the interpretation. The UB-04, also known by its CMS number as the CMS-1450, is the institutional claim form used by hospitals, skilled nursing facilities, home health agencies, hospices and other facility-type providers to bill for the facility side of care: the room, the equipment, the nursing staff, the overhead.

The distinction is about who is billing, not where the care happens. A patient seen in a hospital-owned outpatient clinic can generate two separate claims for the same visit: a UB-04 from the hospital for the facility fee, and a CMS-1500 from the treating physician for the professional fee, even though the patient experienced it as one appointment. This is also the structural reason professional and technical component splits exist in specialties like radiology and cardiology, covered in more depth on our cardiology coding guide.

The split traces back to how Medicare itself is organized: Part A generally covers the institutional side of care and Part B the professional side, and the CMS-1500 and UB-04 grew up as the paper forms built around that division. Every payer that followed Medicare's lead inherited the same two-form structure, which is why the distinction holds regardless of whether the claim is going to a commercial payer, Medicaid, or Medicare itself. An independent diagnostic testing facility, a physician-owned ambulatory surgery center's professional fees, and a solo practitioner's office visit all sit on the CMS-1500 side of the line for the same reason: none of them is billing for a facility.

New billers most often get tripped up on two variations. First, an ambulatory surgery center bills its own facility fee on the UB-04, but the surgeon's professional fee for the same procedure still goes out on a CMS-1500 — two forms, two payers of record, sometimes even two different remittance timelines for what was clinically one operation. Second, a physician who is directly employed by a hospital may still have their professional services billed on a CMS-1500 under the hospital's billing NPI, even though the hospital itself files a UB-04 for the facility side of the same encounter; employment status doesn't change which form describes which piece of the bill. Both scenarios are a common source of confusion for anyone new to institutional billing, and both are covered in full, with the exact claim mechanics, in the spoke below.

Home health and hospice add a third variant worth knowing, because both bill almost entirely on the UB-04 despite delivering care in a patient's home rather than inside a building. What makes them institutional for billing purposes isn't the physical location of the care, it's the type of entity delivering it and how Medicare and most payers classify that entity's services — an episode of home health care or a period of hospice care is billed as a single institutional claim covering a defined timeframe, not itemized visit by visit the way a physician's CMS-1500 claims would be. A physician who certifies a patient for home health or hospice, or who continues to see that patient for unrelated care during the episode, still bills their own professional services separately on a CMS-1500. It's the same pattern as the hospital-owned clinic: one patient, one clinical relationship, two claim types running in parallel because two different kinds of entity are billing for two different kinds of service.

CMS-1500: the fields that decide payment

The CMS-1500 has 33 numbered boxes, but a small subset carries almost all of the payment risk. Boxes 1 through 13 identify the patient, the insured and the relationship between them; errors here cause eligibility mismatches rather than clinical denials — a subscriber ID that doesn't match what the payer has on file, or a patient-relationship-to-insured field that contradicts the eligibility response the front desk pulled that morning. Those errors are usually caught before the claim ever reaches adjudication, because most clearinghouses validate patient and subscriber fields against the eligibility transaction automatically. The boxes that most often cause preventable rejections sit further down the form, and they cluster into three categories: the provider identifier fields that confirm who rendered and who is billing for a service, the diagnosis pointer that links each service line back to a supporting diagnosis, and the billing-versus-rendering provider distinction that determines whose enrollment record the claim is actually checked against.

Notice how many of these trace back to the same three categories. Box 24J and box 33 are both provider identifier fields, and the payer's adjudication system checks them against different enrollment records: 24J against the individual practitioner who rendered the service, 33 against the group that is billing for it. A mismatch between the two — a rendering provider who isn't reassigned to the billing group, for instance — rejects the claim even though every code on it is correct. Box 24E, the diagnosis pointer, is a linking error rather than a coding error, which is exactly why it slips past coding reviews that only check whether the codes themselves are valid. And the billing-versus-rendering distinction in boxes 24J, 32 and 33 together is its own recurring source of rejections, especially for locum tenens coverage, incident-to billing and multi-location groups where the physical location of service, the rendering individual and the billing entity are three different things that all have to agree. UB-04: the fields that decide payment

The UB-04 uses form locators rather than numbered boxes, and it carries more structural information than the CMS-1500 because it is describing an institutional stay or episode rather than a single office encounter. Three form locators do most of the work of telling the payer what kind of claim this is and what it covers.

Beyond those three, the UB-04 has entire blocks of form locators with no CMS-1500 equivalent at all, because a professional claim never needs to describe them. Condition codes, in the FL 18–28 range, flag circumstances that affect how the claim should be processed — things like a beneficiary-requested billing situation or a change from inpatient to outpatient status mid-stay. Occurrence codes, in the FL 31–34 range, record specific dated events tied to the claim, such as an accident date or the admission date itself. Value codes, in the FL 39–41 range, carry dollar amounts or quantities the payer needs for adjudication, separate from the charge lines themselves. None of these exist on the CMS-1500 because a single office visit doesn't need to describe an episode's conditions, dated events and values — it just needs to describe the visit. That extra structure is also why an institutional claim has more places for a single wrong entry to cascade into a rejection than a professional claim does.

Two form locators are worth knowing simply as anchor points for how the rest of the form is organized. FL 1 carries the billing provider's name and address — the institutional equivalent of CMS-1500 box 33 — and sits at the very top of the form because everything else on the claim is read in reference to who is billing. FL 81 sits at the opposite end, carrying the taxonomy code and qualifier that further identifies the billing provider's specialty designation to the payer. Between those two bookends sits every category covered above: identification, bill classification, dated occurrences, condition flags, dollar values, revenue and procedure detail, and diagnosis coding, which is why the UB-04 is structurally more complex than the CMS-1500 — it isn't describing one service, it's describing an entire institutional episode from admission (or the outpatient equivalent) through discharge. Paper forms vs. the 837 electronic transaction

Under HIPAA's electronic transaction standard, almost every claim above a small-volume threshold has to be submitted electronically, as the 837P for professional claims or the 837I for institutional claims, rather than on the printed form. The paper CMS-1500 and UB-04 still matter for two reasons: a small number of providers qualify for an exception and still submit paper, and the field structure of the paper form is what the electronic transaction is built around, so payer remittance advice and denial correspondence frequently still reference box numbers and form locators by their paper-form names even when the claim was never printed.

In practice this means staff who only ever see a clearinghouse dashboard still need to understand what box 24E or form locator 4 represents, because that is the language payer denial reason codes speak.

The remaining paper submitters are a narrow group, generally small-volume or non-standard submitters who qualify for an exception to the electronic mandate, plus certain payer-specific situations that still call for a printed form. For everyone else, electronic submission isn't just the default — it's the more reliable path, because a clearinghouse or scrubbing engine can validate field-level logic (a diagnosis pointer that doesn't map to a listed diagnosis, a Type of Bill frequency digit that contradicts what's already on file) before the claim ever reaches the payer, catching in seconds what a paper submission would only surface weeks later as a rejection letter. The narrow paper exceptions that remain, and why electronic submission catches so much more before it becomes a denial, are covered in 837P and 837I: why almost nobody actually mails a paper claim anymore.

Where clean claims go wrong, across both forms

Strip away the field numbers and both forms fail for a small, repeating set of reasons. Almost every preventable rejection on either form traces back to one of four root causes: a mismatched identifier somewhere in the provider chain, a linking field pointing at something that doesn't exist elsewhere on the claim, a classification code that contradicts the claim's own history with the payer, or a required field left blank because nobody realized the payer's specific policy required it. Recognizing the pattern matters more than memorizing every field, because it's the pattern that tells a biller where to look first when a denial reason code doesn't immediately make sense.

This is also why a coding review and a claims-scrubbing review catch different things, and why a practice needs both rather than assuming one substitutes for the other. A coding review asks whether the CPT, HCPCS or ICD-10 codes themselves are correct and supported by documentation. A claims-scrubbing review asks whether the fields around those codes are internally consistent — whether the pointer resolves, whether the identifiers match, whether the classification code agrees with the claim's own history. A claim can pass a thorough coding review and still fail on submission because nobody checked whether box 24E pointed at a diagnosis that existed in box 21. That's the gap our claims scrubbing service is built to close before a claim ever reaches the payer.

Step by step: catching form errors before submission

  1. Confirm the form before the codesVerify billing provider type first — professional or institutional — so the entire claim is being built on the correct field structure from the start, rather than discovering the wrong form was used after codes and modifiers are already entered.
  2. Validate every identifier in the provider chainRendering NPI, billing NPI, service facility, and for UB-04 claims, taxonomy in FL 81. Each is checked against a different enrollment record, and a single mismatch anywhere in the chain rejects the whole claim.
  3. Trace every linking field to what it points atDiagnosis pointers to box 21 letters, UB-04 principal procedure dates against the billed service date range. A linking field that points at nothing is invisible to a coding review that only checks the codes themselves.
  4. Check classification codes against claim historyType of Bill frequency digit against whether an original claim is already on file; revenue codes against the HCPCS billed on the same UB-04 line.
  5. Run the claim through a scrubbing engine before it leaves the buildingAn electronic pre-submission edit catches field-level logic errors in seconds — the same errors a paper submission would only surface weeks later as a denial.
  6. Read the denial reason code as a field reference, not a mysteryMost payer denial and rejection codes map directly back to a specific box or form locator. Knowing the field structure turns a cryptic reason code into an obvious fix.
Pro tip

When a claim rejects for a reason that seems to make no sense, check the diagnosis pointer or the Type of Bill first. Both are linking fields rather than clinical fields, so the coding can be entirely correct and the claim will still fail if the link between fields is broken. This is one of the fastest checks in a rejection queue and one of the easiest to overlook — a coder reviewing the same claim for the third time often stares straight past a linking error because they're re-checking the codes themselves, not the connections between them. Train denial-queue staff to ask "does this field point somewhere real?" before they ask "is this code right?" and a surprising share of same-day-resolvable rejections stop being escalated at all.

Do and don't

Do
  • Confirm the billing provider type before assuming which form applies.
  • Check that every diagnosis pointer in box 24E maps to a letter used in box 21.
  • Verify the Type of Bill frequency digit matches whether this is an original, correction or void.
  • Match revenue codes to the HCPCS code billed on the same UB-04 line.
  • Keep place of service in box 24B consistent with the facility address in box 32.
  • Treat the billing and rendering provider fields as two separate checks, not one.
Don't
  • Don't assume the service type decides the form; the provider type does.
  • Don't submit a UB-04 correction without confirming the payer has the original claim on file.
  • Don't leave form locator 80 blank when a payer's local policy requires a remark.
  • Don't bill a rendering NPI that is not reassigned to the billing group.
  • Don't treat electronic submission as a reason to ignore the underlying field structure.
  • Don't assume a hospital-employed physician's professional claim and the hospital's facility claim are duplicates — they're two different bills.

Frequently asked questions

How do I know whether to use CMS-1500 or UB-04?

It follows the provider type, not the service. A physician office, individual practitioner or most outpatient clinics bill professional services on the CMS-1500 regardless of where the service happens. A hospital, skilled nursing facility, home health agency or other institutional provider bills on the UB-04, including for outpatient hospital services. A single patient visit to a hospital-owned clinic can generate both: a UB-04 for the facility fee and a CMS-1500 for the physician's professional fee.

Does anyone still submit paper claim forms?

Rarely, and mostly for small-volume or non-standard submitters who qualify for an exception under HIPAA's electronic filing requirement. The overwhelming majority of claims move as the 837P or 837I electronic transaction through a clearinghouse. The paper form layout still matters because it defines the fields, and because some payer correspondence and remittance advice references box numbers by their paper-form names even for electronic submissions.

What is a Type of Bill code and why does it matter so much on UB-04?

The Type of Bill is a three-digit code in form locator 4 that tells the payer the facility type, the bill classification, and the sequence of the claim, such as an original claim, a correction, or a void. Getting any of the three digits wrong is one of the most common reasons a UB-04 claim rejects outright, because the payer's system routes and processes the claim differently based on this single code before it looks at anything else on the form.

Why do CMS-1500 claims get rejected for the diagnosis pointer?

Box 24E on the CMS-1500 links each billed service line back to a specific diagnosis letter from box 21, rather than repeating the diagnosis code itself. A pointer to a letter that does not exist in box 21, or a service line pointing to a diagnosis that does not support medical necessity for that procedure, is a common and entirely avoidable rejection. It is a linking error, not a coding error, which is why it is easy to miss in a coding review that only checks the codes themselves.

Do you handle both professional and institutional claims?

Yes. We submit both the 837P and 837I as appropriate for the client, scrub each against form-specific edit rules before submission, and reconcile remittances back against what was billed on each claim type. Practices with both a physician group and a facility component get both handled under the same engagement.

Want claim forms handled correctly the first time?

We scrub every CMS-1500 and UB-04 claim against payer-specific field rules before submission, so a form-linking error never becomes a denial.
