NPI registration: Type 1, Type 2, and why a group practice needs both.

The National Provider Identifier shows up on every claim, every credentialing application, and every payer enrollment file a practice will ever touch, and it's free to obtain. The part that trips practices up isn't the application itself, it's understanding that the individual provider and the organization are two separate registrations, and that a stale NPPES record can quietly cause claim problems long after the number was first issued.

Reviewed by Dana Whitfield, JD · Healthcare Regulatory Counsel · Part of our practice licensing guide

Key takeaways

  • NPI registration is free, done through NPPES, and doesn't require a third-party vendor or fee to complete.
  • Type 1 is the individual, Type 2 is the organization, a group practice generally needs both, not just the provider's.
  • A mismatched taxonomy code is a real claim-rejection risk, not just an administrative detail, it has to reflect what's actually being billed.
  • Stale NPPES records are subject to deactivation, a bigger problem than a single denied claim, since it can interrupt everything billed under that NPI.

The application: free, online, through NPPES

Every NPI is issued through NPPES (the National Plan and Provider Enumeration System), managed by CMS, at nppes.cms.hhs.gov. There is no application fee, and the standard online application is generally the fastest path to a number. The application asks for the applicant's identifying information, the taxonomy code that describes their specialty or the organization's service line, and, for an organization, the entity's legal business information, which is why this step generally follows entity formation rather than preceding it.

Type 1 versus Type 2: the distinction that trips people up

A Type 1 NPI identifies an individual practitioner, the physician, nurse practitioner, or other licensed provider delivering care. A Type 2 NPI identifies an organization, the legal business entity that bills for that care. These are separate registrations with separate numbers, and a group practice generally needs both: the Type 1 for each individual provider, and one Type 2 for the organization itself.

Type 1 versus Type 2.
Type 1 (Individual)Type 2 (Organization)
Identifies the individual practitionerIdentifies the billing entity
Tied to the person, follows them across employersTied to the legal business entity's EIN
Required for every practicing providerRequired for the organization once it bills as an entity
Taxonomy reflects the individual's specialtyTaxonomy reflects the organization's service line

A common early mistake is applying for the Type 1 NPI and assuming it covers the practice as a whole, only to discover during Medicare or commercial payer enrollment that the organizational NPI was never obtained, which stalls enrollment until the Type 2 application is filed and processed. Both can be applied for independently and early; neither depends on the other being finished first, though the Type 2 does need the entity's legal name and EIN already finalized.

Taxonomy codes and the claim-rejection risk of getting them wrong

A taxonomy code on the NPI record describes the provider's specialty or the organization's service line, and it's not just a label, some payers cross-check the taxonomy code against the services actually billed, and a mismatch between what the taxonomy says and what the claim describes is a real source of rejections and denials, not merely an administrative inconsistency. This matters most when a provider changes specialty focus, adds a subspecialty, or a practice expands into a new service line: the taxonomy code on file has to be updated to reflect that change, or claims billed under the new focus can run into trouble even though the underlying NPI itself is still valid.

  • 1Confirm the taxonomy code matches current practice. A provider who's shifted specialty focus needs the NPPES record updated to match, not left as originally filed.
  • 2Update NPPES when a provider's practice address changes. An outdated address on file is a common, avoidable source of enrollment friction.
  • 3Update the organizational record when the entity's structure changes. A new location, a new legal name, or a change in ownership should be reflected on the Type 2 record.

NPI deactivation: the risk of letting a record go stale

An NPI itself doesn't expire, and there's no renewal fee. But NPPES records that go without confirmation or update over a long period are subject to deactivation, and a deactivated NPI is a materially bigger problem than one denied claim, it can interrupt every claim billed under that number until the deactivation is resolved and the number is reactivated. Practices that treat NPPES as a one-time application rather than a record that needs periodic confirmation are the ones most likely to be surprised by this.

A reactivated NPI doesn't just resume working the moment the record is corrected, either, there can be a gap between when the deactivation is identified and when claims start flowing normally again, since payers and clearinghouses that cached the old status have to recognize the change. That gap is entirely avoidable, which is what makes it worth building NPPES review into a practice's routine credentialing hygiene rather than discovering the problem only when claims start rejecting.

How NPI data feeds Medicare enrollment and payer credentialing

The NPI isn't just an identifier sitting in isolation, it's the anchor that PECOS (Medicare's enrollment system) and every commercial payer's credentialing file reference back to. When a provider's NPPES record and their PECOS or CAQH profile fall out of sync, a name change, an address update made in one system but not the other, a taxonomy code updated in NPPES but not reflected in the payer's file, that mismatch is a common, avoidable source of credentialing delays and claim edits. Because NPPES is the original source of record for the number itself, the practical habit is to update NPPES first whenever something changes, then propagate that same change to PECOS, CAQH, and each payer's credentialing file, rather than updating a payer profile directly and letting NPPES lag behind.

Pro tip

Treat NPPES the same way as any other credential that needs periodic upkeep, review every provider's and the organization's record at least annually, and immediately after any specialty change, address change, or ownership change, rather than waiting for a claim problem to surface it.

Step by step: getting NPI registration right

  1. Apply for each provider's Type 1 NPIFree, through NPPES; can be done early and independently of entity formation.
  2. Apply for the organization's Type 2 NPI once the entity is finalizedRequires the entity's legal business name and EIN, so it follows entity formation.
  3. Select the taxonomy code that matches actual practiceBoth for the individual's specialty and the organization's service line, not a default or approximate code.
  4. Update NPPES whenever specialty, address, or ownership changesA stale record is a claim-rejection and deactivation risk, not a cosmetic issue.
  5. Confirm both NPIs are reflected correctly on every credentialing and payer enrollment applicationMissing the Type 2 during enrollment is one of the most common early setbacks.

Do and don't

Do
  • Apply for both the Type 1 and Type 2 NPI, not just the individual's.
  • Keep taxonomy codes current with what's actually being billed.
  • Update NPPES immediately after a specialty, address, or ownership change.
  • Review every provider's and the organization's NPPES record at least annually.
Don't
  • Don't assume the individual's NPI covers the organization for billing purposes.
  • Don't leave a taxonomy code unchanged after a genuine specialty shift.
  • Don't let an NPPES record sit unconfirmed indefinitely, it's subject to deactivation.

Frequently asked questions

Does an NPI cost anything?

No. NPI registration through NPPES is free for both individual (Type 1) and organizational (Type 2) applications. There's no application fee and no renewal fee, the number itself doesn't expire, though a record can be deactivated if it goes stale, which is a separate issue from cost.

Does a solo practitioner need a Type 2 NPI?

It depends on how the practice bills. A true sole proprietor billing entirely under their own name and Type 1 NPI may not need a Type 2. But the moment the practice bills under a separate legal business entity, a PLLC or PC, which most licensed practices are required to form, payers generally expect a Type 2 NPI for that organization on claims and enrollment applications, even if the practice has only one provider.

What happens if NPPES records aren't updated after a provider changes specialty or location?

Stale NPPES data creates two separate risks. First, a taxonomy code that no longer matches what's actually being billed increases claim rejection and denial risk, since some payers cross-check the taxonomy against the services billed. Second, NPPES records that go long enough without any update or confirmation can be flagged and are subject to deactivation, which is a far bigger problem than a single denied claim, it can interrupt every claim tied to that NPI until it's resolved.

Confirm before you rely on this. Licensing fees, compact membership, training requirements and application processes change, and change by state.
