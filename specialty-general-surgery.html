General surgery medical billing and coding: the complete 2026 guide.

General surgery bills more distinct procedure families than almost any other specialty — gallbladder, hernia, appendix, bowel, wound care, bariatric — and every one of them has its own bundling logic, its own global period traps, and its own diagnosis-specificity requirements. This guide covers what actually changed for 2026, every core procedure family with real CPT and ICD-10 detail, the MUE unit ceilings competitors don't publish, jurisdiction-specific LCD citations instead of “per CMS,” and denial appeal language paired to the actual CARC code, not generic prevention tips.

Reviewed by Phares A., MD · Physiotherapist, CPC (AAPC) · Updated August 2026

Key takeaways

  • MUE unit ceilings are stated per procedure family below, not left for billers to guess. 47562 caps at 1 unit per date of service, and the debridement family caps similarly — know the ceiling before your scrubber flags it as an error.
  • Every top denial gets the actual appeal argument, not just a prevention tip. CO-50, CO-197, CO-59, CO-97/CO-236, and CO-29 each pair with reusable language you can put directly into an appeal letter.
  • Wound care and debridement carry at least five distinct active LCDs across MACs — Novitas, First Coast, WPS, CGS, and Wellpoint each publish their own, with different effective dates, confirmed directly against the CMS Coverage Database.
  • The laparoscopic-to-open conversion rule and a worked 58-vs-78-vs-79 global-period timeline are covered at the code level — the two places we found every competitor guide either skips the mechanics or hand-waves them.

General surgery billing at a glance

General surgery's denial volume concentrates in four places: diagnosis codes that don't carry the laterality, obstruction, or recurrence axis a payer's medical necessity check is actually looking for; NCCI bundling between the primary procedure and everything a surgeon does to get there (lysis of adhesions, incidental findings, wound closure); global-period modifier errors on the 090-day procedures that dominate the specialty's volume; and MUE unit ceilings nobody on staff has actually looked up. A general surgery practice billing anywhere near a 95%-or-higher clean-claim rate is doing something most of this specialty's competitors aren't: catching the diagnosis-specificity and global-period modifier errors before submission rather than in an appeal queue. Every section below exists to make that catch possible at charge entry, not after a remit comes back.

General surgery has also been shifting steadily toward ambulatory surgery centers for the procedures in this guide that can safely move there — laparoscopic cholecystectomy, inguinal hernia repair, and select colectomy cases increasingly go to ASCs where the payer and patient acuity profile supports it. That shift changes the billing picture in ways that are easy to miss if the front-end scheduling and back-end billing teams aren't coordinating on it: facility-fee billing runs through an entirely separate claim and payment system from the surgeon's professional fee, prior-authorization requirements can differ by site of service even for the identical CPT code, and assistant-surgeon and anesthesia coverage patterns at an ASC don't always mirror what the same procedure would look like in a hospital OR. None of the coding logic in this guide changes by site of service, but the operational coordination around it does.

The rest of this guide is the detail behind that table: what 2026 actually changed, every core family's code selection logic, the ICD-10 specificity that determines whether a claim is medically necessary on its face, the modifiers that carry real financial weight, and the payer-specific and MAC-specific nuances that generic billing content skips.

What changed for general surgery in 2026

Two changes matter operationally this year, and neither one is a rounding error.

CMS's efficiency adjustment. The CY2026 Medicare Physician Fee Schedule final rule (CMS-1832-F) applies a −2.5% efficiency adjustment to the work RVUs and intraservice time of nearly all non-time-based codes on the fee schedule — procedures, diagnostic tests, and most surgical codes fall inside it. The adjustment is CMS's response to efficiencies that accrue in how a service is performed over time but never get captured through the normal RVU-update process; it's built from a five-year lookback tied to the Medicare Economic Index productivity adjustment, and CMS intends to reapply it every three years going forward rather than as a one-time cut. Time-based codes, telehealth-list services, and maternity codes carrying an MMM global period are exempt, and any CPT code newly created and initially valued for 2026 is exempt in its first year. Nearly every core general surgery code in this guide — lap chole, hernia repair, appendectomy, debridement — is a non-time-based procedure code and is not on the exempt list, so the practical effect is a modest reduction in the work RVU (and therefore the payment) for most of the codes below relative to 2025, on top of whatever the annual conversion factor does. Don't estimate the dollar impact from memory: pull your own locality's 2026 rate from the CMS Physician Fee Schedule Look-Up Tool by code, because the adjustment interacts with locality-specific practice expense and malpractice RVUs in ways that aren't uniform across codes.

The 2026 CPT code set. AMA's 2026 CPT update carries 418 total changes: 288 new codes, 84 deletions, and 46 revisions, effective January 1, 2026. The general surgery families in this guide — gallbladder, hernia, appendectomy, debridement — were not restructured the way lower extremity revascularization was for cardiology; those core codes carry into 2026 unchanged. The general-surgery-relevant additions cluster elsewhere: a new code, 43889, was added for transoral endoscopic suturing to reduce stomach volume, a minimally invasive, incision-free weight-loss procedure available for adults with a BMI of 30 or higher plus a related comorbidity — a materially different coverage and documentation profile than the surgical bariatric procedures under NCD 100.1 covered later in this guide. Biopsy codes and spinal decompression codes were also revised this cycle but sit outside general surgery's core scope. Audit your charge master against the full AMA 2026 summary before your first claims batch of the year goes out, not after a denial pattern surfaces.

What didn't change, and why that matters operationally. The core gallbladder, hernia, and appendectomy code families in this guide carry into 2026 without deletion or renumbering, unlike cardiology's wholesale revascularization overhaul. That stability is worth stating plainly, because it means 2026 denial spikes on these families are not a code-validity problem the way they would be on a deleted code — if your lap chole or hernia claims are denying more than usual this year, the cause is almost certainly the efficiency adjustment's payment-level impact, a documentation gap, or a payer policy change, not a retired CPT code. Don't burn audit time checking code validity on a family that didn't move; check the payment amount against the current fee schedule and check the diagnosis-to-procedure match instead.

The efficiency adjustment also interacts with site of service in a way that's easy to miss. Practice expense RVUs are calculated separately for facility (hospital, ASC) and non-facility (office) settings, and the 2.5% work RVU cut applies on top of whatever site-specific practice expense value already governs the code — the net dollar effect on a hospital-based lap chole and an in-office debridement won't move by the same percentage even though both codes took the identical work RVU cut. Run your own top 20 general surgery codes through the CMS PFS Look-Up Tool by site of service before assuming a flat percentage revenue impact across the practice.

The new endoscopic bariatric code, 43889, is also a coverage question worth flagging early rather than discovering at the claim-submission stage. Because it's a genuinely new procedure code for 2026, individual payer coverage policy is still developing, and several plans have not yet published a formal medical policy addressing it at all — a prior-authorization request for this specific procedure is more likely to hit a payer's exception-review process than a standard automated authorization pathway this year, and scheduling around a several-week authorization timeline rather than the standard turnaround is the safer operational assumption for now.

E/M coding for general surgery practices

New patient office visits (99202–99205) and established patient visits (99212–99215) are leveled on total time or medical decision making — either pathway fully justifies the code, and you don't need both. For an established patient, 99214 requires 30–39 minutes of total time (face-to-face plus same-day chart review, orders, and documentation) or moderate MDM; 99215 requires 40–54 minutes or high MDM. A new hernia consult with imaging review, a documented risk discussion, and a decision for surgery is routinely a 99204 or 99205 on MDM alone, even in a 20-minute visit, if the note actually documents the number and complexity of problems addressed, the data reviewed, and the risk of the management option selected — the time floor isn't the only path in, and coders who default to time-based leveling under-code these visits constantly.

The failure mode we see constantly in general surgery specifically: a new hernia or gallbladder consult genuinely qualifies as moderate or high MDM — a chronic condition with surgical risk to weigh, imaging and labs reviewed, a real risk discussion about the procedure itself — but the visit note reads like a standard history-and-exam template and doesn't actually document the number and complexity of problems addressed or the amount and complexity of data reviewed, so the claim only supports the lower code the documentation happens to show, regardless of how much clinical complexity was genuinely involved.

A referral for a surgical consult doesn't require special consult codes under Medicare rules — CPT deleted the distinct consultation code set from Medicare recognition years ago, and Medicare wants the visit billed as a new or established patient E/M based on whether the surgeon has seen that patient in the same specialty group within the past three years, not on whether the visit was framed as a “consult” by the referring physician. Some commercial payers still recognize and pay consultation codes (99242–99245); check the specific payer's policy before defaulting to new-patient E/M codes across the board.

Modifier 25 is the correct modifier for a same-day E/M that's separately identifiable from a minor procedure — for example, an established patient presents with a new complaint, the surgeon performs a full E/M workup, and also performs an in-office debridement (000-day global) the same visit. It is not the correct modifier for an E/M billed the same day as a major (090-day) procedure like a scheduled hernia repair or lap chole; that E/M is included in the global surgical package unless it is a significant, separately identifiable service unrelated to the reason for the procedure, and payers scrutinize that scenario heavily. Once a major procedure's global period is underway, an unrelated E/M from the same surgeon needs modifier 24 instead — the full modifier decision tree, including a worked timeline against a real post-op window, is in our guide to modifier 58 vs. 78 vs. 79 during the global surgical period.

The decision-visit rule matters specifically for major surgery: when the decision for major surgery is made the day of, or the day before, the surgery itself, that E/M is separately billable with modifier 57 (decision for surgery), not bundled into the global package. Confusing 25 and 57 on a pre-op visit is a common, easily fixed charge-entry error — 57 applies to major (090-day) procedures, 25 applies to minor (000/010-day) procedures, and using the wrong one on the wrong global-period length is a frequent source of a bundled, non-payable E/M line.

Time-based leveling still has a role once a visit runs long. Prolonged service add-on code 99417 (each additional 15 minutes beyond the minimum time of the primary E/M code, for non-Medicare payers using the CPT time thresholds) or HCPCS G2212 (the Medicare-specific equivalent, keyed to CPT's own prolonged-service time thresholds) apply when a complex pre-op consult or a difficult post-op complication visit genuinely runs past the top time threshold of the base E/M code. These are frequently left unbilled entirely, not because the payer would deny them, but because nobody on staff is tracking total visit time against the threshold in the first place — a workflow gap, not a coding one.

Discharge day management (99238 for 30 minutes or less, 99239 for more than 30 minutes) is billed by the attending surgeon for the final in-person visit on the day a hospitalized surgical patient is discharged, separate from and in addition to the surgical global package — the global period covers routine post-op care, but a hospital discharge visit is its own billable service with its own time documentation requirement. Practices that bundle discharge management into “just part of the post-op course” are leaving a legitimately separate line unbilled on every inpatient surgical case.

Core procedure family: gallbladder and biliary surgery

Laparoscopic cholecystectomy is general surgery's single highest-volume major procedure, and its three core codes form a strict hierarchy that should never be billed together on the same date of service.

47562, 47563, and 47564 describe increasing levels of the same laparoscopic procedure — if a cholangiogram was performed, bill 47563, not 47562 plus a separate imaging code; if the common duct was explored, bill 47564, which subsumes the cholangiography work. These three never appear together on one claim for one gallbladder; the operative note determines which single code represents the highest level of service actually performed, and that's the only one that goes on the claim.

47562 carries a Medically Unlikely Edit of 1 unit per date of service — a patient has one gallbladder, so any claim reporting more than one unit is clinically implausible on its face and is commonly reported as carrying MAI 2, meaning it's an absolute date-of-service edit with no appeal path. That's not a documentation problem to solve; it's almost always a charge-entry duplication to catch before submission, not after denial.

The open cholecystectomy code, 47600, is reportable on its own when the surgeon planned an open approach from the start, or when a laparoscopic case converts to open intraoperatively. Conversion billing is where this family gets misbilled most often, and it's covered in full, code-level detail — including the documentation threshold that decides whether the open or laparoscopic code is the reportable one — in our guide to laparoscopic vs. open procedure coding and the conversion billing rule. The short version: never bill both the laparoscopic and open code for the same gallbladder on the same date; the op note's documented extent of the case, not the instruments the case started with, determines which single code is reportable.

Medical necessity for cholecystectomy tracks the K80 cholelithiasis family closely, and the axis that matters is cholecystitis status and obstruction, not just “gallstones.” Confirmed live against the FY2026 ICD-10-CM code set: K80.00 (calculus of gallbladder with acute cholecystitis, without obstruction), K80.01 (same, with obstruction), K80.10/K80.11 (chronic cholecystitis, without/with obstruction), K80.12/K80.13 (acute and chronic combined, without/with obstruction), and K80.20/K80.21 (calculus without cholecystitis, without/with obstruction) are all billable, HIPAA-valid codes. A claim carrying only a symptom code — abdominal pain without a confirmed calculus or cholecystitis diagnosis — routinely fails medical necessity review for a cholecystectomy where the confirmed, specific K80 code would pass; imaging (ultrasound, HIDA scan) should be in the chart supporting whichever specific K80 code is billed, not just referenced.

Choledocholithiasis and the ERCP interaction. When a common bile duct stone is identified pre-operatively or on intra-operative cholangiogram, many patients undergo ERCP with sphincterotomy and stone extraction either before or after the cholecystectomy, sometimes same admission, sometimes same day. If gastroenterology performs the ERCP and the surgeon performs the lap chole as two genuinely separate procedures by two separate practitioners, both are billed independently with no modifier conflict. The edit risk shows up when the same surgeon performs an intra-operative common duct exploration as part of 47564 and a separate ERCP is also billed the same date — that combination needs a clear operative narrative distinguishing the laparoscopic duct exploration from the endoscopic one, because payers read overlapping duct-clearance procedures on the same date as a probable duplicate absent that distinction.

Cholangiogram billing when it isn't part of the same session. An intra-operative cholangiogram performed as part of the cholecystectomy itself is what moves the code from 47562 to 47563 — it is never billed as a separate imaging code on top of 47562. A cholangiogram performed as a distinct, separately scheduled procedure (for instance, a post-operative T-tube cholangiogram days after the original surgery) is a different clinical scenario entirely and is billed under the radiology code family for cholangiography, not folded into the surgical code retroactively. Confusing these two scenarios — billing 47562 plus a separate cholangiogram code for one same-session study — is a bundling error that denies as a duplicate service.

What the chart needs to show before scheduling. Payers reviewing a cholecystectomy claim for medical necessity are generally looking for two things together, not either alone: a clinical picture consistent with biliary colic or cholecystitis (right upper quadrant or epigastric pain, often meal-related, sometimes with fever, nausea, or a positive Murphy's sign on exam), and imaging confirmation — typically right upper quadrant ultrasound showing cholelithiasis, gallbladder wall thickening, or pericholecystic fluid, or a HIDA scan showing a low or absent ejection fraction for suspected biliary dyskinesia without visible stones. A claim built on imaging alone, without a documented clinical presentation, or on symptoms alone without imaging confirmation, is a weaker medical necessity claim than one carrying both, and it's worth a pre-op documentation check rather than assuming the surgeon's clinical judgment will carry the claim on its own.

Failed or incomplete cholangiogram. If the surgeon attempts a cholangiogram but cannot successfully complete it — cystic duct too small to cannulate, contrast extravasation — and reverts to reporting the base procedure only, modifier 52 (reduced services) on 47562 is the more defensible claim than reporting 47563 for an attempted-but-incomplete study; the operative note has to state explicitly that the cholangiogram was attempted and why it wasn't completed; a note that's silent on the attempt supports only the base code with no modifier.

Core procedure family: hernia repair

Hernia repair is coded by anatomic location first, then by clinical status — reducible, incarcerated, or strangulated — and by whether the repair is initial or recurrent. Getting any one of those three axes wrong on the CPT code, or failing to carry the matching axis on the ICD-10 code, is the most common source of hernia-repair denials.

A sliding inguinal hernia is reported with add-on code 49525 alongside the base inguinal repair code, not as a stand-alone procedure. Mesh implantation for open incisional or ventral hernia repair is reported with add-on code 49568, appended to 49560/49561/49565/49566 — mesh is not separately billable with the inguinal or umbilical families the same way, because mesh placement is already bundled into the base valuation of most inguinal repair codes. The mesh product itself is a separate billing question from the placement code: in a hospital outpatient or ASC setting, the facility bills the mesh device supply cost separately under its own HCPCS C-code as part of the facility claim, while the physician's professional claim (49568 or the base repair code alone) reflects only the surgical work of placing it — the surgeon's office does not separately bill for the physical mesh product itself in the facility setting, and conflating the two when reconciling a case's total reimbursement is a common source of confusion between the professional and facility billing teams on the same case. The full code set including laparoscopic inguinal (49650/49651) and laparoscopic ventral/incisional repair codes, mesh reporting rules by approach, and a complete decision tree by clinical status is in our hernia repair CPT codes guide covering inguinal, umbilical, and ventral repair.

NCCI bundles lysis of adhesions into the primary hernia repair code under most circumstances — freeing adhesions to reach or reduce the hernia contents is expected work, not a separately payable service. It's only reportable separately when the adhesiolysis is extensive, unrelated in location to the hernia repair itself, and documented as adding materially to operative time; the modifier and documentation threshold for defending that split, along with the specific pairs that most often trigger the edit in this specialty, are covered in our guide to NCCI bundling traps in general surgery and what modifier 59 actually fixes. The same edit logic applies when a hernia repair is performed through a separate incision during the same operative session as a lap chole — NCCI generally allows both to be billed with the appropriate modifier when the incisions and pathology are genuinely distinct, but a bundling denial on this specific pairing is common enough that it's worth checking the current PTP indicator before submitting rather than after a denial.

Diagnosis specificity here is unusually granular and directly payer-relevant. Confirmed live against the FY2026 ICD-10-CM code set: the K40 inguinal hernia family carries separate billable codes for bilateral vs. unilateral, obstruction-without-gangrene vs. gangrene vs. neither, and initial vs. recurrent — for example K40.90 (unilateral, without obstruction or gangrene, not specified as recurrent) versus K40.31 (unilateral, with obstruction, without gangrene, recurrent) are both valid, billable codes describing very different clinical and surgical pictures. The K42 umbilical family (K42.0 with obstruction, K42.1 with gangrene, K42.9 without either) and the K43 ventral/incisional family — which separately distinguishes incisional (K43.0/K43.1/K43.2), parastomal (K43.3/K43.4/K43.5), and other/unspecified ventral (K43.6/K43.7/K43.9) — follow the same obstruction/gangrene logic. K46.9, unspecified abdominal hernia without obstruction or gangrene, is billable but should be a last resort: it doesn't identify location, and a location-specific code almost always exists and almost always supports the CPT code better.

Bilateral repair billing. Bilateral inguinal hernia repair performed in the same operative session is reported with the unilateral base code plus modifier 50, not with the code reported twice on separate lines — most payers price a modifier-50 bilateral claim at a percentage of the unilateral rate rather than double it outright, so verify the specific payer's bilateral pricing rule rather than assuming 200%. For a unilateral repair, appending LT or RT to identify the side operated on is not universally required by CPT itself, but a meaningful number of commercial payers and some MACs do require it on unilateral hernia claims to support medical necessity review against a laterality-specific diagnosis code (K40.90 for the right side and a separate future K40.90 claim for the left side, for instance, are otherwise indistinguishable on the claim without it) — check payer policy before assuming it's optional.

Recurrent hernia after mesh. A hernia recurring at a site where mesh was previously placed carries real coding and documentation weight beyond just the recurrent CPT code. The operative note should describe whether the recurrence is a true mesh failure, a new hernia adjacent to the prior repair, or a mesh-related complication (infection, erosion, chronic pain) requiring mesh removal rather than a straightforward re-repair — each of those is a different procedure and a different medical necessity story, and “recurrent hernia” alone as the only documented finding under-describes what's actually being treated and billed.

Incarcerated versus strangulated, and why the distinction changes the urgency story on the claim. An incarcerated hernia is one that cannot be manually reduced back through the fascial defect but still has intact blood supply; a strangulated hernia has compromised blood supply to the incarcerated contents and is a surgical emergency. Both support the higher-complexity incarcerated/strangulated CPT code family (49507, 49521, 49561, 49566, and the umbilical equivalent 49587), but the clinical urgency documented for a strangulated repair — emergent presentation, evidence of ischemia or bowel compromise on exam or imaging — supports both the CPT code and, separately, the case for bypassing a prior-authorization requirement that would otherwise apply to an elective repair. A note that says only “incarcerated hernia repaired” without stating whether reduction was attempted and failed, or whether strangulation was suspected or confirmed, under-documents the urgency the CPT code itself is claiming.

What isn't a covered hernia repair. Athletic pubalgia (“sports hernia”) does not involve a true fascial defect the way an inguinal hernia does, and most payers, Medicare included, do not recognize standard hernia repair codes as appropriate for it absent a confirmed anatomic hernia on exam or imaging — billing 49505 for a sports-medicine pubalgia repair without a documented true hernia is a common source of both denial and, on audit, a coding-accuracy finding. If a true occult hernia is found and repaired in that context, the diagnosis and operative findings need to establish the actual hernia, not just groin pain in an athlete.

Core procedure family: appendectomy and bowel procedures

Appendectomy splits along two axes that decide the code: open versus laparoscopic, and primary versus incidental.

The trap that costs the most money in this family is incidental appendectomy. NCCI policy is explicit: removing a grossly normal appendix incidentally during another abdominal procedure — a hysterectomy, a colectomy, another laparoscopic case — is not separately reportable at all, laparoscopic or open. 44970 specifically may not be billed for an incidental removal; it's reserved for a diseased appendix, and the pathology or the operative note's gross description has to support that the appendix was actually diseased, not just “removed while we were in there.” Billing an incidental appendectomy as if it were a primary procedure is one of the more reliably audited patterns in general surgery, precisely because it's easy for a payer to catch by comparing the primary procedure code, the appendectomy code, and the diagnosis codes on the same claim.

Interval appendectomy and non-operative management. A patient presenting with a well-formed periappendiceal abscess or phlegmon is frequently managed initially with antibiotics and percutaneous drainage rather than immediate appendectomy, with a planned “interval” appendectomy performed weeks later once the acute inflammation has resolved. That interval procedure is billed as its own new encounter under the appropriate appendectomy code (44950 or 44970, depending on approach), with its own diagnosis coding reflecting the current presentation at the time of the interval surgery — it is not connected to the original admission's global period or billed as a staged procedure off the original (non-surgical) encounter, because the index encounter never involved a billable surgical global package to stage against. Confirm the diagnosis code on the interval procedure reflects the appendix's status at that later surgery, not a copy-forward of the original acute presentation's code. The percutaneous abscess drainage itself, when performed by interventional radiology as the initial non-operative step, is billed under its own image-guided drainage code family, separate from and unrelated to whichever appendectomy code is eventually billed at the interval surgery weeks later — the two procedures, performed by two different practitioners at two different encounters, are not a bundling question at all, but claims staff sometimes flag them as a suspected duplicate simply because both involve "the appendix" in the diagnosis history. A brief note in the claim's remarks explaining the staged, non-operative-then-operative treatment course heads off that confusion before it becomes a manual review delay.

Diagnosis specificity for appendicitis is granular enough that it changes the supportable CPT code, not just the payer's confidence in medical necessity. Confirmed live against the FY2026 ICD-10-CM code set: K35.80 (unspecified acute appendicitis) is billable but generic; the specific alternatives — K35.20/K35.200/K35.201 (acute appendicitis with generalized peritonitis, without/with perforation), K35.21/K35.210/K35.211 (same, with abscess, without/with perforation), and K35.30/K35.31/K35.32/K35.33 (localized peritonitis, with gangrene and/or perforation and/or abscess in escalating combinations) — each map to a materially different clinical picture. A claim reporting 44960 (complicated open appendectomy for rupture with abscess or generalized peritonitis) against a diagnosis of simple K35.80 unspecified appendicitis is a mismatch a payer's edit logic can flag on its face; the diagnosis code needs to carry the same complexity the CPT code claims.

Colectomy coding sits on a much wider spectrum than appendectomy or hernia repair — partial colectomy (44140–44147 family, open) and its laparoscopic counterparts (44204–44208 family) vary by anastomosis technique, stoma creation, and extent of resection.

Selecting among these codes correctly depends entirely on the operative note stating what happened to both ends of the resected bowel — reconnected, brought out as a stoma with the distal end closed and left in place, or brought out as a mucofistula. A coder cannot infer this from “colon resection performed” alone; if the note doesn't state the fate of both bowel ends explicitly, query the surgeon before coding rather than guessing at the highest- or lowest-complexity option. Diverticulitis, the most common indication for elective and urgent colectomy in this specialty, is coded under the K57 family, which carries its own perforation/abscess/peritonitis/bleeding specificity axis parallel to the K35 appendicitis logic above — an unspecified diverticulitis code against a Hartmann-type procedure for perforated disease is exactly the kind of CPT-to-ICD-10 complexity mismatch that triggers medical necessity review.

Stoma closure (takedown), typically billed several months after the index procedure once the patient has recovered, uses its own distinct code family (44625/44626 for closure of enterostomy with resection and anastomosis) rather than reusing the original colectomy code — it's a materially different, separately billable procedure with its own global period, not a continuation of the original surgery's global package, since the original 090-day global period has long since closed by the time takedown is scheduled.

The two NCCI rules that apply consistently across this entire family: match the specific technique documented in the operative note (not just “colon resection”) to the correct base code, and check the edit between the colectomy code and any concurrent lysis of adhesions or incidental appendectomy the same way you would for the appendectomy family above — a normal appendix removed incidentally during a colectomy is bundled under the same NCCI logic as a normal appendix removed during any other abdominal procedure.

Secondary diagnosis codes that support medical necessity indirectly. Two categories of secondary code carry real weight in general surgery even though they're never the primary diagnosis on the claim. Modifiers every general surgery biller must own

General surgery leans on a wider modifier set than most specialties because so many of its procedures are major, 090-day-global operations with real post-op complexity.

22 — increased procedural services
  • Reserved for a procedure that took substantially more work than the code's typical case — dense adhesions, significant blood loss, major anatomic distortion — not for a routinely difficult case.
  • Documentation has to quantify the increased complexity (extra time, specific complicating factors, comparison to the typical case) for the payer to consider a 20–50% payment increase; a note that just says “difficult case” without specifics typically gets 22 stripped and the claim paid at the standard rate.
50 / 51
  • 50 — bilateral procedure, when a code doesn't already describe bilateral in its own definition (e.g., bilateral inguinal hernia repair performed same session on two separate hernias).
  • 51 — multiple procedures on the same date, applied by the payer's system to reduce reimbursement on secondary procedures; don't append it yourself unless your specific payer requires manual application, since most process it automatically. Note that CPT designates certain codes as modifier-51 exempt — add-on codes by definition (11045–11047, 44955, 49568) are never subject to the multiple-procedure reduction, because they're already valued as incremental work on top of a primary code, not as a stand-alone procedure competing for reduction against it.

  • Overrides an NCCI bundling edit only where the edit's modifier indicator allows an override at all, and only when the record shows a genuinely distinct service — separate site, separate session, separate structure.

Assistant-at-surgery modifiers — 80 (assistant surgeon), 81 (minimum assistant surgeon), 82 (assistant surgeon when a qualified resident is not available), and AS (non-physician assistant at surgery, for PA/NP/CNS) — carry specific and frequently misunderstood payment rules. Under Medicare, a physician assistant billed with 80/81/82 is paid 16% of the fee schedule amount that applies to the primary surgeon for that procedure; AS is paid 85% of that same 16% rate, roughly 13.6% of the surgeon's allowed amount. Whether an assistant is payable for a given code at all depends on that code's assistant-at-surgery payment indicator in the Medicare Physician Fee Schedule Database — an indicator of 0 means payment is restricted unless supporting documentation establishes medical necessity for an assistant (CMS sets this based on whether more than 5% of national claims for that procedure actually use one), 1 means assistants are never separately payable by statute, and 2 means payment is permitted without additional justification. Full detail on every payment indicator, the documentation that clears an indicator-0 procedure, and how AS differs from 80/81/82 in practice is in our assistant surgeon modifiers guide.

Worked example, assistant surgeon payment. A community general surgeon serves as first assistant on a complex incisional hernia repair with mesh, billed under modifier 82 because no qualified resident was available at the facility that day. If the primary surgeon's allowed amount for the procedure under the current fee schedule is $1,000, the assisting surgeon is paid 16% of that, or $160, appended with modifier 82 on their own claim under their own NPI — not a percentage split negotiated between the two surgeons, and not billable at all if the procedure's payment indicator restricts assistant-at-surgery without supporting documentation. If a PA had assisted instead, billed with AS, the payment would be 85% of that same 16% rate, or about $136. Both assistant claims require their own documentation of the assistant's specific role in the operative note; a note that only names the assistant without describing what they did is a common reason an otherwise-payable assistant claim gets denied on review.

Worked example, modifier 22. A planned laparoscopic cholecystectomy encounters a frozen abdomen from prior surgery, unrelated to the gallbladder, requiring two additional hours of lysis of dense adhesions before the gallbladder can even be visualized, plus a documented estimated blood loss triple the typical case. The operative note states the additional time, describes the specific complicating finding (dense adhesions from a prior unrelated surgery, not routine anatomy), and quantifies the blood loss. That claim supports modifier 22 with a reasonable expectation of a 20–50% payment increase on review. Contrast that with a note that says only “difficult dissection, longer than usual” with no specifics — payers routinely strip 22 and pay the standard rate on a note like that, because there's nothing to adjudicate the increase against.

NCCI edits, bundling, and MUE limits

This is the section that decides whether a bundled or capped code is billable at all, and it's the one competitor guides in this niche either skip or gesture at without numbers. Two values control the outcome, and neither is a guess.

The NCCI modifier indicator attached to a Column 1/Column 2 code pair:

  • 0Never bypassable. No modifier overrides it under any documentation. If the pair carries a 0, the Column 2 code simply isn't separately payable.
  • 1Bypassable with documentation. An NCCI-associated modifier (59 or a specific X-modifier) can override the edit, but only where the record shows the second service was genuinely distinct.
  • 9Edit deleted. The pair no longer applies.

The MUE Adjudication Indicator (MAI) attached to a per-code, per-day unit cap:

  • 1Claim-line edit. Units above the cap deny that line, but a genuinely repeated service can sometimes be split across lines with the correct modifier and documentation.
  • 2Absolute, date-of-service edit. Exceeding it is treated as clinically implausible. No appeal path exists — confirm the MAI before spending staff time on one.
  • 3Appealable, date-of-service edit. A real appeal path exists with documentation the excess units reflect genuinely distinct, medically necessary services.

Reading an NCCI pair correctly means knowing which code is Column 1 (the comprehensive, generally higher-value code) and which is Column 2 (the component code that's bundled into it) — the edit runs in one direction, and the modifier indicator governs whether the Column 2 code is separately payable when it's reported alongside the Column 1 code, not the reverse. A lysis-of-adhesions code reported as Column 2 against a cholecystectomy Column 1 code means the adhesiolysis is bundled into the cholecystectomy; it does not mean the reverse pairing (cholecystectomy against a different Column 1 code) carries the same rule. Look up the specific pair in the direction you're billing it, not just by code number in isolation.

Where these show up most in general surgery: 47562/47563/47564 form a hierarchy rather than a bundling pair, so the fix is charge-entry discipline (bill only the highest level performed), not a modifier. Lysis of adhesions performed to reach the primary pathology is commonly reported as bundled into the primary abdominal procedure under an indicator that does not permit routine override; it's separately reportable only with documentation of genuinely extensive, unrelated adhesiolysis. Debridement and primary wound closure performed the same session on the same wound are frequently bundled — if the wound is closed primarily the same encounter, the debridement work is usually included in the closure code's valuation rather than separately payable, and billing both without a clear modifier-supported distinction between debrided tissue and the closure site is a common denial pattern.

On unit ceilings specifically: 47562 is commonly reported as carrying an MUE of 1 unit per date of service, consistent with removing one gallbladder. The debridement family (11042–11047) is commonly reported at 1 unit per day for each of the primary codes (11042 subcutaneous, 11043 muscle/fascia, 11044 bone), with the add-on codes 11045–11047 tracking additional 20 sq cm increments beyond the first — the practical implication is that a large wound gets coded as one primary code plus however many add-on units the total area actually supports, not as multiple units of the primary code itself. The hernia repair and appendectomy families carry their own MUE ceilings for the same anatomic reason: most base hernia repair codes are commonly reported at 1 unit per date of service per side, meaning a genuinely bilateral repair needs modifier 50 on a single line rather than two units of the same code, and 44950/44970 are commonly reported at 1 unit per date of service, consistent with a patient having a single appendix.

The global surgical package: 90-day, 10-day, and 0-day rules

General surgery's core procedures — gallbladder, hernia, appendectomy — carry a 090-day global period. Debridement (11042–11047) carries a 000-day global. The global package bundles the pre-op visit the day of or day before surgery (except when modifier 57 applies to a major-surgery decision visit), the procedure itself, and routine post-op care for the length of the global period — it does not bundle a complication requiring a return to the OR, an unrelated procedure, or care for an unrelated condition, which is exactly where 58, 78, and 79 come in.

58 — staged or related
  • Planned prospectively at the time of the first procedure, or more extensive than the first, or for therapy following a diagnostic surgical procedure. Starts a new global period.
78 — unplanned related, return to OR
  • Unplanned return to the operating or procedure room by the same surgeon, for a complication related to the original surgery, during its global period. Paid at a reduced, intraoperative-only rate. Does not start a new global period.
79 — unrelated procedure
  • A different procedure, unrelated to the original, by the same surgeon, during the original procedure's global period. Paid at the full rate. Starts its own new global period.

Worked timeline — a patient who underwent 47562 laparoscopic cholecystectomy (090-day global, day 0 of the period):

  • Day 15Patient returns with new-onset right lower quadrant pain, unrelated to the chole, and is diagnosed with acute appendicitis. Same surgeon performs 44970 laparoscopic appendectomy. Bill with modifier 79 — unrelated procedure, same surgeon, during the original global period. Paid at the full rate; this procedure opens its own new 090-day global period.
  • Day 20Patient develops a post-operative bile leak requiring an unplanned return to the OR for washout and drain placement, performed by the same surgeon. Bill with modifier 78 — unplanned, related to the original surgery, return to the OR. Paid at the reduced intraoperative-only rate; no new global period starts, and the original 090-day clock continues running.
  • Day 35As part of managing that complication, the surgeon had planned at the time of the washout to bring the patient back for a second, more extensive planned procedure. That second procedure is billed with modifier 58 — staged or related, planned prospectively at the time of the prior procedure. It starts its own new global period.

Minor-procedure 010-day globals follow the same logic at a smaller scale. Incision and drainage of a superficial abscess, a fairly common general surgery office procedure, typically carries a 010-day global rather than 000 or 090 — the same 58/78/79 framework applies within that shorter window: an unplanned return for a re-accumulated abscess related to the original I&D within the 10 days is modifier 78, an unrelated minor procedure on the same patient during that window is modifier 79, and a planned second-stage procedure is modifier 58. The mechanics don't change with the length of the global period; only the number of days the clock runs for does.

Zero-day-global procedures matter too, even though they're shorter. Debridement (11042–11047) carries a 000-day global, meaning only the day of the procedure itself is bundled — there is no post-op period to navigate the way there is with a 090-day major procedure, so a follow-up debridement two days later on the same wound is simply a new, separately billable service on its own date, not a global-period question at all. The 58/78/79 modifier set is a 090-day (and, less commonly in this specialty, 010-day) global concern specifically; applying it to a 000-day debridement code is a modifier error in the other direction — there's no global period there to bill around.

The documentation line that decides each modifier on audit is the same one every time: the operative note or progress note has to state explicitly whether the return was planned in advance, whether it required a return to the OR, and whether it was related or unrelated to the original diagnosis and procedure. A note that just describes what was done, without stating the relationship to the prior surgery, leaves the modifier choice unsupportable regardless of which one was actually billed. Colonoscopy: the screening-to-diagnostic conversion trap. Many general surgery practices perform their own endoscopy, and the single most common colonoscopy billing error isn't a coding-family question at all — it's failing to correctly convert a screening colonoscopy to a diagnostic one when a polyp is found and removed. Under Medicare, a screening colonoscopy that becomes therapeutic (polypectomy performed) is billed with the diagnostic/therapeutic colonoscopy CPT code plus modifier PT, which waives the patient's deductible the same way a preventive screening would have, even though the code itself is now the diagnostic one; commercial and ACA-marketplace plans use modifier 33 (preventive service) for the same purpose under different regulatory authority. Billing the diagnostic code without the appropriate modifier converts what should have been a no-cost-share preventive encounter into a claim with patient cost-sharing attached, which generates patient complaints and rework even when the underlying CPT code itself was correct. The three confirmed colonoscopy LCDs above — Noridian's L34213, CGS's L34005, and Palmetto's L34454 — each define their jurisdiction's specific covered indications and frequency limits for diagnostic and therapeutic colonoscopy; frequency edits (how soon a repeat colonoscopy is covered after a prior one) are a common denial point when a patient's next colonoscopy is scheduled without checking the interval their specific MAC's policy actually allows.

Robotic-assisted approaches to standard general surgery procedures — robotic cholecystectomy, robotic inguinal hernia repair — are billed to the underlying open or laparoscopic CPT code that describes the procedure actually performed; there is no separate CPT code family for “robotic” as an approach in most of general surgery's core procedures the way there is in some other specialties, so the code selection logic in the sections above applies regardless of whether the case was performed hand-held laparoscopic or robotically assisted. Where robotic approach does matter operationally is prior authorization: a meaningful number of commercial payers apply step-therapy-style logic requiring documentation that a standard laparoscopic approach was considered or is not appropriate before authorizing a robotic case, treating robotic assistance as a coverage question even though it isn't a distinct billing code — confirm the specific payer's stance before scheduling a robotic case on the assumption that authorization will mirror the laparoscopic pathway.

Commercial payers diverge from Medicare and from each other most visibly on cosmetic-versus-functional hernia repair. A ventral or umbilical hernia repair performed for a genuinely symptomatic, functionally impairing hernia is a covered surgical service across essentially every payer; the same repair framed primarily around appearance, without documented functional symptoms (pain, obstruction risk, activity limitation), risks a cosmetic-exclusion denial from commercial plans that Medicare's coverage rules don't even address the same way, because Medicare's medical necessity framework doesn't carry a parallel cosmetic-exclusion clause for hernia repair the way many commercial policies do. Document functional impact explicitly in the pre-op note — pain with activity, risk of incarceration, interference with daily function — regardless of payer, because it's the difference between a clean approval and a cosmetic-exclusion fight on a commercial plan.

Site of service payment differentials matter more in general surgery than the billing side of a practice sometimes tracks. The same lap chole or hernia repair reimburses differently depending on whether it's performed in a hospital outpatient department, a freestanding ambulatory surgery center, or, for the smaller procedures in this specialty like debridement, an office setting — because facility and non-facility practice expense RVUs are calculated separately, and because ASC and HOPD payment for the facility fee itself runs through entirely separate payment systems from the physician's professional fee. A practice that shifted volume to an ASC for cost reasons without re-verifying professional-fee payment at that site of service can be surprised by a lower per-case professional payment than the same case would have generated in a hospital OR, even though the CPT code billed is identical.

Medicaid managed care adds a layer most general surgery practices underestimate. State Medicaid fee-for-service programs often mirror Medicare's coverage logic reasonably closely, but the managed care organizations that administer Medicaid benefits in most states run their own prior-auth lists, their own medical necessity criteria, and frequently their own bundling logic on top of whatever the state's base Medicaid program requires — treating a Medicaid MCO like straight Medicaid fee-for-service is a common and avoidable source of unexpected denials on elective general surgery cases.

Prior authorization in general surgery

Original Medicare does not require blanket prior authorization for most general surgery procedures — elective hernia repair, cholecystectomy, and appendectomy (which is rarely elective in the first place) typically proceed without a Medicare PA requirement. Medicare Advantage plans layer their own utilization management on top of that baseline and frequently do require prior auth for the same elective procedures Original Medicare doesn't gate; verify against the specific MA plan, not against Original Medicare's rules, whenever the patient carries an MA product. Commercial payers vary payer to payer and change their prior-auth lists without much notice, so a verification done for last year's plan year isn't a safe assumption for this one.

Authorization validity windows matter as much as getting the authorization in the first place. A prior authorization approved for a specific procedure and date range doesn't necessarily survive a schedule change — if surgery is rescheduled outside the authorization's approved window, or if the operative plan changes intraoperatively (a planned laparoscopic repair converts to open, or an inguinal repair reveals a second hernia requiring an additional code), the original authorization may not cover the claim as actually billed. Build a step into scheduling that checks the authorization's approved date range and approved CPT codes against what's actually being billed before the claim goes out, not after a CO-197 denial reveals the gap.

Elective, non-urgent hernia repair is the pattern worth building a standing workflow around: because it's genuinely elective in most presentations (reducible, non-incarcerated), it's exactly the kind of case a commercial or MA plan is most likely to require authorization for, while an incarcerated or strangulated repair performed urgently is far less likely to be gated the same way, because most payers' own policies exempt urgent and emergent surgical care from standard prior-auth timelines. Build the verification step around clinical urgency, not around the CPT code alone — the same code can carry a very different authorization requirement depending on whether the operative note documents an elective or urgent presentation.

Bariatric surgery is the major exception, and it's gated by documentation, not just an authorization number. NCD 100.1 (Bariatric Surgery for Treatment of Co-Morbid Conditions Related to Morbid Obesity), confirmed directly against CMS's coverage database, sets three conditions that must all be met for Medicare coverage of open or laparoscopic Roux-en-Y gastric bypass, open or laparoscopic biliopancreatic diversion with duodenal switch, and laparoscopic adjustable gastric banding:

  • 1Body-mass index of 35 kg/m² or greater, documented and calculable from recorded height and weight in the chart.
  • 2At least one obesity-related comorbidity — type 2 diabetes has qualified since February 12, 2009; cardiac and respiratory conditions and hypertension are also commonly qualifying, and the specific comorbidity has to be documented and linked to the obesity, not just listed elsewhere in the chart.
  • 3Documented, unsuccessful prior medical treatment for obesity — the record needs to show what was tried (diet, medication, structured weight-management program) and that it didn't achieve the needed result, not just a note that the patient “has tried to lose weight.”

Two frequently outdated assumptions to correct in internal checklists: facility certification as an American College of Surgeons Level 1 Bariatric Surgery Center or a Bariatric Surgery Center of Excellence is no longer required for dates of service on or after September 24, 2013 — if your prior-auth checklist still asks for a center-of-excellence attestation, it's asking for something CMS stopped requiring over a decade ago. And stand-alone laparoscopic sleeve gastrectomy is not nationally covered outright; coverage is left to individual MAC discretion under specific conditions (BMI ≥35, a qualifying comorbidity, and documented prior treatment failure), so confirm your MAC's current position on sleeve gastrectomy specifically before scheduling one, rather than assuming it's covered the same way RYGBP is. Build that verification into scheduling as its own distinct step, separate from the standard three-condition checklist — a patient can meet BMI, comorbidity, and prior-treatment-failure criteria cleanly and still have the specific procedure denied if the local MAC hasn't extended discretionary coverage to stand-alone sleeve gastrectomy in that jurisdiction. The following procedures remain nationally non-covered for all Medicare beneficiaries regardless of documentation: open adjustable gastric banding, open sleeve gastrectomy, open and laparoscopic vertical banded gastroplasty, intestinal bypass surgery, and gastric balloon for obesity treatment. Pre-bill review checklist

Most of the denials covered in this guide are catchable before submission, not just appealable after. This is the check sequence worth building into charge review for any 090-day major general surgery claim.

  • 1Does the ICD-10 code carry every axis the CPT code implies? Laterality, obstruction/gangrene status, and recurrence for hernia; complexity and perforation/abscess status for appendicitis; cholecystitis and obstruction status for gallbladder disease. If the CPT code claims a complication or recurrence, the diagnosis code needs to say so too.
  • 2Is this claim inside another procedure's global period? If yes, does the operative or progress note state explicitly whether it's planned (58), an unplanned related return to the OR (78), or unrelated (79) — and does the modifier on the claim actually match what the note says?
  • 3Does this code pair trigger an NCCI edit? Check the modifier indicator before appending 59 or an X-modifier, and confirm the documentation supports a genuinely distinct service if an override is even possible.
  • 4Are the units billed within the code's MUE? Especially on 47562, the hernia repair family without modifier 50 support, and the debridement base codes.
  • 5Does a coverage policy apply, and is it cited by document number? Wound care, colonoscopy, and bariatric claims in particular should reference the specific MAC's LCD or the applicable NCD by number, not a generic medical-necessity statement.
  • 6If an assistant or a mesh device is involved, is the corresponding documentation and code present? An assistant's specific role belongs in the operative note, not just their name in the case log; a mesh device belongs on the facility claim under its own supply code, separate from the physician's placement code.

None of these six checks require specialized software or a coding certification to run — they require someone on the team reading the operative note against the claim before it goes out, with this list next to them, rather than trusting that charge entry and coding caught everything upstream. Practices that build this review into a standing pre-bill step, even a five-minute pass on the highest-dollar cases only, consistently see fewer of the denials covered throughout this guide reach an appeal queue in the first place.

Conclusion and next steps

Three fixes account for most of the recoverable revenue in a general surgery claims stream. First, ICD-10 specificity: carry the laterality, obstruction, gangrene, and recurrence axis every time the chart supports it, because that's what separates a passing medical necessity review from a CO-50 denial on an otherwise correctly billed procedure. Second, the correct global-period modifier: 58, 78, and 79 pay differently and read differently to an auditor, and the documentation line that supports each one has to state the relationship to the original procedure explicitly. Third, MAC-specific LCD citation: “per CMS” doesn't hold up when five different MACs each publish their own wound care and debridement coverage article with different effective dates — know which MAC your practice falls under and cite that document, not a generic search result.

None of this replaces a working relationship with the payers your practice actually bills, and none of it substitutes for reviewing the current-year code set and coverage documents before your own claims go out. If your denial rate on gallbladder, hernia, or appendectomy claims is running above what a clean-claim benchmark should look like, the pattern is almost always traceable to one of the three fixes above.

Frequently asked questions

When do we bill 47600 instead of 47562 after a lap chole converts to open?

Bill the open code, 47600, only when the operative note documents that the majority of the dissection was actually completed using open technique after conversion — the coding convention billers use is that more than half the case has to have been carried out open before the open code becomes the reportable one. If the surgeon converted early, after only a brief laparoscopic attempt, most of the real surgical work was still done laparoscopically even though the incision closed open, and the laparoscopic code with modifier 22 for increased complexity is the more defensible claim. Never report both codes for the same gallbladder — they are mutually exclusive on the same date of service. The determining factor is documented extent of the procedure, not which instruments started the case.

Why does K40.90 get denied when K40.31 doesn't?

K40.90 is unilateral inguinal hernia without obstruction or gangrene, not specified as recurrent — it tells the payer almost nothing about surgical urgency or complexity. K40.31 is unilateral inguinal hernia with obstruction, without gangrene, recurrent, and it directly supports both medical necessity for surgical repair and the higher-complexity recurrent-hernia CPT code. Payers increasingly cross-check the ICD-10 code against the CPT code billed: if the surgeon reports a recurrent hernia repair code but the diagnosis code doesn't carry the recurrent axis, or reports it as an incarcerated repair without an obstruction or gangrene axis on the diagnosis, the mismatch is grounds for a medical necessity denial even though the correct surgery was performed.

What's the real difference between modifier 78 and 79 during a global period?

Modifier 78 is for an unplanned return to the operating or procedure room, by the same surgeon, for a complication related to the original surgery, during its global period — it pays at a reduced, intraoperative-only rate and does not start a new global period. Modifier 79 is for a procedure that is unrelated to the original surgery, performed by the same surgeon during the original procedure's global period — it pays at the full rate and starts its own new global period. The distinction that actually decides which one applies is relatedness to the original diagnosis and procedure, not how soon after the first surgery the second one happens.

Do we need prior authorization for elective hernia repair?

Original Medicare does not require prior authorization for the great majority of general surgery procedures, including elective hernia repair, though Medicare Advantage plans layer their own utilization management on top and frequently do require it. Commercial payers vary widely and change their prior-auth lists without much notice — verify against the specific plan's current list before scheduling, not against what that payer required last year. Bariatric procedures are the major exception across nearly all payers, Medicare included, because of the documented-failed-conservative-treatment requirement under NCD 100.1.

How much does an assistant surgeon actually get paid on a general surgery case?

Under Medicare, a physician assistant surgeon billed with modifier 80, 81, or 82 is paid 16% of the fee schedule amount that would apply to the primary surgeon for that same procedure. A non-physician practitioner — PA, NP, or clinical nurse specialist — billed with modifier AS as first assistant is paid 85% of that 16% rate, which works out to roughly 13.6% of the surgeon's allowed amount. Whether an assistant is payable at all for a given procedure depends on the code's assistant-at-surgery payment indicator in the Medicare Physician Fee Schedule, which is set based on how often an assistant is actually used nationally for that procedure.

What's the MUE limit on 47562 and what happens if we bill more than one unit?

47562, laparoscopic cholecystectomy, carries a Medically Unlikely Edit of 1 unit per date of service — a patient has one gallbladder, so a second unit is clinically implausible on its face. This MUE is commonly reported as carrying MAI 2, meaning it's an absolute, date-of-service edit with no appeal path; billing more than one unit isn't a documentation problem to fix, it's a charge-entry error to correct and rebill. Confirm the current MAI classification in the CMS MUE lookup tool before assuming any exceeded-unit denial on this code is worth appealing.

What documentation does NCD 100.1 require before a bariatric surgery claim can go out?

Confirmed body-mass index of 35 kg/m² or greater, at least one documented obesity-related comorbidity, and documented evidence the patient was previously unsuccessful with medical treatment for obesity — all three, not two of three. NCD 100.1 no longer requires facility certification as a Bariatric Surgery Center of Excellence for dates of service on or after September 24, 2013, which is a frequently outdated assumption in older internal checklists. Type 2 diabetes mellitus has counted as a qualifying comorbidity since February 12, 2009. Stand-alone laparoscopic sleeve gastrectomy coverage is left to individual MAC discretion rather than being nationally covered outright, so confirm your MAC's specific position before scheduling one.

Is modifier 59 or an X-modifier the right way to bill lysis of adhesions with another abdominal procedure?

Almost never as a routine matter. Lysis of adhesions is bundled into most primary abdominal procedures under NCCI because some degree of adhesiolysis is an expected, non-separately-payable part of getting to the operative field. It's only separately reportable when the adhesions were dense, unrelated to the primary pathology, and took materially longer to address than the primary procedure itself — and the operative note has to describe that extra time and difficulty explicitly, not just state that adhesions were present. Appending a modifier without that documentation is one of the more commonly audited patterns in general surgery billing.
