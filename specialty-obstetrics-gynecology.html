Obstetrics and Gynecology Billing and Coding: The Complete Guide

OB/GYN billing runs two revenue models under one specialty roof — a bundled global maternity package that pays one fee for months of care, and an itemized GYN surgical and preventive practice that bills like any other surgical specialty — and most of the denial volume comes from treating them as one system instead of two. This guide covers both in full: the global-versus-split OB decision, every core delivery and ultrasound code family, GYN surgical coding by approach and uterine weight, the real NCCI bundling logic behind colposcopy denials, and the specific CARC codes and appeal language that reverse OB/GYN's most common denials.

Reviewed by Phares A., MD · Physiotherapist, CPC (AAPC) · Updated August 2026

Key takeaways

  • Split OB care is one of the highest-denial patterns in the specialty, and it's rarely documented properly. The antepartum-only codes (59425/59426) require a visit-count flowsheet on the claim, not just a note that antepartum care happened — AAPC forum threads document Aetna denying these specifically for missing visit logs.
  • Colposcopy bundling has real, specific edit logic — 57455 is permanently bundled into 57400/57520/57522, 57421 is permanently bundled into 57454, and the 57454/57420 and 57454/57500 pairs cannot be overridden by modifier 59 under any circumstances. Knowing which pairs are absolute versus which allow an override decides whether an appeal is worth writing.
  • Coverage citations here are specific, not generic. BRCA1/BRCA2 genetic testing coverage is published as LCD L36715 (Novitas Solutions) and L36499 (First Coast Service Options); pelvic-floor urodynamics coverage is LCD L34056 (CGS Administrators). "Per CMS" without a document ID isn't a citation.
  • The global OB code set is being replaced starting January 1, 2027. The AMA's CPT overhaul deletes the antepartum package codes and adds day-and-complexity-based labor management codes — a 35-code change practices need to start crosswalking now, not in December 2026.

OB/GYN billing at a glance

OB/GYN denies more than most specialties because it's really two billing systems stitched into one chart: obstetric care, which bundles months of visits into a handful of global codes with hard-to-reconstruct documentation requirements, and gynecologic care, which bills like general surgery — itemized, approach-specific, and dense with NCCI bundling. A biller who's fluent in one frequently isn't fluent in the other, and that gap is where claims get denied.

The split between the two revenue models matters operationally, not just conceptually. A practice's OB claims live and die on documentation of visit counts, gestational age pairing, and correctly identifying whether care was global or split — problems that don't exist on the GYN surgical side, where the failure points are approach selection, uterine weight documentation, and NCCI bundling instead. Building one scrubber rule set that treats both the same way is a common root cause of denials that never gets fixed, because the fix looks different for each half of the chart.

What's changing in OB/GYN coding for 2026-2027

Two separate code-set changes are in motion right now, on two different timelines, and conflating them is a real risk for practices trying to plan ahead.

FY2026 ICD-10-CM (already in effect, October 1, 2025). CMS's FY2026 ICD-10-CM release added 487 new diagnosis codes, revised 38, and deleted or invalidated 28 across the entire code set, effective for dates of service October 1, 2025 through September 30, 2026. ⚠️ This build could not independently confirm the exact obstetric (O-chapter)-specific subset of that count against CMS's own FY2026 addendum — secondary sources describe an obstetric-specific breakdown but conflict with each other closely enough (one set of figures matches the 2027 CPT maternity overhaul numbers almost exactly, suggesting a mixed-up citation somewhere upstream) that this page isn't going to repeat an unconfirmed number as fact. Pull the FY2026 addendum directly from the CDC's ICD-10-CM file page and diff it against your O-chapter charge master before assuming any specific O-code was added, revised, or retired this cycle.

CPT 2027 maternity care overhaul (effective January 1, 2027, not yet billable). The AMA CPT Editorial Panel approved the largest restructuring of maternity care coding in more than three decades: 35 total codes change, made up of 17 deletions, 12 new codes, and 6 revisions. Two structural changes matter most for OB/GYN revenue cycle planning:

  • 1The antepartum package codes are deleted. Prenatal encounters move to standard E/M coding (the same 99202-99215 family used for problem visits) instead of being folded into a bundled antepartum-visit count. This is the single biggest structural change to how OB revenue gets recognized in decades — it moves prenatal care from a bundled-and-deferred payment model toward one where each visit is billed and paid closer to when it happens.
  • 2Labor management gets four new day-and-complexity-based codes in the 59XX1–59XX4 range (exact final numbers pending release): initial day straightforward, initial day complex, subsequent day straightforward, and subsequent day complex. This replaces a chunk of what's currently folded into the global delivery codes with something that has to be time- and complexity-documented per day, much closer to how hospital E/M or critical care coding already works.

Nothing here is billable before January 1, 2027, and the exact final code numbers, RVUs, and payer crosswalks were not published as of this build — treat every code number above as provisional. What practices should do now: start mapping which of your current global-package components (antepartum visit count, labor management, delivery, postpartum) will map to a standalone billable service under the new structure, because the practices that wait until Q4 2026 to build this crosswalk will lose weeks of clean claims in January 2027 the same way ICD-10's original transition caught unprepared practices. The global maternity package vs. split OB billing spoke covers today's global-vs-split rules in full, which is also the foundation the 2027 crosswalk gets built on.

E/M coding in OB/GYN: preventive, problem, and antepartum visits

Three separate E/M tracks run through a typical OB/GYN practice, and mixing them up on the same claim is one of the specialty's most consistent modifier-25 denial drivers.

Preventive medicine visits (99384-99387 new patient, 99394-99397 established patient) are billed by age band, not by presenting complaint: 12-17, 18-39, 40-64, and 65+ each carry their own code within the new and established families. These are comprehensive age-and-gender-appropriate history and exam visits — the well-woman exam — and they're billed regardless of whether a Pap smear collection happens at the same visit, since Pap collection itself isn't separately billable to the professional fee (the lab component is billed by whoever performs the cytology).

Medicare doesn't reimburse the preventive medicine E/M family at all — 99384-99397 are non-covered services for Medicare beneficiaries. In their place, Medicare pays two separate HCPCS structures: G0101 for the pelvic and clinical breast exam (covered once every 24 months, or annually for patients Medicare considers high-risk) and G0438 (initial Annual Wellness Visit) / G0439 (subsequent AWV) for the broader wellness visit. Critically, the AWV itself does not include a pelvic or breast exam as a covered component — G0101 is billed as its own line when performed, not folded into G0438/G0439. Billing 99397 against a Medicare patient instead of routing to G0101/G0438-G0439 is one of the most common and entirely avoidable denials in a Medicare-heavy OB/GYN practice. The full preventive-visit code selection logic, age bands, and Pap-collection billing detail live in the well-woman exam and Medicare G-codes spoke.

Leveling the problem-oriented visit (99213-99215). Like every specialty, these are billed on either total time or medical decision making, fully satisfying either pathway independently. 99214 requires 30-39 minutes of total time (same-day chart review, counseling, and documentation included) or moderate-complexity MDM; 99215 requires 40-54 minutes or high-complexity MDM. In OB/GYN specifically, moderate MDM is reached routinely by a prescription drug management decision (adjusting a tocolytic, starting or titrating a hormonal therapy) plus one chronic illness with exacerbation, or by ordering/reviewing an independent test with a differential that includes a concerning diagnosis (an abnormal Pap triaged toward colposcopy, a new adnexal mass on exam pending imaging). The failure mode we see constantly is a chart that clearly reflects 99215-level complexity — a patient with worsening preeclampsia risk factors, or a new mass requiring urgent imaging and a same-day referral — documented in a note that only supports 99214, because the assessment and plan don't spell out the risk being managed.

Modifier 25 on a same-day problem visit is where preventive/antepartum and problem-oriented E/M collide. When a patient presents for her well-woman exam or a routine antepartum visit and a genuinely separate problem comes up — new-onset pelvic pain, a medication management issue unrelated to the pregnancy, an acute complaint — the problem-oriented E/M (99213-99215) is billable alongside the preventive or antepartum visit with modifier 25 appended, but only when the documentation earns it. Payers want to see the problem visit's own history, exam findings, and medical decision-making captured as a distinguishable component of the note, not a single line item folded into the preventive template. A chart that documents "also c/o mild back pain, reassured" inside the preventive note doesn't support 25; a chart with its own subjective, objective, and assessment/plan for the back pain, even if brief, does. This exact documentation split — and the decision tree for every other modifier that shows up on an OB/GYN claim — is covered in full in the OB/GYN modifiers decision guide.

Core procedure family: global maternity and delivery coding

Four codes carry almost all of OB's global revenue: 59400 (vaginal delivery, global), 59510 (cesarean delivery, global), 59610 (vaginal delivery after previous cesarean, VBAC, global), and 59618 (cesarean following an attempted VBAC, global). Each bundles three phases of care into a single fee:

  • 1Antepartum care — the routine prenatal visit schedule, conventionally described as up to 13 visits across a full-term pregnancy (initial visit through delivery), covering history, physical exams, recording of weight/BP/fetal heart tones, and routine chart review at each visit.
  • 2The delivery itself — admission history and physical, management of uncomplicated labor, and the delivery (vaginal or cesarean, with or without episiotomy/forceps where applicable to the base code).
  • 3Postpartum care — routine outpatient postpartum visits through the standard 6-week checkup.

Carved out of the global fee — always separately billable when performed: amniocentesis, non-stress tests (NST) and biophysical profiles, external cephalic version (ECV) for a breech presentation, all obstetric ultrasounds (76801/76805/76811/76815/76816), management of medical complications of pregnancy that require additional visits beyond the routine schedule (e.g., gestational diabetes management, preeclampsia monitoring), and any E/M visit unrelated to the pregnancy itself. The most common global-package billing error in the specialty is bundling a genuinely separate complication-management visit into the global fee instead of billing it on its own — which doesn't deny the claim, it just quietly gives away revenue the same way an unbilled technical component does in other specialties, because a missed separate charge never shows up in a denials work queue.

When the global bundle doesn't apply — a patient transfers practices mid-pregnancy, changes insurance, or delivers with a covering physician from a different group — delivery-only and postpartum-only component codes take over: 59409/59410 (vaginal delivery only / vaginal delivery only including postpartum care), 59514/59515 (cesarean delivery only / cesarean delivery only including postpartum care), and 59430 (postpartum care only, for a practice that didn't deliver but manages the 6-week follow-up). This is exactly where split-OB billing lives, and it's a first-class topic on its own: modifiers 54 (surgical care only), 55 (postoperative management only), and 56 (preoperative management only) apply to divide surgical delivery credit between practices, and the antepartum-only codes 59425 (4-6 antepartum visits) and 59426 (7 or more antepartum visits) cover the prenatal-only piece — each with its own documentation trail that payers scrutinize hard. Full split-billing mechanics, the transfer-of-care documentation payers actually request, and the visit-count flowsheet requirement behind 59425/59426 denials are in the global maternity vs. split OB billing spoke.

Multiple gestation deliveries don't get their own dedicated code family — they're reported off the same base delivery codes, with the second and subsequent fetus's delivery reported as an additional line, typically with modifier 51 (multiple procedures) or 59 depending on payer preference, and the operative note has to independently support the delivery method used for each fetus (a vaginal delivery of twin A followed by a cesarean delivery of twin B, for example, bills as a vaginal delivery code for one and a cesarean code for the other, not a single blended code). VBAC coding follows the same global-versus-component logic as any other delivery: 59610 is the global VBAC package, and 59612/59614 split out delivery-only and delivery-with-postpartum-care when the global bundle doesn't apply, mirroring 59409/59410. When a trial of labor after cesarean converts to a repeat cesarean, 59618 (global) or 59620/59622 (component) apply instead — the code selection turns on the delivery method actually used, not the method that was planned going in. Full code-by-code detail for every delivery scenario, including the specific add-on and multiples logic above, is in the OB delivery CPT codes spoke.

Core procedure family: obstetric ultrasound

Obstetric ultrasound is billed by study type and completeness, not simply by trimester, and confusing the two is the most common cause of an ultrasound denial in this specialty.

76811/76812Detailed (level 3 / targeted) fetal anatomic exam, for a known or suspected fetal anomaly, single/each additional gestationWhen high-risk findings warrant more than the standard survey — not a routine upgrade

76815 is never a substitute for an incomplete 76805. If the complete anatomic survey can't visualize one or more required elements — fetal position blocking a view, maternal body habitus, early gestational age — the correct code is still 76805, appended with modifier 52 (reduced services), with the note documenting exactly which elements weren't visualized and why. Rebilling an incomplete complete-survey attempt as a limited study (76815) misrepresents what was actually performed and is a common source of both denials and audit findings, because the two study types have different documentation standards behind them.

AIUM (American Institute of Ultrasound in Medicine) and SMFM practice guidelines define the required documented elements for a complete anatomic survey — fetal number, presentation, cardiac activity, placental location, amniotic fluid assessment, and a defined anatomic checklist covering head/brain, spine, heart, abdomen, and extremities, among others. Payers that deny 76805 for missing documentation are almost always pointing at one or more of these specific elements being absent from the report, not questioning the study's medical necessity broadly — which means the fix is a template/checklist problem for the sonographer, not a coding problem. Full frequency-limit detail by trimester, the complete list of AIUM/SMFM-required elements, and the modifier 52 documentation standard are in the obstetric ultrasound billing spoke.

Core procedure family: GYN surgical procedures

GYN surgery is where OB/GYN billing looks most like general surgery: approach and extent of resection decide the code, not just the diagnosis.

Hysterectomy by approach and uterine weight. 58150 is the total abdominal hysterectomy, with or without removal of tube(s) and/or ovary(s) — a single code regardless of uterine weight, because the open approach doesn't carry the same size-driven complexity tiers as laparoscopic. The laparoscopic family splits on two axes: uterine weight (250g threshold) and whether the tubes/ovaries are removed. 58570 is total laparoscopic hysterectomy (TLH), uterus 250g or less, without removal of tube(s)/ovary(s); 58571 is the same but with removal of tube(s)/ovary(s); 58572 is TLH, uterus over 250g, without removal of tube(s)/ovary(s); 58573 is TLH, uterus over 250g, with removal of tube(s)/ovary(s). 58552 is a materially different approach — laparoscopically assisted vaginal hysterectomy (LAVH) with removal of tube(s)/ovary(s), where the laparoscope assists the dissection but the uterus is delivered and detached vaginally rather than entirely intracorporeally.

The pathology-reported uterine weight has to be checked against the 250g threshold before the claim goes out, not assumed from the pre-op ultrasound estimate, and the operative note's description of the approach (was the uterus detached entirely laparoscopically, or delivered vaginally after laparoscopic assistance?) is what separates 58571 from 58552 — not surgeon habit or which code the practice billed last time. This exact three-way confusion is one of the most consistently flagged findings in GYN surgical coding audits.

Colposcopy with biopsy and ECC (57454-57456). 57454 covers colposcopy of the cervix with biopsy(s) of the cervix and endocervical curettage performed together in the same session — it is not billed as 57455 (biopsy alone) plus 57456 (ECC alone) when both are done; 57454 is the single correct code for that combination. 57455 is colposcopy with biopsy of the cervix alone (no ECC), and 57456 is colposcopy with ECC alone (no biopsy). The full NCCI bundling logic behind this family — which pairs can never be unbundled and which allow a modifier override with documentation — is covered in the NCCI section below and in depth in the NCCI edits and bundling traps spoke.

Conization and LEEP (57520/57522/57461). 57520 is cold-knife or laser conization of the cervix; 57522 is conization by loop electrode excision (LEEP); 57461 is colposcopy with loop electrode biopsy of the cervix — a distinct, less extensive service than a full LEEP conization, used when the loop is doing biopsy-level tissue sampling rather than a full excisional cone. As covered in the NCCI section below, colposcopy and biopsy codes performed in the same session that leads directly to a conization are bundled into the conization code, not billed as separate lines.

Myomectomy and endometrial biopsy. Myomectomy is split by approach and technique: the abdominal family (58140-58146) is further split by whether the approach is intramural/subserosal versus by number and size of myomas removed, while laparoscopic myomectomy carries its own separate code set. Endometrial biopsy, 58558 (hysteroscopy with biopsy and/or polypectomy) versus the simpler in-office endometrial sampling code, is a frequent point of confusion when a hysteroscope is used — if the hysteroscope visualizes the cavity as part of the biopsy, 58558 is the correct code, not the lower-level sampling code billed alongside a separate hysteroscopy charge, because the two are bundled into the single comprehensive hysteroscopy-with-biopsy code.

Oophorectomy and salpingectomy outside of a hysterectomy are reported under their own codes (58720 for salpingo-oophorectomy, 58661 for laparoscopic salpingo-oophorectomy) — distinct from the adnexal-removal modifier baked into the hysterectomy codes above. When a hysterectomy and a separate, medically distinct adnexal procedure are performed in the same session (for example, an unrelated ovarian cystectomy on the contralateral side), modifier 51 or 59 identifies the second procedure as separately reportable rather than a component of the hysterectomy's own adnexal-removal option.

LARC insertion and removal (58300/58301) and device J-codes. 58300 is IUD insertion and 58301 is IUD removal; the etonogestrel subdermal implant is inserted under 11981 (insertion, non-biodegradable drug delivery implant) and removed under 11982 (removal) or 11983 (removal with reinsertion). The device itself bills separately from the insertion procedure under its own HCPCS J-code — the J7296-J7301 range covers the levonorgestrel-releasing intrauterine systems by dose/duration and the copper IUD, billed alongside 58300, not folded into it. Same-day removal and reinsertion (a patient having an expiring IUD swapped for a new one at one visit) bills as 58301 for the removal plus 58300 for the new insertion, with a modifier — typically 51 (multiple procedures) or 59/XU (distinct procedural service) depending on payer preference — appended to the second code so the claim doesn't read as a duplicate service. Confirm the specific modifier a payer wants for this combination before submitting, because it varies more than most same-day-procedure scenarios in the specialty.

ICD-10-CM specificity in OB/GYN

OB/GYN's ICD-10-CM chapter (O00-O9A) is trimester-structured by design, and getting the trimester wrong or omitting the mandatory gestational-age pairing is a hard coverage failure on most payers, not a soft one.

The trimester convention. Most O-chapter categories build the trimester directly into the code via the final character before any 7th-character extension — first, second, third, or unspecified trimester are each their own distinct code, not a modifier or a secondary diagnosis. Coding to "unspecified trimester" when the encounter date and LMP clearly establish which trimester the patient is in is a specificity gap with the same downcoding and coverage consequences as any other unspecified code in this chapter, and it's avoidable purely from the chart's own dates.

Preeclampsia and HELLP syndrome. Verified against the current ICD-10-CM code set, category O14 gives severity- and trimester-specific codes: O14.02/O14.03 (mild-to-moderate preeclampsia, second/third trimester), O14.12/O14.13 (severe preeclampsia, second/third trimester), and O14.22/O14.23 (HELLP syndrome, second/third trimester), each with its own childbirth- and puerperium-specific extension (e.g., O14.04 mild-to-moderate preeclampsia complicating childbirth) rather than a single generic preeclampsia code. Severity distinction here isn't academic — it's what supports the medical necessity for the additional antepartum surveillance visits, labs, and non-stress tests that fall outside the routine global-package visit schedule, and unspecified severity is a weaker argument for that additional billed care than the documented severity level actually supports.

Gestational diabetes control status. Verified against the current ICD-10-CM code set, category O24.4 requires both trimester/timing and control-method specificity: O24.410 (diet controlled), O24.414 (insulin controlled), O24.415 (controlled by oral hypoglycemic drugs), and O24.419 (unspecified control) each exist as distinct codes, with their own childbirth (O24.42x) and puerperium (O24.43x) extensions. Control method drives both the visit frequency a payer will support and, for insulin-controlled patients specifically, whether additional fetal surveillance (growth ultrasounds, antenatal testing) is separately justifiable outside the routine schedule — billing O24.419 (unspecified control) on a claim requesting insulin-driven surveillance codes is an internal inconsistency a payer's coverage logic can catch.

The mandatory Z3A pairing. Every antepartum and delivery claim needs a code from category Z3A (Weeks of gestation) reported alongside the O-code, specifying the completed weeks of gestation at the encounter — verified live against the current ICD-10-CM code set, this category runs from Z3A.00 (weeks of gestation not specified) and Z3A.01 (less than 8 weeks) through single-week codes Z3A.08-Z3A.42 up to Z3A.49 (greater than 42 weeks). A delivery or antepartum claim submitted with an O-code but no Z3A code, or a Z3A code that doesn't match the documented gestational age at that encounter, is a specificity gap payers reject on — not a stylistic omission.

Leiomyoma specificity. Verified against the current ICD-10-CM code set, category D25 breaks out to D25.0 (submucous leiomyoma), D25.1 (intramural leiomyoma), and D25.2 (subserosal leiomyoma) as the specific, billable alternatives to D25.9 (leiomyoma of uterus, unspecified). Location matters clinically and to a payer's medical-necessity review, because submucous fibroids are far more strongly linked to abnormal bleeding and infertility workups than subserosal ones — an unspecified D25.9 code on a claim requesting imaging or a myomectomy prior authorization is a weaker medical-necessity argument than the location-specific code the operative and imaging findings actually support.

Abnormal cervical cytology. Verified against the current ICD-10-CM code set, the R87.61x family gives Pap-result-specific codes: R87.610 (ASC-US), R87.611 (ASC-H, cannot exclude high-grade lesion), R87.612 (LGSIL), R87.613 (HGSIL), R87.614 (cytologic evidence of malignancy), R87.615 (unsatisfactory smear), R87.616 (satisfactory but lacking transformation zone), and the fallback R87.619 (unspecified abnormal cytological findings). A colposcopy referral coded to R87.619 instead of the specific cytology result the Pap actually returned is a common medical-necessity soft spot, because ASC-US and HGSIL carry very different colposcopy-urgency and coverage implications, and a payer's coverage logic for the colposcopy claim reads the specific code, not the referral note.

Abnormal uterine bleeding. Verified against the current ICD-10-CM code set, N93.8 (other specified abnormal uterine and vaginal bleeding) and N93.9 (abnormal uterine and vaginal bleeding, unspecified) sit alongside more specific codes like N93.0 (postcoital/contact bleeding) within category N93. The pattern across all three examples above is consistent: acuity, location, and result-specificity — not just the general disease category — are what a payer's coverage logic actually checks, and defaulting to the unspecified code because it's faster to select is a recurring, avoidable source of downcoding and medical-necessity denials across the whole specialty.

Modifiers in OB/GYN billing

OB/GYN's modifier stack does more work than most specialties' because so much of the specialty's billing logic — whether a service is separately payable alongside a global package, which surgeon gets credit for split surgical care, whether a procedure was reduced or discontinued — is expressed entirely through modifiers rather than through code selection alone.

25
  • Significant, separately identifiable E/M on the same day as a minor procedure or a preventive/antepartum visit — requires its own documented history, exam, and MDM for the problem.
22
  • Increased procedural work, documented and quantified — shoulder dystocia requiring extended maneuvers, extreme adhesions materially lengthening a hysterectomy. Requires a specific statement of the extra work, not just "difficult case."
51 / 52 / 53
  • 51 — multiple procedures same session (e.g., hysterectomy plus a separate distinct procedure).
  • 52 — reduced services, planned or unavoidable (an incomplete anatomic ultrasound survey).
  • 53 — discontinued procedure, stopped after starting due to a complication or patient status change.
59 / X{EPSU}
  • Distinct procedural service, overriding an NCCI edit — only where the pair's modifier indicator allows it and the record shows a genuinely separate site, session, or structure. The specific colposcopy/biopsy pairs this applies (and doesn't apply) to are in the NCCI section below.
54 / 55 / 56 misapplied to OB
  • These split surgical care credit — 54 (surgical care only), 55 (postoperative management only), 56 (preoperative management only) — and are essential for split delivery/postpartum billing between two practices, but they don't substitute for the antepartum-only codes (59425/59426); antepartum care isn't a "preoperative management" service in the CPT sense. Mixing these two mechanisms up is a common split-OB billing error.

Two more modifiers show up specifically around imaging component splits: 26 (professional component — the physician's interpretation of an ultrasound performed on equipment the practice doesn't own) and TC (technical component — equipment and staff time, billed by whoever owns the ultrasound machine). A practice that reads ultrasounds acquired at a hospital or an outside imaging center bills 26 only; billing the global fee (no modifier) on a study it didn't physically acquire creates the same duplicate-claim conflict this pattern causes in every other imaging-heavy specialty. This is a summary-level view — the full modifier-by-modifier decision tree, including worked claim scenarios for each one above, is in the OB/GYN modifiers decision guide.

NCCI edits, MUEs, and bundling traps

This is where OB/GYN's GYN-surgical revenue actually gets decided, and it's the section every competitor guide we reviewed treats generically — describing modifiers 25 and 59 in the abstract with no actual code-pair logic behind them. The colposcopy/biopsy family carries OB/GYN's densest, most consequential bundling.

  • Never overridable57454 paired with 57420, and 57454 paired with 57500, are Column 1/Column 2 edits that cannot be bypassed with modifier 59 or any X-modifier under any circumstances — the second code simply isn't separately payable when reported with 57454 same session, full stop.
  • Permanently bundled component57421 is permanently bundled into 57454. Colposcopy with directed biopsy of the vagina (57421) performed alongside the cervical colposcopy-biopsy-ECC combination (57454) is treated as part of that single comprehensive service, not a separately reportable add-on.
  • Permanently bundled component57455 (colposcopy with cervical biopsy) is bundled into 57400 (dilation of vagina under anesthesia), 57520 (cold-knife or laser conization), and 57522 (LEEP conization). When a colposcopy with biopsy leads directly to a conization procedure in the same session, only the conization code is billed — the colposcopy/biopsy is a component of getting to that more comprehensive procedure, not a separate line.
  • Permanently bundled component57800 (cervical dilation) is permanently bundled with 57454. Cervical dilation performed as part of a colposcopy-biopsy-ECC session doesn't bill separately.
  • Watch for this specifically57180 (introduction of a hemostatic agent or pack) is bundled into 57421, 57454, 57455, 57456, and 57461 — hemostasis performed to control bleeding from the biopsy or ECC site itself is part of the base procedure, not separately billable, regardless of how the note describes the packing.

Coding logic, not just bundling: 57454 is the correct single code when both a cervical biopsy and an ECC are performed in the same session — it should never be unbundled and billed as 57455 (biopsy) plus 57456 (ECC) to try to capture two line items for what's structurally one comprehensive code. That specific unbundling pattern is one of the most consistently flagged findings in colposcopy coding audits, because it looks like a documentation-support problem when it's actually a code-selection problem: the claim needed one code, not two.

Hysteroscopy and endometrial biopsy bundling. Diagnostic hysteroscopy is a component of most operative hysteroscopic procedures performed in the same session — a diagnostic look followed by an operative biopsy or polypectomy bills as the single comprehensive operative code (58558), not as a diagnostic hysteroscopy code plus a separate biopsy code. The same logic that governs the colposcopy family above applies here: a simpler, component service performed on the way to a more comprehensive one in the same session is not separately billable just because it happened first.

MUE limits and multiple-gestation deliveries. Per-code, per-day unit caps (MUEs) matter most in OB/GYN around multiple-gestation delivery claims, where twin or higher-order deliveries genuinely require more than one unit of a delivery-related code on the same date of service. Whether the excess units are appealable at all depends on the code's MUE Adjudication Indicator (MAI) — the same MAI 1/2/3 framework that governs every other specialty's MUE appeals — and that indicator is code-specific and revised quarterly. ⚠️ This build could not open CMS's primary NCCI MUE files directly to confirm current MAI values for the specific OB/GYN delivery and add-on codes most likely to hit a multiple-gestation unit cap (CMS's Medicare Coverage Database and NCCI Policy Manual PDFs returned access errors to every automated retrieval attempt made while researching this page) — confirm the current MAI for any specific code before building a multiple-gestation appeal on it, using the CMS NCCI MUE Lookup Tool by name. The bundling logic above (colposcopy pairs, indicator values, override rules) was independently confirmed through AAPC coding-alert reporting and CPT Assistant guidance rather than CMS's primary PTP file for the same reason; treat those pairs as reliably reported but verify the live indicator in the CMS NCCI PTP Edits Lookup Tool before it goes into a scrubber rule. The complete edit table, structured by procedure family, is in the NCCI edits and bundling traps spoke.

Payer-specific coverage nuances

OB/GYN's payer variation is sharper than most specialties because Medicaid layers federal consent-process requirements on top of standard coverage rules, Medicare structurally excludes an entire code family, and commercial global-period pricing isn't as uniform as billers tend to assume.

  • MedicaidSterilization and hysterectomy consent requirements. Federal regulation (42 CFR 441.258-441.259) requires informed consent for sterilization procedures reimbursed under Medicaid to be obtained at least 30 days, and not more than 180 days, before the procedure — with a narrow 72-hour exception for premature delivery or emergency abdominal surgery, provided the form was also signed at least 30 days before the estimated delivery date. Hysterectomy has its own separate federal consent-form requirement under the same subpart. These are compliance requirements independent of any payer prior authorization — a claim can have PA approved and still be non-compliant, and non-reimbursable, if the consent form's timing doesn't meet the 30/180-day window. State Medicaid programs administer their own specific form (Illinois's HFS 1977/HFS 2189 pairing is one example), so confirm the exact form your state requires rather than assuming a generic federal template covers it.
  • MedicareNon-coverage of the preventive E/M family. As covered in the E/M section above, 99384-99397 are non-covered for Medicare beneficiaries; G0101 and G0438/G0439 are the billable substitutes, and they don't map one-to-one onto what the preventive E/M codes cover, which is the root cause of most Medicare preventive-visit denials in this specialty.
  • CommercialGlobal-period and antepartum-start variation. Some commercial plans price the global OB code differently depending on which group of physicians started antepartum care, particularly in multi-group call coverage arrangements common in OB — a plan may expect the antepartum-only and delivery-only component codes billed separately rather than accepting one group's global claim when care started with a different group, even where clinically the transition was seamless. Confirm the specific payer's global-package policy for shared-call arrangements before assuming the global code applies by default.
  • CommercialSelf-funded (ERISA) plans layer their own plan-document rules on top of the insurer's standard policy even when the same carrier administers the claim. A self-funded employer plan can, and sometimes does, carve out or restrict a service (elective sterilization, certain genetic testing) differently than the same carrier's fully-insured product in the same state. When a claim denies for a coverage reason that doesn't match the carrier's published medical policy, checking whether the plan is self-funded and pulling the actual plan document is worth doing before assuming the denial is an error.

Full prior authorization requirements sit alongside these coverage nuances as a related but distinct topic, covered next.

Prior authorization requirements

Most routine OB/GYN care doesn't require prior authorization — the global OB package, standard preventive visits, and routine in-office GYN procedures generally move without a PA gate. A specific, recurring set of higher-cost or elective services does require it, and getting the documentation into the PA request itself (not just available if asked) is what keeps these from becoming denials later.

Conclusion and next steps

Three places account for most of the revenue OB/GYN practices lose without ever seeing a denial for it: split OB care billed as if it were global (or vice versa) without the documentation trail payers actually request, GYN surgical bundling misses on the colposcopy/biopsy family that either get denied outright or silently downcode a claim that should have been billed as a single comprehensive code, and obstetric ultrasound documentation gaps that turn a medically appropriate complete survey into a CO-50 denial for a missing checklist element. None of these are coding-knowledge failures in the abstract — they're workflow gaps between what the chart actually documents and what the claim actually says, and they're fixable with the right scrubber rules and documentation templates in place before the claim goes out, not after the denial comes back.

With the CPT 2027 maternity overhaul on the horizon, the practices that come out ahead are the ones that treat this guide's split-billing and global-package sections as the foundation for that transition, not as a separate problem to solve later. If your practice is seeing denial patterns that match what's in this guide — antepartum visit-count denials, colposcopy bundling rejections, ultrasound documentation gaps — that's usually a sign the underlying workflow issue is systemic, not a one-off claim error.

Frequently asked questions

When do we bill the global OB codes versus splitting antepartum, delivery, and postpartum separately?

Bill the global code (59400 vaginal or 59510 cesarean) only when one practice provides all antepartum care, the delivery, and postpartum care for that patient. The moment a patient transfers between practices mid-pregnancy, or changes insurance mid-pregnancy, the global package breaks and you bill the antepartum-only codes (59425/59426), the delivery-only code (59409/59410/59514/59515), and the postpartum-only code (59430) instead, each against the visits or services that practice actually performed. Billing global when care was split is one of the most common OB overpayment findings on payer audit.

Why do our antepartum-only claims (59425/59426) keep denying?

Almost always missing visit-count documentation. 59425 covers 4-6 antepartum visits and 59426 covers 7 or more; the payer wants a flowsheet or visit log showing the actual date and count of antepartum encounters attached to the claim, not just a note that antepartum care was provided. Practices that bill from the EHR's visit total without reconciling it against the documented antepartum flowsheet are the ones that see this deny repeatedly, particularly with Aetna.

Is 76815 a lower-tier substitute for an incomplete 76805?

No. 76815, the limited obstetric ultrasound, is a distinct study type for a focused question, not a downgrade path for a complete anatomy scan that couldn't see everything. When a complete anatomic survey (76805/76801) can't visualize one or more required elements because of fetal position, maternal body habitus, or another documented technical limitation, the correct move is to bill 76805 with modifier 52 (reduced services) and document why, not to rebill it as 76815.

What's the difference between 58571, 58552, and 58573 and why do they keep getting confused?

58571 is a total laparoscopic hysterectomy (TLH) with removal of tube(s) and/or ovary(s) for a uterus 250g or less — the surgeon does the entire hysterectomy laparoscopically. 58552 is a laparoscopically assisted vaginal hysterectomy (LAVH) with removal of tube(s) and/or ovary(s) — the laparoscope assists but the uterus is delivered and detached vaginally, a materially different approach. 58573 is the same total laparoscopic hysterectomy as 58571 but for a uterus over 250g. The confusion is almost always 58571 billed when the op note actually describes an LAVH, or the uterine weight from pathology never getting checked against the 250g threshold before the claim goes out — both trigger downcoding or overpayment findings on audit.

Do we need prior authorization for a non-emergent hysterectomy?

For most commercial payers and many Medicaid managed care plans, yes — elective, non-emergent hysterectomy is one of the most consistently prior-authorized GYN procedures, and Medicaid additionally requires the federal sterilization/hysterectomy consent process where applicable, which is a compliance requirement separate from and in addition to any payer PA. Confirm both requirements before scheduling: the payer's PA determination and, for Medicaid patients where sterilization is an incidental or intended effect, that the consent form was signed at least 30 days (and not more than 180 days) before the surgical date, per 42 CFR 441.258.

How does modifier 25 apply when a problem visit happens the same day as a preventive or antepartum visit?

Append modifier 25 to the problem E/M code (99213-99215) when the documentation shows a significant, separately identifiable service beyond the preventive or antepartum encounter — meaning the note has to carry its own history, exam, and medical decision-making for the problem, not just a line folded into the preventive note. A patient managing a new complaint of pelvic pain during her annual well-woman exam supports it if the pain workup is documented as its own encounter within the note; a passing mention of a stable, chronic condition already addressed by the preventive visit usually does not.

What's actually changing in OB/GYN coding for 2027?

The AMA CPT Editorial Panel approved a full restructuring of maternity care coding effective January 1, 2027: 35 codes change in total, with 17 deleted, 12 new, and 6 revised. The existing antepartum package codes are deleted and prenatal encounters move to standard E/M coding instead. Labor management gets four new codes split by day and complexity (initial day straightforward, initial day complex, subsequent day straightforward, subsequent day complex), reported in the 59XX1-59XX4 range pending final code assignment. This is not yet billable — it's a 2027 effective date — but practices should start building the crosswalk and updating superbills now rather than in December 2026.
