Pediatrics Medical Billing and Coding: The Complete 2026 Guide

Pediatric billing runs on a different engine than adult E/M-driven specialties: a huge share of visits are preventive rather than problem-oriented, vaccines are billed by component instead of by dose, age itself decides the code, and Medicaid/EPSDT is the dominant payer for much of a typical panel rather than a minority carve-out. Every competitor guide we reviewed treats NCCI and EPSDT in generalities — no published indicator values, no state named by name, no CARC paired with the actual fix. This guide covers the full 2026 code set, the specific bundling pairs that deny weekly, and the state-level EPSDT mechanics nobody else publishes.

Reviewed by Phares A., MD · Physiotherapist, CPC (AAPC) · Updated August 2026

Key takeaways

  • Well-child code selection is age-on-date-of-service, not time or MDM. A patient who turns 5 the morning of the visit gets 99393, not 99392, regardless of what the scheduler queued.
  • 90460/90461 are billed per vaccine component, not per dose. A six-component combination vaccine is 90460 plus five units of 90461 — billing one unit per injection instead undercounts every hexavalent or pentavalent shot given.
  • Modifier 25 on a same-day sick+well claim goes on the sick E/M, first position — not on the preventive code, and not stacked with other modifiers ahead of it.
  • EPSDT's primary diagnosis is Z00.121 (abnormal findings) or Z00.129 (no abnormal findings) — picking the wrong one can strip medical necessity from a same-day problem visit billed alongside it.
  • 99460 requires a Z38.x primary diagnosis and only the first examiner may bill it. Whoever sees the newborn on day two bills 99462 instead, even if it's the same practice.
  • 2026 added CPT 90482/90483 for stand-alone immunization counseling when no vaccine is given — time-based, and Medicare-noncovered by CMS's own status indicator, so their revenue depends entirely on which commercial and Medicaid MCO payers choose to recognize them.
  • EPSDT is federal law, but the reimbursement mechanics are state-specific. Indiana Medicaid, for example, applies a 30-visit-per-year soft limit without prior authorization — a rule that doesn't exist in every state's EPSDT manual.

Pediatrics billing at a glance

Four structural features separate pediatric billing from an adult-facing E/M specialty. First, volume skews heavily preventive: a well-run pediatric panel bills far more 99381-99395 preventive codes than problem-oriented E/M, which means the age-band table below is used more often than any MDM grid. Second, vaccine administration is billed by component, not by injection or dose, so a coder has to know how many antigens are in a combination product before touching the claim. Third, Medicaid and EPSDT dominate payer mix for much of the country's pediatric population, and EPSDT's federal mandate gets implemented through fifty different state mechanisms, not one uniform program. Fourth, age itself is a coding variable in a way it almost never is in adult medicine — the same clinical encounter is billed under a completely different code depending on whether the patient is 11 or 12 on the date of service.

Layered on top of all of that is a payer-mix complexity most adult specialties don't carry: coordination of benefits between a custodial and non-custodial parent's plans, guardian-of-record data that has to match the payer's file exactly, and a dependent-insured population where eligibility errors are disproportionately common because the subscriber isn't the patient.

E/M coding for pediatrics: sick visits, same-day well+sick, and leveling

Problem-oriented office visits (99202-99215) level the same way in pediatrics as anywhere else — medical decision making or total time, whichever the note supports, not both stacked together. What's specific to pediatrics is volume and mix: a large share of sick visits are self-limited (otitis media, viral URI, uncomplicated gastroenteritis) that genuinely support 99213 on straightforward MDM, and the failure mode isn't usually overcoding — it's under-documenting a visit that actually had moderate complexity because of an underlying chronic condition (asthma, seizure disorder) the note didn't connect to the day's decision-making.

Same-day sick-plus-well is the single highest-volume modifier scenario in pediatric billing, because a parent bringing a child in for a well visit frequently mentions an active problem in the same encounter. Bill the preventive code (99392-99395) plus the problem E/M (99202-99215) with modifier 25 on the sick E/M code, in the first modifier position on that line — not on the well-visit code, and not buried behind another modifier. The documentation has to independently support each service: the well-visit note covers growth, development, and anticipatory guidance; the sick-visit note has its own history, exam, and assessment/plan for the specific complaint, not a single blended paragraph. Commercial payers generally pay both lines; Medicaid fee-for-service and Medicaid MCOs are inconsistent about it state to state and plan to plan, so confirm the specific plan's same-day policy rather than assuming national parity. Documentation strategy, MDM-vs-time worked examples, and the exact same-day payer variance pattern get fuller treatment in our pediatric modifiers guide, introduced in full below.

Preventive medicine and well-child visit coding (99381-99395)

Age on the date of service — not time spent, not MDM complexity — determines which preventive code applies, and it's the single most consequential coding rule in the specialty because of how much volume runs through it. New-patient codes are 99381-99385; established-patient codes for a return preventive visit are 99391-99395. Full mechanics, add-on billing worked examples, and a printable age-band reference are covered in our well-child visit coding guide; the age bands themselves:

Each code bundles the full preventive package under the Bright Futures/AAP periodicity schedule most states and payers reference: age-and-gender-appropriate history, comprehensive exam, growth and developmental surveillance, anticipatory guidance, and review of the immunization record. What it does not bundle is a standardized screening instrument administered and separately scored the same day, which is where the add-on codes matter:

  • 196110 — developmental screening with a standardized instrument (e.g., ASQ-3, PEDS), scored and interpreted, billed separately from the preventive E/M with a Z13.4x screening diagnosis (Z13.40 unspecified, Z13.41 autism, Z13.42 global developmental milestones).
  • 296127 — brief emotional/behavioral assessment with a standardized instrument (e.g., PHQ-9, Vanderbilt), scored, billed per instrument. 96110 and 96127 can both be billed the same visit when each is separately documented and scored — they're not the same screen under two codes.
  • 396160/96161 — standardized health risk assessment instrument, patient-focused (96160) or caregiver-focused (96161), such as a social determinants of health or postpartum depression screen administered to the parent at a well-child visit.

All three add-ons are billed with modifier 25 on the preventive E/M code when a screening is performed the same day — the reasoning mirrors the sick-visit scenario above, because the screening is a separately identifiable service layered onto, not included in, the base preventive code. The specific NCCI and MUE mechanics governing how many of these can be billed together same-day are covered later in this guide.

Vaccine administration and immunization billing

Vaccine administration is where pediatric billing diverges hardest from every other specialty, because the counting unit is the component, not the injection. Our vaccine administration billing guide covers the full code family with worked combination-vaccine examples; the core logic:

Vaccine administration code selection.
CodeWhen to use it
90460First or only component of a vaccine, with face-to-face physician/QHP counseling, patient through age 18
90461Add-on: each additional component of the same vaccine, same visit, same counseling requirement — billed in units, not appended once
90471–90474No counseling furnished, or patient over 18; billed per vaccine administered (not per component) — 90471/90472 for injectable, 90473/90474 for oral/intranasal

Component counting is the part every competitor guide skips past. A combination vaccine like a hexavalent DTaP-IPV-Hib-HepB product carries six distinct antigen components: diphtheria, tetanus, and pertussis (three, as DTaP), inactivated poliovirus, Haemophilus influenzae type b, and hepatitis B. That single injection is billed as 90460 for the first component plus five units of 90461 for the remaining five — not one unit of 90460 per shot given. A pentavalent product with five components follows the same logic: 90460 plus four units of 90461. Undercounting components on combination vaccines is one of the most common silent revenue losses in the specialty, because a claim billed with too few units doesn't deny — it just pays less than the components actually administered.

VFC (Vaccines for Children) stock changes what belongs on the claim, not the administration code. When the vaccine came from VFC inventory, there is no vaccine product charge at all — federal law prohibits billing for the product itself, and the claim should reflect an administration-fee-only line, with the fee capped at whatever the state sets (fee caps vary by state and are published in each state's VFC provider agreement). Privately purchased stock bills both the product code and the administration code normally. Mixing VFC and private stock on the same claim without flagging which dose came from which source is a common audit finding, not just a revenue issue.

Nirsevimab (RSV monoclonal antibody) coding is dose-driven rather than component-driven: 90380 reports the 50 mg/0.5 mL dose for infants under 5 kg, and 90381 reports the 100 mg/1 mL dose for infants at or above 5 kg — the 200 mg second-season dose for high-risk children is billed as two units of 90381, not a distinct code. Pair the product with administration code 96380 if counseling was furnished at the visit, or 96381 if it wasn't; that direction (96380 = counseling, 96381 = no counseling) is the reverse of the 90460/90471 pattern and is a frequent source of confusion for coders who assume the numbering follows the same logic. Use Z23 (encounter for immunization) as the diagnosis. New for 2026: when a caregiver receives immunization counseling but declines the vaccine that day, or the visit is counseling-only by design, bill 90482 or 90483 instead of an administration code, since no product was given.

Newborn care and hospital services

Newborn coding is governed by two hard rules: which day of the stay it is, and who saw the baby first. Our newborn care billing guide covers the full code family with multi-day-stay sequencing examples; the core rules:

Newborn care code selection by scenario.
CodeScenario
99460Initial hospital or birthing center E/M of a normal newborn, per day — billable only by the first physician/QHP to examine the newborn
99461Initial E/M of a normal newborn outside a hospital or birthing center (e.g., attended home birth)
99462Subsequent hospital day(s) of normal newborn care, per day
99463Normal newborn admitted and discharged on the same calendar date — replaces 99460 entirely for that encounter, not billed in addition to it
99468/99469Neonatal critical care, initial/subsequent day, for a critically ill neonate 28 days or younger requiring cardiac/respiratory support and continuous monitoring — a materially different clinical and coding pathway from 99460–99463

99460 requires a Z38.x primary diagnosis — the specific liveborn-infant code by place of birth and delivery type (Z38.00 single liveborn, vaginal, in-hospital; Z38.01 cesarean, and so on through the twin/multiple variants). A normal newborn claim carrying any other code as primary is one of the most reliably flagged patterns in newborn billing; any actual condition being managed (jaundice, feeding difficulty) goes as a secondary diagnosis alongside the Z38.x primary, not instead of it.

The multi-day sequencing error that drives the most denials: billing 99460 on every day of a multi-day stay instead of billing it once, on the day the first examiner saw the newborn, and 99462 for each subsequent day. Only the physician or QHP who performed that first examination bills 99460 — a different provider in the same group who rounds on day two bills 99462, not 99460 again. Confusing 99460 with 99463 is the other frequent error: 99463 applies only when the newborn is both admitted and discharged on the same calendar date, and it replaces 99460 for that encounter rather than stacking with it.

Modifiers in pediatric billing

Four modifiers carry real weight in this specialty, and getting the wrong one on a claim either denies it outright or, on the ACA-preventive modifier, costs the family an unexpected cost-share. Full decision trees and worked scenarios for each are in our pediatric modifiers guide.

25
  • Same-day sick+well: append to the problem-oriented E/M code, first modifier position, when the note independently supports both services.
  • Also required on a separately identifiable E/M billed alongside a screening add-on (96110/96127/96160/96161) or a 90482/90483 counseling code the same day.
59 / X{EPSU}
  • Distinct procedural service — in pediatrics, most often two vaccine administrations at genuinely separate injection sites, or a distinct procedure unrelated to the visit's main service.
  • Prefer the specific X-modifier (XS for separate structure/site) over generic 59 wherever it applies; the record has to independently support the distinction.
33
  • ACA-mandated preventive service, no patient cost-share — relevant to commercial plans subject to Section 2713 preventive coverage requirements.
  • Append to the preventive code so the payer's adjudication system applies the $0 cost-share correctly instead of defaulting to a standard copay/coinsurance calculation.
25 on the well-visit code
  • Modifier 25 belongs on the problem-oriented E/M, not the preventive code, on a same-day sick+well claim. Reversing it is a common enough error that it's worth a specific scrubber rule.

GA and GZ — the ABN-related modifiers — show up in pediatrics only in the narrow case of a Medicare-eligible child, typically a patient on SSI or with end-stage renal disease who carries Medicare as primary or secondary. GA signals a valid ABN is on file for a service Medicare may not cover; GZ signals no ABN was obtained for a service expected to deny. These are rare in a general pediatric panel but relevant to practices serving medically complex or disabled pediatric patients.

NCCI edits, MUEs, and bundling in pediatric coding

This is the section every competitor guide treats as a vague warning rather than a working reference. Two indicator systems decide whether a bundled or capped pediatric code pair is billable at all, and both are specific, published values — not judgment calls. Full bundling reference by code family is in our NCCI edits and MUE limits guide.

The pairs coders hit weekly in a pediatric panel:

  • 199392 + 99213, same day. Not a true NCCI bundling pair — both are separately payable — but payers frequently deny the second line as included in the preventive visit unless modifier 25 is present on the 99213 in the first position and the documentation independently supports it. This is the highest-volume denial-prevention rule in the specialty.
  • 296110 + 96127, same visit. Both are separately billable when each represents a distinct standardized instrument, separately scored and documented — a developmental screen and an emotional/behavioral screen are not the same service reported twice, so this pair is not subject to a bundling denial when the documentation is clean.
  • 390460 vs. 90471 code family selection. Not an NCCI edit but a frequent claim-level error: billing 90471 (no-counseling admin codes) when counseling was actually furnished and documented, or the reverse, understates or overstates the claim and can trigger a payer's own edit logic distinct from CMS's NCCI file.
  • 4MUE unit caps. Billing-industry sources report an MUE of 3 units per date of service on 96127 (one unit per standardized instrument scored), which tracks with how the code is typically used — multiple brief instruments in one visit. This build could not confirm a specific published MUE value for 96110 against CMS's own MUE file (CMS's site returned access errors to automated retrieval attempts made while researching this page), so treat any unit cap you see cited for 96110 as unconfirmed and check the current value in CMS's MUE lookup tool before building a scrubber rule around it. ⚠️

The NCCI modifier indicator (0 = never bypassable, 1 = bypassable with documentation and an NCCI-associated modifier, 9 = edit deleted) and the MUE Adjudication Indicator (1 = claim-line edit, splittable; 2 = absolute date-of-service edit, no appeal path; 3 = appealable date-of-service edit with documentation) apply in pediatrics exactly as they do in every other specialty — the difference is which code pairs actually collide, and that's the table above. ⚠️ This build could not open CMS's primary NCCI PTP edit file or Policy Manual directly to confirm current indicator values for the specific pediatric pairs above (CMS's site returned an access error to automated fetch attempts during this build); every value should be re-verified against the CMS NCCI PTP Edits Lookup Tool and MUE files before it's built into a claims scrubber, since both update quarterly.

Payer-specific and EPSDT/Medicaid nuances

EPSDT — Early and Periodic Screening, Diagnostic, and Treatment — is a federal Medicaid mandate under 42 U.S.C. §1396d(r) covering every Medicaid enrollee under 21, and it's the payer mechanism behind a large share of pediatric billing volume, not a peripheral program. What every generic guide gets wrong is treating it as one uniform benefit: the federal statute sets the floor, but periodicity schedule adoption, visit-limit mechanics, and referral triggers for dental, vision, and hearing all vary by state. Our EPSDT and Medicaid billing guide covers state-by-state mechanics in depth; the structure that matters at claim level:

  • 1Periodicity schedule. Most states adopt the Bright Futures/AAP periodicity schedule as their EPSDT screening interval, but adoption is a state choice, not a federal requirement, and a small number of states use a modified version. Confirm which schedule your state's Medicaid agency has adopted before treating "Bright Futures" as automatically the billing standard.
  • 2Visit-count soft limits. Federal EPSDT law prohibits a hard cap on medically necessary visits or prior authorization for the screening itself, but states can and do apply soft utilization-control limits. Indiana Health Coverage Programs, for example, applies a 30-office-visit-per-year limit before prior authorization is required — a mechanism that doesn't exist in every state's Medicaid manual, which is exactly why "EPSDT covers it" isn't a complete appeal argument on its own.
  • 3Referral triggers. EPSDT requires referral to dental, vision, and hearing services when screening identifies a need, and CMS's SHO #24-005 guidance reinforces timely follow-through on those referrals as part of state compliance — a documented referral that never happened is a compliance gap for the state and a missed-service flag for the practice's own quality reporting.

Layered on top of EPSDT: the ACA's preventive-service mandate (modifier 33) applies to commercial plans covering well-child visits and immunizations at no cost-share, a separate legal basis from EPSDT and relevant even to practices with no Medicaid panel at all. And Medicaid MCOs commonly diverge from their own state's fee-for-service program on same-day sick+well payment — an MCO may deny the second line as bundled where FFS pays it, or vice versa, so the same-day policy has to be checked per plan, not assumed from the state's FFS manual.

Prior authorization in pediatric care

Most routine pediatric care — well visits, vaccines, uncomplicated sick visits — doesn't require prior authorization, which is exactly why the exceptions catch practices off guard when they show up. Five scenarios account for most pediatric PA denials:

  • 1Specialist referrals under Medicaid MCOs. An out-of-network or higher-cost in-network specialist referral commonly needs MCO authorization before the visit, even though the EPSDT screening that generated the referral did not require one itself.
  • 2ADHD and behavioral health evaluations. Formal ADHD diagnostic workups and autism diagnostic testing frequently require prior authorization, particularly under Medicaid MCOs, and the request should go in as soon as the screening (96110/96127) flags a concern, not after the referral is already written.
  • 3RSV monoclonal antibody outside VFC. Some state Medicaid programs require prior authorization for nirsevimab when it's sourced from private stock rather than VFC, particularly for the second-season high-risk dose — confirm your state's specific rule before assuming VFC eligibility alone clears the requirement.
  • 4Non-formulary medications. Common in pediatric psychiatry and chronic-condition management (certain ADHD stimulants, biologics for pediatric autoimmune conditions) where the specific formulation, not just the drug class, drives the PA requirement.
  • 5Out-of-network specialty referrals. Particularly relevant for pediatric subspecialties (pediatric cardiology, pediatric endocrinology) that may not have in-network coverage in a given MCO's network at all, requiring a network-gap exception rather than a standard referral.

The practical workflow: submit the PA request at the point the screening or evaluation identifies the need, not at the point of the specialist's first visit, and track the specific documentation each payer requires (screening tool results, symptom duration, prior treatment attempts) so the request isn't returned for missing information on a resubmission cycle that delays care.

Frequently asked questions

How do we pick the right well-child visit code by age?

Use the patient's age on the date of service, not their age at the last visit and not the time spent in the room. CPT defines five age bands for both new-patient (99381-99385) and established-patient (99391-99395) preventive codes: infant under 1, early childhood 1-4, late childhood 5-11, adolescent 12-17, and 18-39 for the rare pediatric patient who ages into that band mid-panel. A patient who turns 5 the morning of the visit gets 99393, not 99392, even if the front desk scheduled it under the old age band. Build the age cutover into your scheduling and EHR order sets so the code suggested at check-in already matches the date of service.

Can we bill a sick visit and a well visit on the same day?

Yes, when the documentation genuinely supports both as separate, medically necessary services. Bill the preventive code (99392-99395) plus the problem-oriented E/M (99202-99215) with modifier 25 appended to the sick visit code, not the well visit code, and place it in the first modifier position on that line. The note has to show the sick complaint was actually evaluated and managed as its own service, with its own history, exam, and plan, not folded into the well-visit note as an afterthought. Commercial payers generally pay both; state Medicaid fee-for-service and Medicaid MCOs are inconsistent about it, so check your specific plan's same-day policy before assuming the second line will pay.

What's the difference between 90460 and 90471?

90460 requires face-to-face counseling by the physician or another qualified health professional about the vaccine, and it only applies through age 18; it's billed per component of the vaccine, with add-on 90461 for each additional component in a combination product. 90471-90474 are used when no counseling is furnished, or when the patient is over 18, and they're billed per vaccine administered, not per component. Billing 90471 when counseling was actually documented, or reaching for 90460 on an adult sibling who came in for the same shot, are both common enough to be worth a scrubber rule.

How does nirsevimab billing work for RSV season 2026?

The product code depends on dose, not season: 90380 reports the 50 mg/0.5 mL dose for infants under 5 kg, and 90381 reports the 100 mg/1 mL dose for infants at or above 5 kg. A high-risk child receiving the 200 mg second-season dose is billed as two units of 90381, not a separate code. Pair either with administration code 96380 if counseling was furnished at the visit, or 96381 if it wasn't - that direction trips coders up because it's the opposite pattern from 90460/90471. Use Z23 as the encounter diagnosis, and confirm whether the dose came from VFC stock or private stock before deciding whether a product charge belongs on the claim at all.

Why do EPSDT claims deny even though the visit was covered?

Most EPSDT denials trace back to two things: the primary diagnosis doesn't carry the Z00.121/Z00.129 well-child code the state's claims edits expect, or the visit falls outside the periodicity interval the state adopted (usually Bright Futures) without a documented interperiodic reason. Federal law prohibits a state from hard-capping medically necessary EPSDT visits or requiring prior authorization for the screening itself, so a denial citing a visit-count limit as an absolute bar is frequently appealable by citing 42 U.S.C. §1396d(r) and the state's own EPSDT manual. Confirm your state Medicaid agency's specific periodicity and documentation requirements by name before assuming a generic "EPSDT covers it" argument will carry the appeal.

What changed in CPT coding for pediatric immunization counseling in 2026?

CPT added codes 90482 and 90483, effective January 1, 2026, for stand-alone immunization counseling when no vaccine is actually given that day - 90482 for 3 to 10 minutes of counseling, 90483 for more than 10 up to 20 minutes. They're time-based and require the counseling time to be documented. CMS assigned both a status indicator of "I," meaning Medicare will not pay them at all, so their value is almost entirely with commercial payers and Medicaid MCOs that choose to recognize them - check payer policy before building them into your fee schedule. When billed alongside a separately identifiable E/M the same day, modifier 25 goes on the E/M code.

Do pediatric practices need prior authorization for behavioral health referrals?

Frequently, yes, especially under Medicaid managed care. ADHD evaluations, autism diagnostic testing, and any referral to an out-of-network behavioral health specialist commonly require prior authorization from the MCO before the visit, even though the underlying EPSDT screening that identified the concern did not require one. The distinction matters: EPSDT screening itself can't be gated behind prior auth, but the downstream diagnostic and treatment services it triggers often can be, which is exactly the kind of soft-cap CMS's EPSDT guidance permits states to apply for utilization control.

Which primary diagnosis code goes on a newborn's first hospital visit?

A code from the Z38 family - liveborn infant by place of birth and delivery type, such as Z38.00 for a single liveborn delivered vaginally in-hospital or Z38.01 for cesarean. CPT 99460 specifically requires a Z38.x primary diagnosis; pairing it with any other primary code is one of the most common reasons a normal newborn claim gets flagged or denied outright. Any actual newborn condition being evaluated - jaundice, feeding difficulty - is coded as a secondary diagnosis alongside the Z38.x primary, not in place of it.
