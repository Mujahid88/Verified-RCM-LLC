Our complete podiatry guide

Podiatry prior authorization: orthotics, DME, and surgical procedures.

Podiatry runs more prior-authorization systems in parallel than almost any other specialty: a certification process for diabetic shoes that isn't prior authorization at all, an actual PA gate on custom orthotics and several DME categories, and a payer-by-payer surgical review list built around conservative-treatment documentation. Mixing these up is the single most common cause of a denied or delayed podiatry claim that had nothing wrong with the care itself. This guide separates the three systems, gives the codes and thresholds each one runs on, and covers the peer-to-peer and ABN mechanics for when a request doesn't clear on the first pass.

Reviewed by Phares A., MD · Physiotherapist, CPC (AAPC) · Part of our podiatry billing and coding guide

Key takeaways

  • Medicare's diabetic shoe benefit is a certification process, not prior authorization. A physician managing the patient's diabetes signs a certification statement after an in-person visit — there's no pre-approval request to submit or await. Commercial payers frequently run diabetic shoes and custom orthotics through an actual prior-authorization workflow instead, so the two get confused constantly.
  • Bunionectomy (CPT 28292–28299) and hammertoe correction (CPT 28285–28289) are the surgeries payers gate hardest — both commonly require prior authorization with commercial plans, built around a documented conservative-treatment trial rather than a cosmetic exclusion outright.
  • Emergent and urgent care is generally exempt from prior authorization under the federal prudent-layperson standard for emergency services — debridement for a limb-threatening diabetic foot infection is the clearest podiatric example, though plan-specific documentation still matters after the fact.
  • A prior-authorization denial and an ABN solve different problems. One is a coverage decision you can appeal or take to peer-to-peer; the other is the Medicare-specific paperwork that lets the patient choose to proceed and pay if the claim is ultimately denied.

Three different systems, and podiatry runs all of them

The reason podiatry prior authorization trips up billing staff more than most specialties is that it isn't one workflow — it's at least three, and which one applies depends on what's being requested, not on the diagnosis. A custom AFO and a diabetic shoe both get called "orthotics" in casual conversation, but one typically runs through a payer's DME prior-authorization queue and the other, under traditional Medicare, runs through a physician-certification process that never touches a PA request at all. Confusing the two is how a diabetic shoe claim ends up sitting in a "pending authorization" bucket that doesn't exist, or how a custom AFO gets dispensed without the actual PA it needed.

Diabetic shoes: certification, not prior authorization

This is the distinction that causes the most confusion in podiatry billing, so it's worth stating plainly: under traditional Medicare, the Therapeutic Shoes for Persons with Diabetes benefit is administered through a physician-certification process, not a prior-authorization request that a supplier submits and waits to hear back on. There is no "PA number" to obtain before dispensing the shoes. What exists instead is a paperwork chain with its own strict timing rules, and missing any link in it denies the claim just as surely as a missed PA would.

Codes A5512, A5513, and A5514 (inserts) are billed separately from the A5500 shoe code itself, and the benefit generally covers up to three pairs of inserts per calendar year alongside one pair of shoes — confirm current-year unit limits against the active LCD before billing, since coverage articles are revised. The KX modifier attaches only when every criterion in the LCD's medical-necessity section is actually met; appending it as a formality rather than a confirmed fact is exactly the kind of documentation shortcut that draws a post-payment audit.

Commercial payers and many Medicare Advantage plans handle this differently. It's common for a commercial DME policy to require an actual prior-authorization submission before a diabetic shoe or custom orthotic claim is payable — a request filed and awaiting a determination, not a certification statement filed after the fact. ⚠️ The specific commercial payers requiring PA on diabetic shoes, and their documentation thresholds, vary by plan and were not independently confirmed against every major carrier's current policy for this guide; verify per payer before assuming either the Medicare certification model or a PA model applies.

Ankle-foot orthoses and DME beyond shoes

AFOs and KAFOs (HCPCS L1900 through roughly L2999, with related codes for additions and components) sit in a documentation category of their own. Most AFO/KAFO codes require a face-to-face encounter and a written order prior to delivery under CMS's Master List rules — a lighter requirement than prior authorization — but a subset of orthosis codes is separately named on CMS's DMEPOS Required Prior Authorization List, where a claim genuinely cannot be submitted for payment without an affirmed PA decision first. Whether a given AFO code sits in the face-to-face-only category or the full-PA category is code-specific and changes as CMS updates the Required Prior Authorization List, so check the current list by HCPCS code rather than assuming AFOs as a class fall on one side or the other.

Which surgeries need prior authorization — and which don't

Forefoot reconstructive surgery is where payers apply the most scrutiny, and it's not because they doubt the diagnosis — it's because the line between "functionally impairing deformity" and "elective cosmetic improvement" is exactly the line a step-by-step conservative-treatment record is supposed to prove. Commercial policies commonly require prior authorization for bunionectomy and hammertoe/claw-toe correction; several other podiatric procedures are typically exempt because they're rarely elective in the first place.

The emergency exemption rests on the federal prudent-layperson standard: a group health plan or issuer must cover emergency services without requiring prior authorization, evaluated on whether a prudent layperson would reasonably have believed an emergency medical condition existed — not on the eventual diagnosis. For podiatry, that principle applies most clearly to surgical intervention for a diabetic foot infection presenting with systemic signs, rapidly progressing cellulitis, or suspected necrotizing infection, where emergent surgical debridement is the standard of care and delay for a PA determination would itself be the harm. ⚠️ The prudent-layperson rule governs coverage of the emergency encounter itself; it doesn't guarantee every downstream inpatient day or follow-up procedure is automatically PA-exempt, and plan-specific notification requirements (notifying the payer within a set window after emergency admission) still commonly apply — confirm the specific plan's post-emergency notification terms.

Documented as functional
  • Bunionectomy or hammertoe correction charted with dated conservative-treatment attempts, specific modalities tried, and a stated functional impact (gait abnormality, recurrent ulceration risk, shoe-fit failure) clears review faster and survives a payer audit.
Documented as cosmetic-adjacent
  • A chart note built around appearance ("patient doesn't like how the toe looks") without a functional-impairment statement or a dated conservative-treatment trial reads to a reviewer as elective cosmetic surgery — the fastest path to an automatic denial, independent of whether the surgery itself was clinically appropriate.

The prior-authorization timeline, and what to do if care can't wait

The worked timeline below reflects standard prior-authorization mechanics; exact day counts vary by payer type and by whether the request is standard or expedited, so treat the ranges as the general shape of the process rather than a universal countdown that applies to every plan.

  • 1Request submitted. Complete clinical documentation — conservative-treatment dates, imaging, and functional-impact statement for surgical requests; certifying physician statement and Standard Written Order for diabetic shoes — goes to the payer. An incomplete submission generally doesn't start any decision clock; payers are explicit that the timer begins only once the request is complete.
  • 2Standard review. Under CMS's Interoperability and Prior Authorization Final Rule (CMS-0057-F), impacted payers — Medicare Advantage, Medicaid/CHIP fee-for-service and managed care, and Qualified Health Plan issuers on the federal exchange — must decide a standard request within 7 calendar days. ⚠️ CMS-0057-F does not directly regulate commercial group and individual plans outside the FFE exchanges; those payers' standard-review timelines are set by state law or the plan's own policy and can differ, so verify per payer rather than assuming the 7-day federal timeline applies universally.
  • 3Expedited review. When the standard timeline itself could seriously jeopardize the patient's health, an expedited request generally gets a faster decision — commonly cited around 72 hours across payer types — but exact expedited criteria and timing are payer-specific; request expedited review explicitly and document the clinical urgency that justifies it.
  • 4If care is needed before a decision. For true emergencies, the prudent-layperson standard means emergency services proceed without waiting on prior authorization at all. For urgent-but-not-emergent situations where a standard PA clock hasn't resolved in time, the general options are: request expedited review with supporting urgency documentation, escalate to a supervisor or medical director at the payer, or proceed with an ABN on file for a Medicare DMEPOS item so the patient can knowingly accept financial responsibility if the claim is later denied. ⚠️ Whether proceeding without a PA determination is advisable is a case-by-case clinical and financial-risk decision; this is general process guidance, not a recommendation to bypass a required authorization.
  • 5Denial and peer-to-peer. If the request is denied, the ordering or treating clinician can typically request a peer-to-peer conversation with a payer-side physician reviewer before or alongside a formal written appeal.

Peer-to-peer review: how it actually works

A peer-to-peer review is a direct clinical conversation — not a paperwork resubmission — between the physician (or supervising physician for a podiatrist-ordered service, depending on the plan) who ordered the service and a clinical reviewer at the payer, usually a physician, about a denied or pending prior-authorization request. Some payers offer it proactively when they intend to deny a request, as a chance to change the outcome before the formal denial letter goes out; others make it available only after a denial, as a step ahead of or alongside a written appeal.

  • 1Who should be on the call. The ordering or treating clinician makes the clinical argument. Billing and authorization staff can schedule the call, gather records, and sit in to take notes, but the payer's reviewer expects to hear the case from someone who examined the patient.
  • 2What to bring. The same specifics that should have been in the original submission, made concrete: exact conservative-treatment modalities and dates, the radiographic measurement if one applies (hallux valgus angle for bunionectomy), why the case meets the payer's own written medical-necessity criteria rather than a generic restatement of the diagnosis, and any change in the patient's condition since the original request.
  • 3What it resolves and what it doesn't. A successful peer-to-peer can reverse the determination on the spot or shortly after. It doesn't extend timely-filing deadlines on its own, and if it doesn't resolve the denial, the formal written appeal with its own deadline still needs to be filed.

⚠️ Overturn-rate figures for peer-to-peer review vary widely by source, payer, and procedure type; general commentary in the medical-billing and legal-analysis press has cited rates above 50% in some specialties, but this guide could not independently verify a single authoritative figure applicable across payers, so treat any specific percentage as directional rather than a guarantee.

Pro tip

Build the peer-to-peer script around the payer's own published medical-necessity criteria, not the clinical picture in general terms. For a bunionectomy denial, that means walking through the exact conservative-treatment months, naming the shoes/orthotics/injections tried and why they failed, and stating the measured hallux valgus angle from the read imaging — because that's the checklist the reviewer is actually holding, whether or not they say so on the call.

ABNs when a PA is denied or uncertain

An Advance Beneficiary Notice of Noncoverage solves a different problem than a prior-authorization appeal does, and podiatry practices that treat them as interchangeable end up either collecting money they shouldn't or writing off money they didn't have to. A prior-authorization denial is a payer coverage decision that can still be appealed or taken to peer-to-peer at no cost to the patient. An ABN is the Medicare-specific mechanism that lets a patient knowingly choose to proceed with a service the practice believes Medicare is likely to deny as not reasonable and necessary — including a DMEPOS item where a prior-authorization request came back non-affirmed — and accept financial responsibility if that denial happens.

  • 1When it applies. Before delivering a DMEPOS item or service where a PA request was denied, or where the practice has genuine reason to believe Medicare won't cover it, issue the ABN before the item is delivered or the service is rendered — not after the claim is already denied.
  • 2What it has to say. A valid ABN identifies the specific item or service, states the reason the practice believes Medicare may not pay, and gives a genuine cost estimate — a generic, pre-checked, or blanket ABN used for every DME dispense doesn't hold up and doesn't shift liability.
  • 3How it's billed. The claim goes out with a GA modifier to signal a valid, patient-signed ABN is on file. GA and the ABN function as a pair — the modifier without the signed form on file doesn't protect the practice, and the form without the modifier on the claim doesn't either.
  • 4What it doesn't do. An ABN doesn't apply to commercial-payer PA denials the same way — it's a Medicare fee-for-service mechanism. For commercial plans, the analogous patient-financial-responsibility conversation is governed by the plan's own member contract and the practice's own notice-of-non-coverage process, not the Medicare ABN form.

Frequently asked questions

Does Medicare require prior authorization for diabetic shoes?

No. Traditional Medicare covers therapeutic shoes for persons with diabetes through a certification process, not a prior-authorization request submitted for pre-approval. A physician managing the patient's diabetes must certify medical necessity in writing after an in-person visit within 6 months of delivery, and the certification must be signed within 3 months before delivery. Commercial payers are different — many require an actual prior-authorization submission before dispensing custom orthotics or diabetic shoes under their own DME policies, so verify per payer before assuming Medicare's certification rules apply.

Which podiatric surgeries typically require prior authorization from commercial payers?

Elective forefoot reconstructive procedures are the ones payers gate hardest — bunionectomy (CPT 28292–28299) and hammertoe/claw toe correction (CPT 28285–28289) commonly require prior authorization with commercial plans, with medical-necessity criteria built around a documented conservative-treatment trial and, for bunionectomy, a radiographic deformity angle. Urgent and emergent procedures, such as debridement for a limb-threatening diabetic foot infection, are generally exempt from prior authorization under the federal prudent-layperson standard for emergency services, though the exact procedure list and thresholds are payer- and plan-specific.

What's the difference between a prior authorization denial and needing an ABN?

They apply to different situations. A prior-authorization denial is a payer's coverage decision before or during a request, and it can be appealed, including through peer-to-peer review, without the patient paying anything upfront. An Advance Beneficiary Notice of Noncoverage (ABN) is a Medicare-specific, patient-facing document used when the practice believes Medicare is likely to deny a service as not medically necessary — including after a negative prior-authorization affirmation decision on DMEPOS — so the patient can knowingly choose to proceed and accept financial responsibility. Billing with a GA modifier signals a valid ABN is on file and shifts liability to the patient if Medicare denies the claim; without it, the practice generally can't collect from the patient for a denied service.

How does a peer-to-peer review work after a podiatry prior authorization is denied?

A peer-to-peer review is a direct conversation between the ordering or treating clinician — the podiatrist or supervising physician, not billing staff — and a clinical reviewer at the payer, usually a physician, about a denied or pending request. It's typically offered when the payer intends to deny, or can be requested by the practice after a denial, and works best when the clinician brings the same specifics that should have been in the original submission: exact conservative-treatment dates and modalities, radiographic findings, and why the case meets the payer's own written criteria. General guidance and legal-analysis commentary have described peer-to-peer overturn rates exceeding 50% in some specialties, though rates are not uniformly published across all payers and procedure types.
