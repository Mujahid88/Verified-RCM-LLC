Cardiology prior authorization: what requires it and how to avoid delays.

Prior authorization touches most of cardiology's higher-cost services, and a missing or delayed authorization adds directly to AR days before a claim is ever billed, it's one of the most preventable revenue delays in the specialty because the failure happens entirely upstream of the payer.

Reviewed by Phares A., MD · Physiotherapist, CPC (AAPC) · Part of our cardiology billing and coding guide

Key takeaways

  • Stress echo, nuclear imaging, cardiac MRI, elective PCI, EP implants, and most device procedures commonly require authorization.
  • Traditional Medicare and Medicare Advantage aren't the same, MA plans frequently require authorization where traditional Medicare wouldn't.
  • An ABN is not a substitute for authorization, they solve different problems and are triggered by different situations.
  • Start the request the moment a procedure is scheduled, not the week before, incomplete submissions restart the clock.

This is exactly the kind of detail worth confirming at the same time you check the applicable Medicare LCD or commercial policy bulletin, covered in our Medicare LCD coverage guide.

Traditional Medicare versus Medicare Advantage

This distinction gets missed constantly. Traditional Medicare Part B generally applies medical necessity review after the fact, through LCDs, rather than requiring pre-approval before most diagnostic cardiac imaging. Medicare Advantage plans are administered by commercial insurers and frequently layer their own prior-authorization requirements on top, sometimes for services traditional Medicare wouldn't gate at all. Checking "the patient has Medicare" isn't enough; confirm whether it's traditional Medicare or a specific MA plan, because the authorization requirements can be genuinely different.

Prior authorization is not an ABN

These solve two different problems and get conflated often enough to be worth stating plainly. Prior authorization is the payer's pre-approval that a specific service will be covered, obtained before the service happens. An Advance Beneficiary Notice is a Medicare-specific form that tells the patient a service might not be covered and that they may be financially responsible if it isn't, used when medical necessity itself is uncertain, particularly for repeat advanced imaging without a new qualifying diagnosis. An ABN doesn't substitute for authorization where authorization is actually required, and obtaining authorization doesn't eliminate the need for an ABN in a genuinely uncertain-necessity situation.

Where the delay actually comes from

The process itself is straightforward, verify eligibility, gather the clinical documentation the payer's policy requires, submit through the payer's portal, fax, or phone line, but delays compound at each step: an incomplete initial submission restarts the review clock, and payers reject or pend requests that don't include the exact clinical documentation their specific policy requires, which is often more granular than what the ordering physician's note naturally contains. The fix is starting the request at the point of scheduling, not the week before, and building the payer's specific documentation checklist into the request itself rather than submitting and waiting to find out what's missing.

Pro tip

Build a per-payer authorization checklist for your five highest-volume procedures, typically stress echo, nuclear imaging, elective PCI, device implant, and ablation, listing exactly what documentation each payer's policy requires. Attaching this checklist at the scheduling step, rather than discovering requirements one denial at a time, is the single highest-leverage change most cardiology practices can make to authorization-related AR days.

Frequently asked questions

Does traditional Medicare require prior authorization for cardiac imaging?

Traditional Medicare Part B generally does not require prior authorization for most diagnostic cardiac imaging the way commercial payers and Medicare Advantage plans do, though it applies medical necessity review through LCDs after the fact rather than pre-approval before the service. Medicare Advantage plans, run by commercial insurers, frequently do require prior auth even for services traditional Medicare wouldn't, always check the specific plan, not just whether the patient has Medicare.

What's the difference between prior authorization and an ABN?

Prior authorization is a payer's pre-approval that a service will be covered before it's performed. An Advance Beneficiary Notice (ABN) is a separate Medicare-specific form telling the patient a service might not be covered and that they may be financially responsible, used when medical necessity is uncertain, not as a substitute for obtaining authorization where one is actually required.

How far in advance should we start a prior authorization request?

As soon as the procedure is scheduled, not the week before, turnaround varies by payer and by procedure complexity, and incomplete initial submissions restart the clock. Building the authorization request into the same workflow step as scheduling, rather than a separate follow-up task, is what actually prevents last-minute delays.

Verify before billing. Prior authorization requirements vary by payer, plan, and change periodically. This page reflects standard industry practice and is provided for general education, confirm current requirements against your specific payer contracts before scheduling a procedure.
