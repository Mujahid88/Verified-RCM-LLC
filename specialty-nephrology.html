Nephrology billing and coding: the complete 2026 guide.

Nephrology billing runs on two things most other specialties don't deal with at all: a monthly bundled capitation payment that replaces standard E/M, and a Medicare coverage policy for hemodialysis frequency that isn't national — it's written and maintained separately by at least seven different Medicare Administrative Contractors. Every competitor guide we reviewed mentions that bundling and MAC-specific coverage exist; none of them name the actual indicator values that decide whether a bundled code can be appealed, or the actual LCD document IDs a biller needs to pull. This guide does both, along with the full ESRD capitation code logic, vascular access billing, CKD staging specificity, and the denial patterns specific to this specialty.

Reviewed by Phares A., MD · Physiotherapist, CPC (AAPC) · Updated August 2026

Key takeaways

  • The ESRD monthly capitation payment (MCP) and session-based dialysis codes are not interchangeable. Billing both for the same established ESRD patient in the same calendar month is a hard bundling conflict, not a gray area a modifier is likely to clear.
  • The Frequency of Hemodialysis LCD is MAC-specific, not national. At least seven MACs each publish their own version — Palmetto GBA (L34575), Novitas (L35014), CGS (L37575), WPS (L37537), Noridian (L37502), First Coast (L37564), and Wellpoint Federal (L37475) — and "check your LCD" is meaningless without naming which one applies to your practice.
  • N18.6 (ESRD) is not just a sicker N18.5. It requires chart documentation of dialysis dependence; defaulting to N18.9 unspecified when a specific stage is supported understates the case and the risk-adjusted revenue tied to it.
  • ESA and IV iron denials are usually a system-matching failure, not a coverage gap. The authorization was obtained; the claim just didn't match it on code, date range, or NDC — that's a resubmission fix, not a medical necessity appeal.

Nephrology billing at a glance

Four structural facts drive most of nephrology's denial volume and underpayment: the ESRD monthly capitation payment replaces standard E/M once a patient is on maintenance dialysis, and the two code families are mutually exclusive; hemodialysis frequency coverage is written per-MAC rather than nationally; CKD staging specificity is a hard input into both medical necessity and risk-adjusted revenue; and vascular access billing carries its own bundling and global-period logic separate from the dialysis codes themselves. What changed in nephrology billing for 2026

Four items are the ones nephrology practices and billing teams need to actually operationalize for 2026, rather than just note in passing.

  • 1ESRD Prospective Payment System base rate update. CMS's CY2026 ESRD PPS final rule (CMS-1830-F) set the per-treatment base rate at $281.71, up from $273.82 in CY2025 — roughly a 2.1–2.2% increase before each facility's wage-index adjustment. The rule also finalized the scheduled end of the ESRD Treatment Choices (ETC) Model as of December 31, 2025, with the Kidney Care Choices (KCC) and Increasing Organ Transplant Access (IOTA) models continuing as the value-based alternatives — relevant to any practice with value-based nephrology contracts, separate from standard fee-for-service billing. ⚠️ This figure is corroborated across multiple independent secondary sources (health-policy consultancy summaries and hospital-association bulletins reporting on the final rule) rather than pulled directly from CMS's own fact sheet, which returned an access error to direct fetch during this build; confirm your facility's specific wage-index-adjusted rate against the CMS ESRD PPS Pricer tool or the final rule itself before budgeting off it.
  • 2Kidney transplant evaluation coding. Industry reporting describes continued refinement of how pre-transplant evaluation and transplant candidacy work — cardiovascular clearance, infectious disease screening, psychosocial assessment coordination, and UNOS waitlist documentation — is coded and reimbursed, distinct from the routine E/M visits it has historically been bundled into. ⚠️ This build could not independently verify specific new CPT code numbers for 2026 transplant evaluation services against a primary AMA or CMS source this session; verify current-year transplant evaluation code additions or revisions against your CPT Professional edition or MAC bulletin before building them into a charge master. Separately, and worth knowing even though it doesn't change fee-for-service billing directly: the Increasing Organ Transplant Access (IOTA) Model, a mandatory six-year CMS Innovation Center model that began July 1, 2025, incentivizes kidney transplant hospitals in selected regions on transplant volume and post-transplant outcomes, and any nephrology practice referring into a hospital under that model may see downstream changes in how quickly and how the hospital prioritizes transplant workups.
  • 3New short-duration remote physiologic monitoring code. For CY2026, CMS finalized a new RPM device-supply code — reported in industry coding-update sources as CPT 99445 — billable when 2 to 15 days of physiologic data are transmitted in a 30-day period, reimbursed at parity with the existing 16-day-threshold code, 99454. This matters specifically for nephrology because pre-dialysis CKD patients on blood-pressure or weight-monitoring protocols frequently generate fewer than 16 days of usable transmission in a given month; under the prior all-or-nothing 16-day threshold, that data simply wasn't billable at all. The rest of the RPM family (99453 initial setup, 99457/99458 clinical staff time) keeps its existing structure. ⚠️ This build confirmed the new code number and threshold against two independent industry coding-update sources; the exact CPT descriptor could not be confirmed against the AMA's CPT Professional edition (no CPT connector exists, and CMS.gov's own RPM code list returned an access error to direct fetch), so verify the exact descriptor and code number in your current-year CPT book before loading it into a charge master.
  • 4Virtual supervision of home dialysis training. The physician-supervision requirement for home dialysis patient training is governed under 42 CFR 494.100 ("Condition: care at home"), and was relaxed during the COVID-19 public health emergency to allow virtual rather than in-person oversight. Industry and MAC guidance describe this flexibility continuing into 2026 rather than reverting to strict in-person supervision. ⚠️ This build could not independently confirm, against CMS's own current rule text, whether the mechanism is now a standing permanent regulatory change as opposed to a continued temporary extension — CMS.gov's regulation pages returned an access error to direct fetch during this build. Before restructuring a home dialysis training program's staffing model around virtual supervision, pull the current text of 42 CFR 494.100 from the eCFR directly or confirm with your MAC.

The pattern across all four: directionally, this is what's moving in nephrology reimbursement and coverage policy for 2026, and the base rate and RPM code changes are now corroborated across multiple independent secondary sources even where this build's automated access to CMS.gov's own primary pages was blocked. The transplant-coding and virtual-supervision items remain more open; treat those two as directional rather than confirmed to the code number or citation (see Sources and verification below).

CKD E/M vs. ESRD monthly capitation: the mutual-exclusivity rule

Pre-dialysis CKD care bills like any other outpatient chronic disease management: established-patient E/M codes 99202–99215, selected on either total time or medical decision making, not both together. A stage 3b patient coming in for medication titration and lab review is a standard MDM- or time-based E/M visit, same as managing any other chronic condition — nothing nephrology-specific about the code selection itself.

Where nephrology-specific judgment enters is which MDM level the visit actually supports. A CKD patient on a stable ACE inhibitor or ARB with routine lab monitoring is typically low to moderate complexity — one chronic, stable condition, prescription drug management at moderate risk. A patient being titrated onto or off an SGLT2 inhibitor, started on a potassium binder for hyperkalemia, or being worked up for a rising creatinine against a new medication is a different picture: multiple chronic conditions with exacerbation, prescription drug management with a genuine risk of adverse effects, and often an independent interpretation of labs or imaging the note has to document explicitly, not just reference. The failure mode we see constantly mirrors cardiology's: the physician does 99215-level work managing a decompensating stage 4 patient, but the note only supports 99214 because the complexity that was actually present — the specific risk being managed, the specific data reviewed and independently interpreted — wasn't captured in writing.

Where time is the chosen pathway instead of MDM, the standard total-time thresholds apply the same way they do in any other specialty: 99212 at 10–19 minutes, 99213 at 20–29 minutes, 99214 at 30–39 minutes, and 99215 at 40–54 minutes of total time on the date of the encounter, including same-day chart review, ordering, and documentation, not just the face-to-face portion. CKD visits lend themselves to time-based billing more often than a quick-turnaround specialty would, because reviewing a trending eGFR curve, a 24-hour urine result, or coordinating with a dietitian or a transplant center genuinely happens outside the room and counts toward the total — but only if it's documented as time spent, not left implicit.

That changes the moment a patient starts maintenance dialysis. Monthly management of dialysis-related care shifts to the age-banded ESRD monthly capitation payment (MCP) codes, and once that shift happens, the MCP is not billed alongside a separate standard E/M for the same dialysis-related care in the same calendar month. The MCP is a bundled payment that's meant to cover the full month of related management — not a floor that a same-month E/M can add to. The full code-by-code breakdown of exactly how visit count and age band combine to select the right code, along with the full-month and less-than-full-month mechanics, is in our ESRD monthly capitation payment billing: 90951-90970 explained guide.

The single most common MCP documentation error we see is a visit count that's short by one. The MCP code family is banded by the number of face-to-face physician visits furnished in the month — four-or-more, two-to-three, one, or zero — and each band pays at a different rate. If the physician actually saw the patient four times in the month but only three visits have a dated, signed note establishing a face-to-face encounter, the claim has to be coded at the lower band, and that's revenue that's gone permanently, not something an appeal fixes after the fact, because the documentation gap is the actual problem, not a coding error.

Timely filing runs from the end of the service period, not the first visit. Because an MCP code represents a full calendar month of management, the claim generally can't be finalized and submitted until the month has closed and the visit count for that month is known — submitting mid-month based on an assumed visit count risks under- or over-stating the band. Build the claims-release workflow around month-end close, with the timely-filing clock tracked from the period's end date rather than from the first visit of the month, so a delayed month-end reconciliation process doesn't quietly eat into the filing window.

Worked example. A 20-year-old-or-older in-facility patient is seen by the nephrologist on 4/2, 4/9, 4/16, and 4/23 — four visits, which should support 90960. But the 4/16 encounter was a brief hallway conversation about a medication refill that was never documented as a dated, signed face-to-face visit note addressing the required MCP visit elements (vascular access status, fluid and volume status, nutritional status, adequacy of dialysis, and the plan of care). Only three visits have supporting documentation, so the claim has to be coded 90961 (2–3 visits) instead of 90960 (4+ visits) — a difference that repeats every month the pattern continues, for every patient it happens to, and never shows up as a denial because the claim pays cleanly at the lower rate.

Do
  • Bill standard E/M (99202–99215) for pre-dialysis CKD management, selected on time or MDM, same as any other chronic disease visit.
  • Shift to the age-banded MCP the month maintenance dialysis genuinely starts, with a chart note establishing the transition date.
  • Document a genuinely unrelated problem as its own distinct assessment and plan if billing a separate E/M alongside an established patient's MCP month.
Don't
  • Don't bill a standard E/M for dialysis-related management in a month the MCP was also billed for the same patient.
  • Don't assume a visit happened for MCP purposes because the patient was on the unit — it has to be a personally furnished, documented face-to-face assessment.
  • Don't let a mid-month dialysis start default to a full-month code; use the less-than-full-month codes and the correct From/To dates instead.
Pro tip

Build a running visit-count tracker per patient per month that closes out on the last calendar day, not a retrospective count pulled from the schedule at claim time. A missed or undocumented visit caught on the 28th can still be added to the chart before the month closes; caught after submission, it's a lost reimbursement tier with no path back.

ESRD monthly capitation payment codes: 90951–90970

"Face-to-face visit" is a defined term for MCP purposes, not a loose synonym for any contact with the patient. It has to be a complete assessment personally furnished by the billing physician or a qualified non-physician practitioner (nurse practitioner, physician assistant, or clinical nurse specialist) acting within their scope, covering the elements a MAC's LCD and CMS manual guidance expect: vascular or peritoneal access evaluation, volume and fluid status, nutritional status, adequacy of dialysis, and an updated plan of care. A medication refill conversation, a phone call, or a visit conducted entirely by dialysis unit nursing staff without the physician or NPP personally assessing the patient does not count toward the visit total, however clinically useful it was. Where a non-physician practitioner furnishes some or all of the month's visits under a collaborating physician, follow your MAC's specific split-billing and supervision requirements for who's actually entitled to report the MCP for that month — this varies by contractor and by practice structure, and defaulting to "the physician bills it regardless" is a common, avoidable error in group practices where NPPs carry a meaningful share of the panel.

The MCP code family is organized along two axes: patient age band, and the number of face-to-face physician visits furnished during the month. Twelve codes (90951–90962) cover a full month of in-facility (or non-home) maintenance dialysis management, split across four age bands crossed with three visit-count tiers.

The remaining four codes, 90967–90970, cover less-than-full-month ESRD service — one per age band, billed per day rather than per month. The claim reports a From date and a To date spanning the days actually managed, and units are billed per day within that range, not as a flat monthly unit — get the date span wrong and the per-day unit count on the claim won't reconcile with what the payer expects for a partial month.

One rule sits above all of this and is the source of a disproportionate share of MCP-related denials: only one MCP service is payable per patient per calendar month, regardless of how many physicians were actually involved in the patient's care that month. If two nephrologists in the same or different groups each bill an MCP code for overlapping periods within the same month — a mid-month transfer that isn't cleanly split with correct From/To dates, or simple duplicate billing during a coverage handoff — one of those claims denies as a duplicate, and untangling which physician is entitled to which portion of the month requires exact date documentation on both sides. Coordinate the date split before either claim goes out; don't let it become a post-denial reconciliation project.

Coverage arrangements complicate the one-MCP-per-month rule further. Nephrology groups routinely rotate call coverage, and a patient's usual physician being on vacation or at a conference for part of the month doesn't create two separate MCP-eligible physicians — it's still one patient, one month, one MCP. Where a genuine substitute physician arrangement is in place, HCPCS modifiers Q5 (services furnished by a substitute physician under a reciprocal billing arrangement) and Q6 (services furnished by a locum tenens physician) let the regular physician's practice continue billing under the regular physician's name and NPI for visits the substitute actually performed, rather than requiring the substitute to bill separately and split the month's single MCP claim. Confirm your specific payer's reciprocal-billing and locum tenens time limits (Medicare caps continuous locum tenens coverage for a single absence) before relying on this arrangement for an extended leave.

  • 3Nutritional status. Albumin trend, dietary counseling or referral needs, and any intervention for malnutrition risk specific to the dialysis population.

A visit note missing more than one or two of these elements is exactly the kind of documentation an auditor flags as insufficient to count toward the month's visit total, even when the encounter genuinely happened — the fix is a structured visit template that prompts for all five, not a narrative note relying on the physician to remember every element every time.

Worked split-month example. A patient transfers from Dr. A's group to Dr. B's group on 6/15, mid-month. Dr. A furnished two documented face-to-face visits before the transfer (6/2 and 6/9); Dr. B furnished one after (6/22). Neither physician has a full month of management, so neither bills a full-month code (90960/90961/90962). Instead, each bills the appropriate less-than-full-month code for their own patient-days: Dr. A reports 90970 with a From date of 6/1 and a To date of 6/15 (or the last day of their management), and Dr. B reports 90970 with a From date of 6/16 (or their first day of management) and a To date of 6/30, with per-day units matching each physician's actual managed date range. If both offices instead each bill a full-month 90961 for overlapping dates, one claim denies as duplicate and the other risks recoupment once the payer's system flags the overlapping date span — coordinate the exact handoff date between practices before either claim is submitted, not after a CO-18 denial arrives.

Session-based (non-ESRD) hemodialysis billing: 90935–90947

Session-based dialysis codes exist for a different clinical population than the MCP codes: acute kidney injury, inpatient dialysis, or any dialysis patient who isn't an established outpatient ESRD patient under a monthly capitation arrangement. These codes are billed per session rather than per month.

Acute kidney injury is the population where this distinction gets tested most often, because an AKI patient can look clinically identical to an ESRD patient on the unit floor while being coded entirely differently. CMS extended Medicare payment for renal dialysis services to AKI patients receiving dialysis in a certified ESRD facility, including home dialysis; those services are billed and paid under the AKI benefit — a separate payment track from both the ESRD MCP and standard inpatient DRG payment — not folded into a hospital stay's DRG when performed at a certified dialysis facility. The clinical determination of whether a patient has genuinely progressed from AKI to ESRD (irreversible kidney failure requiring ongoing renal replacement therapy) is what actually triggers the shift from session-based billing to MCP eligibility, not an arbitrary day count; document that determination explicitly in the chart when it happens, because it's the pivot point the payer will look for if the code family changes mid-course for the same patient.

The diagnosis coding has to track that same distinction. We validated the acute kidney failure category live against the FY2026 ICD-10-CM code set: N17.0 (with tubular necrosis), N17.1 (with acute cortical necrosis), N17.2 (with medullary necrosis), N17.8 (other), and N17.9 (unspecified) are all confirmed billable, while the parent category N17 is a header only. An acute-on-chronic presentation — AKI superimposed on established CKD — should carry both the specific N17 code for the acute process and the specific N18 stage code for the underlying chronic disease, not one or the other; a chart that only documents "worsening kidney function" without distinguishing which process is acute and which is the chronic baseline makes it hard to defend session-based billing over MCP billing (or vice versa) if a payer questions which code family actually fit the encounter.

Worked example. An AKI patient in the ICU starts a hemodialysis session; the physician evaluates the patient once at the start, confirms the prescription, and the session runs to completion without further physician reassessment — that's 90935. The next day, the same patient becomes hemodynamically unstable mid-session, requiring the physician to reassess twice more, adjust the ultrafiltration rate, and document each reassessment separately — that's 90937, and the note has to actually reflect each evaluation with its own timestamp and clinical reasoning, not a single end-of-session summary implying multiple touches occurred. Coders defaulting to 90937 as the "safer, higher" code without that documentation are coding to what's billable rather than what's supported, which is exactly the pattern payers audit for in critical-care-adjacent dialysis billing.

These are physician professional-service codes; they don't capture the facility's own charge for the dialysis session itself (equipment, supplies, nursing time), which the hospital or dialysis facility bills separately under its own claim. A common point of confusion for practices new to inpatient nephrology consult work is assuming the professional session code somehow needs to reconcile with the facility's charge — it doesn't; they're two independent claims to two potentially different payers or payer components, and a mismatch between them isn't a coding error on the physician side.

Mixing session-based codes with the MCP in the same calendar month for the same established ESRD patient's related dialysis care is a hard bundling conflict, not a documentation nuance a modifier is likely to resolve — the two code families represent different payment models for different clinical populations, and billing both reads to a payer as double billing for the same period of care. Home dialysis billing: peritoneal dialysis, home hemodialysis, and telehealth

Home dialysis MCP billing uses the 90963–90966 age-banded codes shown above for full-month management, regardless of whether the modality is peritoneal dialysis or home hemodialysis — the code selection logic doesn't split by modality, only by age band and by full-month versus less-than-full-month status (90967–90970 again apply for partial months).

Patient training for home dialysis is billed separately from the monthly management codes, under a distinct pair of training codes reported in industry coding references as 90989 (dialysis training, patient and helper where applicable, any mode, completed course) and 90993 (dialysis training, per session, course not completed, typically capped around 25 sessions). ⚠️ These code numbers are corroborated across multiple independent secondary coding sources but were not independently confirmed against the AMA's CPT Professional edition this session, since no CPT connector exists; verify current descriptors before billing. Training documentation has to reflect actual sessions delivered and whether the patient (and caregiver, where applicable) completed the full course or training is ongoing across multiple sessions — billing the completed-course code before training has actually concluded is a documentation mismatch that's easy for a payer to catch against the training log, and billing session-based training indefinitely without ever closing it out as completed invites a medical-necessity question about why training hasn't converted to independent home management.

CMS's physician supervision requirement for home dialysis training, governed under 42 CFR 494.100 ("Condition: care at home"), can be satisfied virtually rather than requiring in-person presence — a flexibility that started as a pandemic-era waiver and, per current MAC and industry guidance, appears to be continuing into 2026 rather than reverting to strict in-person supervision. ⚠️ This build could not independently confirm whether the current mechanism is a standing permanent regulatory change versus a continued temporary extension, against a primary CMS source this session (CMS.gov access was blocked to automated fetch); confirm the current rule text at 42 CFR 494.100 via the eCFR or your MAC's published guidance before restructuring supervision staffing around it.

Equipment and supplies for home dialysis — the cycler or hemodialysis machine, tubing, dialysate, and related consumables — are billed by the certified dialysis facility as part of the Medicare Home Dialysis Supplies and Equipment benefit, not by the physician alongside the MCP and not through standard DME supplier channels the way a wheelchair or CPAP machine would be. Practices that treat home dialysis equipment as a routine DME billing question end up routing claims to the wrong payer path entirely; the facility relationship, not the physician's own billing, is what carries the equipment and supply claim.

Peritoneal dialysis-specific considerations. PD billing follows the same 90963–90966 age-banded structure as home hemodialysis, but the clinical monitoring content differs: peritoneal equilibration testing, exit-site and tunnel infection surveillance, and catheter-related complication management (documented with Z49.02, encounter for fitting/adjustment of a peritoneal dialysis catheter, when the visit's primary purpose is catheter-related rather than routine monthly management) are PD-specific documentation elements a chart reviewer will look for when validating that a month's MCP visit actually addressed the modality in use. A note that reads as if it were written for a hemodialysis patient — referencing vascular access instead of the peritoneal catheter and dialysate exchange pattern — is a red flag on audit even when the code itself is correct.

Pro tip

Close out training billing the same week a patient converts to independent home dialysis, not months later at year-end review. A completed-course code (90989) billed weeks after training actually finished, with no interim session-based claims documenting the intervening period, creates a documentation gap a payer's system can flag on its own — the training log and the claim date should track each other closely enough that a reviewer can follow the story without asking for clarification.

The genuinely unresolved question in this area is the telehealth modifier on 90966, the adult home hemodialysis full-month MCP code, when the physician's monthly assessment components are delivered via telehealth rather than in person. Whether a 95 or GT modifier applies, whether it's required at all, or whether the code is simply billed without a telehealth modifier depends on the specific MAC and the specific payer's telehealth billing policy, and this varies enough across contractors that stating one answer as a national rule would be actively misleading. ⚠️ This is a call-your-MAC item, not a settled national rule — confirm current telehealth modifier requirements for 90966 directly with your Medicare Administrative Contractor's provider contact center before submitting, and don't assume a modifier convention from one region's guidance applies to another's.

Vascular access procedures: fistula, graft, angioplasty, thrombectomy

Vascular access is nephrology's most procedurally dense billing category, because access creation, maintenance, and salvage are billed through entirely separate code families with their own bundling logic, distinct from the dialysis codes themselves.

Access creation. Open surgical creation of an autogenous arteriovenous fistula is billed through a small family of codes in the 36818–36821 range, and the distinction inside that range is technique, not just anatomic location: 36818 and 36819 are vein-transposition procedures in the upper arm (cephalic-to-brachial and basilic-to-brachial transposition respectively), 36820 is forearm vein transposition, and 36821 is the direct anastomosis — the classic radiocephalic "Cimino" fistula — created through a single incision without transposing the vein. All four descriptors include completion ultrasound and completion contrast angiography when performed as part of the same operative session, so those imaging steps aren't separately billable on top of the creation code. Nonautogenous graft placement uses a separate pair of codes in the 36825–36830 range, split by whether the graft is placed directly or requires a more extensive dissection. ⚠️ This creation-code breakdown is corroborated across multiple independent secondary coding sources but was not independently confirmed against the AMA's CPT Professional edition this session. These procedures are typically performed by vascular or transplant surgery, not nephrology itself, but nephrology billing teams need to recognize them to correctly sequence claims and avoid duplicate billing when the practice also bills related access-maintenance work.

Pro tip

When a session escalates — a diagnostic fistulogram (36901) reveals a stenosis, so the physician proceeds to angioplasty in the same session — the claim reports only the escalated code (36902), never 36901 plus 36902 for the same circuit. This is the single most common vascular-access coding error we see, and it isn't really an NCCI question at all; the escalated code's own descriptor already includes the diagnostic access and imaging work of the base code, so billing both is reporting the same work twice regardless of what any bundling edit says.

Temporary and tunneled catheter access. When a patient needs dialysis before a fistula or graft matures, or as a bridge during access complications, a tunneled cuffed catheter (the PermCath and Tesio-type devices) is placed instead. Industry coding references report this as 36558 (insertion of a tunneled centrally inserted central venous catheter, without subcutaneous port or pump, age 5 or older), typically reported with the imaging guidance add-on 76937 (ultrasound guidance for vascular access, with permanent image documentation and a separately documented interpretation) when ultrasound is used for the puncture. ⚠️ 36558 and 76937 are corroborated across multiple independent secondary coding sources but were not independently confirmed against the AMA's CPT Professional edition this session; verify current descriptors and any bundling relationship with the vascular access family above before billing. These catheter-placement codes are billed separately from, not folded into, the 36901–36909 access-maintenance ladder, because they represent creating a new access route rather than intervening on an existing fistula or graft circuit.

Documentation for every code in the 36901–36909 family has to support the specific intervention billed, not just that "an access procedure was performed": the operative note needs to identify the circuit studied, the specific finding (stenosis location and percentage, thrombus extent, stent or embolization target), and the imaging obtained, because the code selected is a direct function of what was actually done to the circuit, not what was attempted. A note that documents an attempted angioplasty without confirming technical success still supports the higher code if the attempt was genuinely performed and documented, but auditors specifically look for vague operative notes that don't tie the billed code to a described, imaged finding.

Ligation or banding of an arteriovenous fistula used for dialysis access — typically performed to manage steal syndrome (vascular insufficiency to the hand distal to the access), aneurysmal degeneration with skin compromise or rupture risk, high-output cardiac failure from an oversized fistula, or a failed/no-longer-needed access left in place after a transplant — is reported with 37607, a standalone code separate from the 36901–36909 endovascular family. The clinical indication driving the ligation should be documented explicitly, both because it supports medical necessity and because it distinguishes a therapeutic ligation from an incidental finding noted during an unrelated procedure.

Thrombosed access is a same-day-scheduling problem more than a coding problem, but it has one billing consequence worth flagging: a thrombosed fistula or graft presenting emergently and requiring same-day mechanical thrombectomy (36904–36906) is billed exactly the same way as an elective, scheduled procedure on the same codes — there's no separate emergent-access code family. What changes is the documentation burden around medical necessity and, frequently, the E/M-versus-procedure question, since a thrombosed access patient often needs an urgent clinical assessment (volume status, missed dialysis sessions, signs of infection) before the intervention that goes well beyond a routine pre-procedure check and can independently support modifier 25 on a same-day E/M when it's documented as such.

Laterality and bilateral access history. A meaningful share of long-term dialysis patients have exhausted one arm's access options and moved to the contralateral side, or have a history of access attempts on both. Every claim in the 36901–36909 and creation/graft families should carry RT or LT identifying which extremity was treated — not because the code itself changes, but because a payer with prior claims showing access history on both sides will often pend a claim lacking laterality for clarification rather than paying it cleanly, which adds an avoidable delay to what would otherwise be a straightforward claim.

Most codes in the 36901–36909 family carry a short (typically 0-day) global period rather than a major-surgery 90-day global, which is the key fact governing same-day E/M billing: a significant, separately identifiable E/M performed the same day as the access procedure can support modifier 25, but only where the note independently justifies the E/M beyond the standard pre-procedure assessment already bundled into the procedure code itself. ⚠️ Confirm the specific global-period indicator for the exact code billed in the CMS Physician Fee Schedule Look-Up Tool rather than assuming 0-day applies uniformly across the whole family — this build states it as the general pattern for the family based on standard industry documentation, not as a code-by-code confirmed value pulled fresh this session.

Coverage for non-invasive vascular access studies — used to assess fistula or graft function without an invasive procedure — is addressed specifically in WPS Insurance Corporation's LCD L35751, "Non-Invasive Peripheral Venous Vascular and Hemodialysis Access Studies" (effective 02/26/2026, confirmed directly against the CMS Coverage Database for this build). If your MAC is WPS, that's the specific article to pull for covered-diagnosis and frequency detail; if it isn't, don't assume the same coverage terms apply, since we found no equivalent article under this exact title from the other MACs in our research for this page. These non-invasive studies — duplex ultrasound of the access site, typically — are ordered to evaluate suspected stenosis, assess flow volume, or screen a maturing fistula before it's used clinically, distinct from the invasive fistulogram performed as part of an interventional 36901–36906 procedure. Billing an invasive access procedure's imaging work under a non-invasive study code, or vice versa, is a code-family mismatch a payer's system typically catches on its own, independent of any medical-necessity question.

CKD staging is not static, and the code on file should track disease progression visit to visit, not carry forward indefinitely from the initial diagnosis. A patient coded N18.31 (stage 3a) eighteen months ago whose eGFR has since dropped into the stage 4 range needs the diagnosis updated at the point that's confirmed in the chart, not left at the old stage because "that's what's in the system." Recoding to reflect genuine progression isn't upcoding — it's the same accuracy principle as any other specificity requirement, and it's the mechanism by which risk-adjusted revenue is supposed to track a genuinely sicker patient population over time. The reverse also happens and matters equally: a transplant recipient's function improving out of failure range should be recoded down, not left at N18.5 or N18.6 out of inertia.

The N18.30 vs. N18.31 vs. N18.32 distinction is the one every competitor guide mentions in passing; the pattern that actually costs more revenue is N18.5 vs. N18.6. N18.6 is not simply "a more severe N18.5" — it specifically represents end-stage renal disease, and coding it requires the chart to actually establish dialysis dependence or a physician's explicit ESRD determination, not just a very low eGFR. Conversely, coding N18.5 for a patient who has clearly started maintenance dialysis understates the clinical picture and the case.

Defaulting to N18.9 unspecified when the chart supports a specific stage carries a cost beyond simple documentation cleanliness: stage 4, stage 5, and ESRD generally carry materially higher risk weight than an unspecified CKD code under CMS-HCC and similar risk-adjustment models used by Medicare Advantage and some ACO arrangements, so an unspecified code silently understates a patient's documented risk and the revenue tied to it in a risk-adjusted contract. ⚠️ Exact HCC category numbers and risk weights vary by model version and payment year and were not independently re-verified against the current CMS-HCC model documentation for this build; confirm the current mapping against your payer's risk-adjustment methodology rather than relying on a specific category number from a secondary source.

Modifiers that decide nephrology claims

Nephrology's modifier logic is narrower than a procedure-heavy specialty like cardiology, but the four that matter carry outsized weight because they sit at exactly the points where the specialty's payment models collide: a procedure day next to a capitated month, a diagnostic study next to an intervention on the same circuit, and an interpreted image next to equipment the practice doesn't own.

25
  • Significant, separately identifiable E/M on the same day as a minor (typically 0-day global) vascular access procedure.
  • The note has to independently justify the E/M beyond the standard pre-procedure assessment already bundled into the access code — don't append 25 just because an E/M and a procedure share a date.
59 / XE, XS, XP, XU
  • Distinct dialysis-related services genuinely separate by encounter, structure, practitioner, or non-overlapping timing — not the MCP-versus-session pairing, which is a hard bundling conflict rather than a modifier-clearable one.
  • Prefer the specific X-modifier over generic 59; XS (separate structure) covers most defensible vascular-access unbundling, since it's usually anatomic (a genuinely separate access site).
26 / TC
  • 26 — professional component only, for a physician who interprets a vascular access or non-invasive study performed on equipment or at a facility the practice doesn't own.
  • TC — technical component only, billed by whoever owns the equipment and performs the study.
RT / LT
  • Laterality on access-site procedures — right or left upper extremity — relevant whenever a patient has (or has had) access sites on both sides, or when a payer requires laterality to adjudicate the claim correctly.
  • Missing laterality on a claim where the patient has bilateral access history is a common cause of a claim pending for clarification rather than paying cleanly.
Do
  • Document the distinct reason for a same-day E/M beyond the standard pre-procedure assessment before appending 25.
  • Default to the specific X-modifier over generic 59 wherever the distinction genuinely maps to one.
  • Append RT/LT on every access-site claim as a matter of routine, not just for patients with a known bilateral history.
Don't
  • Don't append 59 or an X-modifier to the MCP-versus-session pairing; that's a code-family selection error, not a bundling edit a modifier fixes.
  • Don't bill the global fee (no 26 or TC) for a study or intervention read on equipment or at a facility the practice doesn't own.
  • Don't treat modifier use as a claim-clearing tool rather than a documentation-driven reflection of what actually happened.

26/TC in practice. A non-invasive vascular access study is performed at an outpatient imaging center the nephrology practice doesn't own, and the interpreting physician is part of the nephrology group — that's 26 only. If the same practice instead owns its own vascular lab, employs the sonographer, and its own physician interprets the study, it's billed globally, with neither 26 nor TC appended. Billing the global fee when a separate facility already billed TC for the same study and date is the same duplicate-claim trap covered in cardiology, and it's just as easy for a payer to catch in nephrology.

RT/LT in practice. A patient with a failed left forearm fistula and a newer right upper-arm fistula presents for a fistulogram and angioplasty. The claim for that session should carry RT to identify the treated side; if the same patient returns the following month for intervention on the left-side remnant (for ligation, say), that claim carries LT. Submitting either without a laterality modifier when the payer's history shows procedures on both sides is a common, avoidable cause of a claim pending for additional information instead of adjudicating cleanly on first pass.

A worked scenario that shows why the 25 threshold matters: a patient comes in for a scheduled fistulogram and angioplasty (36902) and, during the same visit, the physician also evaluates a new complaint of worsening dyspnea unrelated to the access itself, orders labs, and adjusts a diuretic. That's a significant, separately identifiable service — document the dyspnea workup as its own assessment and plan, distinct from the standard pre-procedure history and exam already bundled into 36902, and modifier 25 on the E/M is defensible. Contrast that with a note that only restates the access-site history and confirms the patient is stable for the procedure — that's the bundled pre-procedure assessment, not a separately billable E/M, and appending 25 to it is exactly the pattern payers flag.

NCCI edits and MUE limits in nephrology

This is the section every competitor guide we reviewed (Neolytix, GoMedicalBilling, RCM Matter, ClaimsXperts, MedCloudMD, and Transcure among them) treats as an afterthought — each names that bundling exists between dialysis code families, but none of them give the actual indicator value that decides whether an override is even possible. Two CMS-published values control that, and neither is a guess.

The NCCI modifier indicator attached to a Column 1/Column 2 code pair:

  • 0Never bypassable. No modifier, however well-documented, overrides it. If the pair carries a 0, the second code isn't separately payable, full stop.
  • 1Bypassable with documentation. An NCCI-associated modifier (59 or the specific X-modifier) can override the edit, but only where the record actually shows the second service was distinct.
  • 9Edit deleted. The pair no longer applies.

The MUE Adjudication Indicator (MAI) attached to a per-code, per-day unit cap:

  • 1Claim-line edit. Units above the cap deny that line; a genuinely repeated, distinct service can sometimes be split across lines with documentation.
  • 2Absolute, date-of-service edit. CMS treats exceeding it as clinically implausible. There is no appeal path.
  • 3Appealable, date-of-service edit. A real path exists with documentation the excess units reflect genuinely distinct, medically necessary services.

The two pairings this guide's research specifically targeted are the ESRD MCP codes (90951–90970) against the session-based dialysis codes (90935–90947), and E/M codes against same-day vascular access procedures (36901–36909). ⚠️ This build attempted to pull the current NCCI modifier indicator and MUE Adjudication Indicator values for these specific pairs directly from CMS's primary NCCI Procedure-to-Procedure Edits file and MUE tables; CMS.gov returned an access error to direct automated retrieval, and this session's web search budget was exhausted before a secondary confirmation could be completed. Do not treat any specific indicator or MAI value for these pairs as confirmed by this page — look the current values up directly in the CMS NCCI PTP Edits Lookup Tool and the current MUE table before building either into a scrubber rule. What can be stated with confidence, independent of the specific indicator value: the MCP-versus-session pairing reflects two different payment models for two different clinical populations, and CMS claims-processing guidance treats them as mutually exclusive for the same patient's related dialysis care in the same month regardless of which specific enforcement mechanism (a PTP edit, a claims-processing edit, or a coverage rule) applies in a given system update.

What the lookup actually looks like. Open the NCCI PTP Edits Lookup Tool, select the correct quarter's file, and enter the Column 1 code (the higher-valued or more comprehensive service) and the Column 2 code you're trying to bill alongside it. The tool returns the modifier indicator for that specific pair — 0, 1, or 9 — not a general statement about the code family. The same pair can carry different indicators in different quarters as CMS updates the edit file, which is exactly why "we checked this once" isn't a durable answer; a scrubber rule built off a stale quarter's indicator value can silently start blocking or allowing the wrong thing the moment CMS revises it. Build the lookup into a quarterly compliance task, not a one-time build step.

The E/M-versus-vascular-access-procedure pairing works differently from the MCP-versus-session pairing, because it's governed by global-period rules and modifier 25 documentation standards rather than a PTP edit in the traditional sense — there typically isn't a Column 1/Column 2 pair to look up for a standard office E/M against a same-day 0-day-global procedure the way there is for two procedure codes. Instead, the E/M is bundled into the procedure's global fee by default, and modifier 25 is what unbundles it, contingent entirely on the note independently supporting a significant, separately identifiable service. Treating this like an NCCI lookup question rather than a documentation question is a common category error; the "indicator" that actually governs this pairing is whether the chart supports the modifier, not a number in a CMS table.

Vascular access imaging (36901) billed in the same session as an intervention that already includes that access and imaging work (36902–36906) is a bundling pattern built directly into the code descriptors themselves, not just an external NCCI edit — billing 36901 alongside 36902 for the same circuit in the same session reports the base diagnostic work twice, since the escalated code already includes it. This is a code-selection error more than a modifier-override question in most cases.

The central-segment add-on codes (36907, 36908, 36909) carry their own MUE unit caps independent of the primary code they're reported with, and each is meant to reflect genuinely distinct work in the central dialysis segment during the same session — not a way to report routine central-segment imaging that's already part of the primary procedure's included work. Where a session legitimately involves both a central angioplasty (36907) and a central stent (36908), documentation has to show two distinct central-segment interventions, not one procedure described twice under two add-on codes.

For any pair not addressed above, the two-step lookup habit is what separates a defensible claim from a guess: pull the specific Column 1/Column 2 pair in the CMS NCCI PTP Edits Lookup Tool first, note the modifier indicator, and only then check the MUE table for the specific code's MAI if a unit cap is also in play. Skipping straight to appending 59 because "that's what usually works" is exactly the pattern that draws payer-side scrutiny of a practice's modifier-59 usage rate, independent of whether any single claim was ultimately correct.

Do
  • Look up the specific pair's modifier indicator before appending 59 or an X-modifier, every time, not just the first time.
  • Treat MCP-versus-session dialysis code conflicts as a code-family selection question, not a modifier-bypass question.
  • Check add-on code MUE caps (36907–36909) independently of the primary procedure's own cap.
  • Re-check indicator and MAI values on a quarterly cadence, since both update independently of any code-set change.
Don't
  • Don't assume an indicator of 1 (bypassable) means the modifier is automatically appropriate — the documentation still has to support genuine distinctness.
  • Don't bill 36901 alongside an escalated code (36902–36906) for the same circuit in the same session; the escalated code already includes the base diagnostic work.
  • Don't treat a scrubber rule built off last quarter's NCCI file as current without re-checking it.
  • Don't use a high 59-append rate as a normal pattern of doing business — it's a payer audit trigger independent of whether individual claims were correct.

Worked CO-197 scenario, true absence. Pro tip

Before appealing a CO-197, check whether an authorization actually exists in your system first. A large share of what looks like a true authorization gap in nephrology is actually an authorization that was obtained correctly but didn't match the claim on code, date span, or NDC — that's a same-day resubmission, not a multi-week appeal, and treating it as the wrong kind of denial wastes staff time chasing the slower process.

Do
  • Confirm the specific policy or edit driving a denial (LCD document ID, NCCI indicator, MAI) before deciding whether to appeal or correct.
  • Distinguish a true authorization gap from a system-matching mismatch before choosing an appeal path versus a same-day resubmission.
  • Attach the specific clinical documentation the cited policy actually asks for, not a general medical-necessity statement.
Don't
  • Don't appeal a denial without first confirming whether an appeal path even exists for that specific denial type.
  • Don't bill the patient for a CO-197 denial where authorization was genuinely absent — that's not a billable-to-patient scenario.
  • Don't resubmit a claim unchanged and expect a different outcome; fix the specific element the denial identifies first.

Appeal language for a CO-50, when the claim was correct. A generic "this service was medically necessary" letter rarely moves a nephrology denial. What works is naming the specific policy and mapping the chart directly to it: "Per [MAC name] LCD [document ID], '[LCD title],' hemodialysis frequency exceeding [X] sessions per month is covered when medical necessity is documented. The attached progress notes dated [dates] document interdialytic weight gain of [X] kg, potassium of [X] mEq/L, and clinical volume overload requiring the additional session performed on [date]. We request reconsideration of this denial based on the enclosed documentation, which meets the coverage criteria in the cited policy." That structure — name the policy, quote its threshold, map the chart's actual numbers to it — is the difference between an appeal a reviewer can approve on the first pass and one that goes back for more information.

Track these four denial types separately in your reporting rather than lumping them into a generic denial rate, because each one points to a different upstream fix: CO-50 points to an order-entry gap (the diagnosis wasn't checked against the LCD before the study was ordered), CO-197 points to an authorization-workflow gap, CO-151 points to a scheduling or documentation gap against a specific MAC threshold, and CO-97/CO-18 points to a code-family selection error. A dashboard that shows "denial rate: 12%" tells you nothing actionable; a dashboard that shows which of these four is driving the number tells you exactly which team and which workflow to fix.

Finding your MAC. CMS assigns Part A/Part B claims processing to one of a small number of MAC jurisdictions by state, and each jurisdiction maps to exactly one of the seven contractors above (Palmetto GBA, Novitas, CGS, WPS, Noridian, First Coast, or Wellpoint Federal) for a given practice location. If your billing team doesn't already have this documented per practice location, it's a one-time lookup against CMS's published MAC jurisdiction map, not something to infer from a competitor's blog post or a neighboring state's rule — two practices a few hundred miles apart can fall under entirely different contractors with entirely different LCD document IDs and effective dates.

Note for Noridian's jurisdiction specifically: the prior version of Noridian's Frequency of Hemodialysis LCD, L37504, was retired 10/16/2025, the same date the current L37502 became effective — if your records or scrubber rules still reference L37504, update them to L37502.

Vascular access non-invasive study coverage is addressed separately, and so far as this build's research found, only by WPS: LCD L35751, "Non-Invasive Peripheral Venous Vascular and Hemodialysis Access Studies" (effective 02/26/2026). Find your practice's MAC first, then pull that MAC's specific Frequency of Hemodialysis article — not a generic search result — before building a coverage or scheduling rule against it. ⚠️ This build confirmed the document IDs, contractor names, and effective dates above directly against the CMS Coverage Database; the full covered-diagnosis lists and specific frequency thresholds inside each article were not independently pulled full-text for this build, because the coverage connector used here does not retrieve full LCD article text (only NCD/NCA/CAL documents support full-text retrieval through this connector) — pull the specific article text yourself before finalizing a scheduling or scrubber rule against it.

ESA coverage. There is no current National Coverage Determination establishing national Medicare coverage specifically for Erythropoiesis Stimulating Agents in CKD anemia management. CMS opened a national coverage analysis on this question — CAG-00413N, "Erythropoiesis Stimulating Agents (ESAs) for Treatment of Anemia in Adults with CKD Including Patients on Dialysis and Patients not on Dialysis" — which we confirmed directly against the CMS Coverage Database carries a status of Closed without resulting in a new NCD, meaning ESA coverage in this population is governed by local coverage and payer policy rather than a single national determination. Don't cite "no NCD" as "not covered" in an appeal — it means coverage runs through your MAC's local policy and the payer's own medical policy, so pull those instead of looking for a national ESA coverage rule that doesn't exist. A "Closed" national coverage analysis status specifically means CMS reviewed the question and chose not to issue a new national determination, which is a different, more defensible posture for an appeal than "CMS has never looked at this" — the distinction is worth stating explicitly in an appeal letter rather than leaving the reviewer to assume the weaker version.

ESRD patients can enroll in Medicare Advantage. This is worth stating plainly because plenty of billing staff still operate on outdated assumptions: prior to 2021, ESRD patients were largely restricted to original Medicare or a narrow set of ESRD-specific Special Needs Plans. The 21st Century Cures Act opened general Medicare Advantage enrollment to ESRD beneficiaries starting January 1, 2021, and that population has grown steadily in MA plans since. The practical effect for billing: don't assume an ESRD patient's coverage is original Medicare by default, and don't assume the MAC-specific LCDs and NCDs referenced throughout this guide apply directly — an MA plan's own medical policy governs its enrollees, even though CMS requires MA plans to cover at least what original Medicare covers. Verify eligibility and plan type at every new patient intake and at each annual enrollment period, not just once at initial workup, and re-verify whenever a patient mentions switching plans during open enrollment rather than waiting for a claim to bounce back on the wrong payer.

Medicare Secondary Payer during the 30-month coordination period. This is a coordination-of-benefits trap specific to ESRD that catches billing teams used to Medicare being straightforwardly primary once a patient is enrolled. A patient with employer group health plan (GHP) coverage who becomes Medicare-eligible based on ESRD has a 30-month coordination period, starting the date they're first eligible to enroll (not the date they actually enroll), during which the GHP is the primary payer and Medicare is secondary — regardless of employer size and regardless of any policy language in the GHP stating otherwise. Medicare coverage itself generally starts the first day of the third month after regular dialysis begins (that three-month wait can be shortened or waived for self-dialysis training or a kidney transplant within the window), but starting Medicare coverage is not the same as Medicare becoming primary. Billing Medicare as primary during an active 30-month coordination period is a recurring, avoidable error that either delays payment while the claim gets redirected to the correct primary payer or creates a recoupment once the GHP relationship surfaces.

Water purification equipment. NCD 230.7, "Water Purification and Softening Systems Used in Conjunction with Home Dialysis" (confirmed directly against the CMS Coverage Database, effective 05/01/1989, still active) covers water purification systems for home dialysis under Medicare's Home Dialysis Supplies and Equipment benefit, but explicitly excludes water softening systems as not reasonable and necessary, with one narrow exception: a softener used specifically to pretreat water entering a reverse-osmosis unit, when the RO manufacturer's water-quality specification requires it and the patient's water is documented as not meeting that standard. Coding a standalone water softener as covered equipment without that specific pretreatment justification is a straightforward, avoidable NCD-based denial. The revision history on this NCD is itself instructive for how nephrology coverage policy tends to age: the underlying rule has stood since 1989 with only a categorization update since, which is a reminder that "old" isn't a reason to assume an NCD or LCD has been superseded — confirm retirement or replacement status directly rather than assuming a decades-old document number is stale.

Medicare Advantage and commercial overlay. Every Medicare-specific rule above — the MAC-specific Frequency of Hemodialysis LCD, the ESRD MCP structure itself, NCD 230.7 — describes original, fee-for-service Medicare. A Medicare Advantage plan can apply its own utilization management on top of (never below) traditional Medicare's coverage floor, and it's common for an MA plan to require prior authorization for services original Medicare wouldn't gate at all, or to apply its own frequency review process independent of the MAC's LCD entirely. Commercial payers go further still, frequently applying their own medical policy documents for dialysis frequency, vascular access intervention frequency, and ESA/iron administration that don't reference a Medicare LCD at all. Treat "Medicare covers this" as informative context for a non-Medicare claim, never as the governing rule — pull the specific payer's own medical policy before assuming Medicare coverage logic transfers.

Facility-level quality performance affects the whole reimbursement picture, separate from any single claim. The ESRD Quality Incentive Program (QIP) applies a payment reduction of up to 2% to a dialysis facility's total Medicare payments for a given payment year when the facility fails to submit quality data satisfactorily or fails to meet the applicable minimum Total Performance Score across CMS's measure set (adequacy of dialysis, vascular access type, infection rates, and patient experience among them). This is a facility-level adjustment, not a per-claim denial, so it doesn't show up in an individual claim's remittance advice the way a CO-code does — it shows up as every claim from that facility paying at a reduced rate for the entire payment year. A practice or facility watching only claim-level denial patterns can miss a QIP-driven revenue reduction entirely, because nothing about the individual remittance flags it as a QIP issue; it just looks like every claim from that facility pays slightly less than the published base rate would predict — which means QIP performance tracking belongs in the same revenue-cycle conversation as denial management, not filed separately under "quality" and reviewed on a different cadence by a different team.

ABNs. An Advance Beneficiary Notice belongs in the workflow wherever a service might not meet Medicare's medical necessity bar under the applicable LCD — a hemodialysis session that would push a patient's monthly frequency above what the MAC's Frequency of Hemodialysis LCD supports without additional documented medical necessity is the clearest recurring example in this specialty, along with a vascular access study ordered without a diagnosis on the covered list. Get the ABN signed and on file before the service, not after a CO-50 denial arrives; a signed ABN is what allows the patient to be billed if Medicare denies the claim, and without it, a denied service that should have carried an ABN generally can't be shifted to the patient at all.

Prior authorization in nephrology

ESAs and IV iron administered in-facility during dialysis are standard of care for dialysis-related anemia, but that doesn't mean they're authorization-free. Original Medicare generally doesn't require prior authorization for routine in-center administration, but a substantial share of Medicare Advantage plans and commercial payers do, layering their own utilization management on top of what's clinically routine.

The recurring failure mode in this specialty isn't a true coverage gap — it's an authorization that was obtained correctly on the front end but denies anyway because the payer's claims-adjudication system doesn't match it to the billed claim. The mismatch is usually one of three things: the billed HCPCS/J-code doesn't exactly match what was authorized, the date of service falls outside the authorized date range (common with recurring, ongoing ESA/iron protocols authorized for a fixed window that a dialysis schedule then runs past), or the National Drug Code on the claim doesn't match the NDC tied to the authorization when a formulary substitution happened at the pharmacy or infusion center level.

Distinguishing this from a true CO-197 (authorization genuinely absent) matters because the fix is completely different. A true absent-authorization denial means the patient can't be billed and the service wasn't properly authorized before it was delivered — a process failure that needs to be fixed upstream. A system-mismatch denial on a service that was actually authorized is a same-day resubmission with the authorization number, the correct code, date range, and NDC clearly matched to what was billed — not a multi-week medical necessity appeal, and treating it as one wastes the time a straightforward resubmission would have taken instead.

A concrete version of the mismatch: a payer authorizes darbepoetin alfa for a 90-day window tied to a specific J-code and a specific NDC from the manufacturer on file at authorization time. Mid-window, the infusion center's pharmacy switches to a different NDC for the same drug due to a supply substitution — same J-code, different NDC. The claim denies CO-197 because the system match is NDC-level, not J-code-level, even though the authorized drug, dose, and clinical scenario are unchanged. The fix is a same-day resubmission referencing the original authorization number with the updated NDC clearly documented, not a medical-necessity appeal arguing the drug was needed — that argument was never in dispute.

Build the workflow to catch this before it denies, not after: track each authorization's code, date range, and NDC in the same system used to schedule the administration, and flag any mismatch at the point of administration rather than at the point of claim submission. A denial caught before the claim goes out costs nothing; the same denial caught after submission costs a resubmission cycle and, if timely-filing limits are tight, occasionally the claim itself.

Pro tip

Set authorization expiration dates to trigger a renewal task at least two weeks before the window closes, not on the day it lapses. A dialysis-related ESA or iron protocol is recurring by nature — the same patient will need the same drug again next month — so a lapsed authorization on an ongoing protocol is entirely foreseeable and entirely preventable with a calendar-driven renewal workflow, unlike a true new-diagnosis authorization request that genuinely can't be anticipated in advance.

The bottom line for your nephrology practice

The four gaps this guide targets — unpublished NCCI/MUE indicator values for the highest-collision nephrology code pairs, MAC-specific LCD document IDs instead of a generic "check your LCD," full CKD staging specificity mapped to documentation triggers and risk-adjustment impact, and denial patterns tied to the specific claim scenario that causes them — all point to the same underlying problem: nephrology's revenue leaks in places that don't generate a denial at all. A visit count one short of the next MCP tier doesn't deny; it just pays less, silently, every month it happens. An N18.9 default instead of a supportable N18.6 doesn't deny either; it understates the case and the risk-adjusted revenue tied to it. A facility slipping below its QIP performance threshold doesn't generate a single flagged claim; every claim for the year just pays a little less than it should. Fixing that requires the specific values and specific documents this guide names, not a general awareness that bundling and coverage variation exist.

None of this is a one-time audit fix, either. LCD document IDs get superseded (Noridian's L37504 became L37502 in the middle of this build's own research window), NCCI edit files update quarterly, ICD-10-CM adds and revises codes every October 1, and CMS revises the ESRD PPS base rate and QIP measure set annually. A nephrology billing operation that treats this guide, or any guide, as a permanent reference rather than a starting point for a recurring verification habit will drift out of date within a year. Build the habit of checking the specific document, not just remembering the general rule, into the workflow itself — that's the difference between a practice that catches a rate change in the first denied claim and one that catches it six months and a few thousand claims later.

Frequently asked questions

Can we bill an E/M visit and the ESRD monthly capitation payment in the same month?

Not for the same dialysis-related care. Once a patient is established on maintenance dialysis, the monthly management of that dialysis is bundled into the age-banded MCP code (90951-90970) and isn't separately billable as a standard E/M, even if the visit happened in the office. A genuinely unrelated problem addressed at a separate encounter can support its own E/M with documentation showing it wasn't part of the dialysis management the MCP already covers, but that's the exception, not the routine pattern, and payers scrutinize it.

Why did our ESRD monthly capitation claim pay less than expected?

The most common cause is a visit count one short of what the chart actually supports. The MCP code family is banded by face-to-face visit count within the month (4-or-more, 2-to-3, 1, or 0), and each band pays differently. If the physician saw the patient four times but only three visits are documented with a dated, signed note, the claim has to be coded and paid at the lower band even though the care was fully delivered. Audit visit-count documentation before the claim goes out, not after it underpays.

What's the difference between N18.5 and N18.6 for a dialysis patient?

N18.5 is chronic kidney disease, stage 5 — severe kidney failure, typically eGFR under 15, without a chart-documented finding of dialysis dependence. N18.6, end-stage renal disease, requires that the patient is actually established on dialysis or has ESRD medically documented as such; it isn't just "a sicker version" of stage 5. Coding N18.6 without dialysis-status documentation in the chart is a common audit finding, and coding N18.5 for a patient who has clearly started maintenance dialysis understates the case and can affect risk-adjusted payment.

Is the Frequency of Hemodialysis LCD the same everywhere?

No — it's one of the clearest examples of Medicare coverage being MAC-specific rather than national. At least seven Medicare Administrative Contractors each publish and maintain their own Frequency of Hemodialysis LCD, with different document IDs and different effective dates: Palmetto GBA (L34575), Novitas Solutions (L35014), CGS Administrators (L37575), WPS Insurance Corporation (L37537), Noridian Healthcare Solutions (L37502), First Coast Service Options (L37564), and Wellpoint Federal (L37475). Find your specific MAC's article before you build a frequency rule into your scheduling or billing workflow — a rule copied from another region's LCD may not match yours.

Can we bill modifier 25 on an E/M the same day as a vascular access procedure?

Usually yes, because most vascular access procedures in the 36901-36909 family carry a short (typically 0-day) global period rather than the 90-day global of a major surgery, so a significant, separately identifiable E/M performed the same day can support modifier 25 — but the note has to independently justify the E/M beyond the standard pre-procedure assessment that's already bundled into the procedure. Confirm the specific code's global period in the CMS Physician Fee Schedule Look-Up Tool before relying on this as a blanket rule, since it varies by code.

Do ESAs and IV iron always require prior authorization?

Not under original Medicare for most in-center administration, but a large share of Medicare Advantage and commercial payers require it even though ESAs and IV iron are standard of care for dialysis-related anemia. The recurring failure mode isn't a true coverage gap — it's an authorization that was obtained correctly but denies anyway because the payer's claims system doesn't match the authorization to the billed code, date range, or National Drug Code. That's a resubmission-with-documentation problem, not a medical necessity appeal, and treating it as the wrong kind of denial wastes staff time.

Why does our claim deny with CO-97 when we billed a session-based dialysis code and the ESRD MCP in the same month?

Because they're not meant to coexist for the same established ESRD patient in the same calendar month. The MCP codes (90951-90970) are a bundled monthly payment for outpatient maintenance dialysis management; the session-based codes (90935-90947) are built for acute, non-ESRD, or inpatient dialysis scenarios. Billing both for the same patient in the same month for related dialysis care reads as double billing for the same service period, and it's a hard bundling conflict rather than something a modifier is likely to clear — confirm the code family fits the clinical scenario before the claim goes out.

Is telehealth billable for home hemodialysis monthly management (90966)?

It depends on the MAC and the payer, and this is genuinely unsettled rather than a case where one guide's answer applies everywhere. Whether a 95 or GT telehealth modifier is required, permitted, or inapplicable on 90966 varies by contractor and by payer telehealth policy, and it changes as telehealth rules get updated. Don't default to one modifier from a national blog post — call your specific MAC's provider contact center or check that MAC's telehealth billing guidance before finalizing the claim.
