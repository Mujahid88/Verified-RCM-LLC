Our complete podiatry guide

Podiatry claim denials and appeals: CO-50, routine foot care, and more.

Podiatry denies more than most specialties for one structural reason: the same nail-care CPT code is a covered, medically necessary service for one patient and a statutorily excluded routine service for the next, and the only thing separating them is documentation the payer can see on the claim. This guide walks through the five denial patterns that account for most of podiatry's lost revenue — CO-50, CO-96, CO-97, CO-4, and frequency caps — with the real appeal argument for each, a worked reconsideration letter for the single most common wrongly-denied scenario, and where an ABN changes the entire calculus.

Reviewed by Phares A., MD · Physiotherapist, CPC (AAPC) · Part of our podiatry billing and coding guide

Key takeaways

  • CO-50 and CO-96 look similar but call for opposite responses. CO-96 on genuinely routine foot care is a closed write-off. CO-50 means the payer checked for medical necessity and didn't find it on the claim — worth a chart check before treating it the same way.
  • The Q7/Q8/Q9 class-finding modifier is what converts a routine service into a covered one — not the diagnosis code alone. A qualifying systemic condition documented in the chart but missing the matching modifier on the claim denies exactly like unqualified routine care.
  • An ABN on file changes the appeal question entirely. Modifier GA (ABN signed, denial expected) shifts liability to the patient with no appeal needed. Modifier GZ (no ABN, denial expected) leaves the practice unable to bill the patient and usually without a winning appeal either.
  • Most Medicare Administrative Contractors cap covered nail debridement at once every 60 days. Billing before that interval elapses denies as a frequency edit regardless of how well-documented the qualifying condition is.

Why podiatry denies differently from other specialties

Most specialties fight medical-necessity denials over whether a service was appropriate for the diagnosis. Podiatry fights a different battle: whether the same CPT code is covered at all, for this patient, on this date, given the systemic condition on file. Section 1862(a)(13) of the Social Security Act statutorily excludes routine foot care — cutting or removing corns and calluses, trimming nondystrophic nails, and other hygienic foot maintenance — from Medicare coverage. That exclusion has a documented exception for patients whose systemic condition makes professional foot care medically necessary rather than cosmetic, and the entire denial-and-appeal picture in this specialty runs on whether that exception is provable on the claim, not just true in the chart.

That's the lens for every denial below: is this a genuinely excluded service with no exception (a write-off), or a covered service that denied because the proof of the exception didn't make it onto the claim (a winnable correction or appeal)?

The DME side of the frequency table works differently from the debridement side, and it's worth spelling out because the two get conflated. Medicare's therapeutic shoe benefit for persons with diabetes is a statutory once-per-calendar-year allotment, not a rolling 12-month window: one pair of custom-molded shoes plus two additional pairs of inserts, or one pair of extra-depth shoes plus three additional pairs of inserts, per calendar year. A claim for a second pair of shoes in the same calendar year denies on frequency regardless of how well the qualifying diabetic foot condition is documented, because the benefit itself is exhausted, not the medical necessity. The fix when that happens genuinely by accident — a patient who lost or damaged a covered pair mid-year — isn't an appeal either; it's confirming with the DME MAC whether a replacement is separately payable under its own policy, which is a different question from the annual allotment.

Commercial payers don't always mirror Medicare's exclusion

It's a mistake worth flagging explicitly because it costs practices real money in both directions: Section 1862(a)(13)'s routine foot care exclusion is a Medicare statute, and while most commercial payers and Medicare Advantage plans model their own routine-care exclusions on it, the class-finding framework and the exact list of qualifying systemic conditions are not automatically identical across every commercial plan. Some commercial payers cover routine foot care more broadly for diabetic patients without requiring the same Q7/Q8/Q9 modifier structure; others apply a stricter or differently worded exclusion than Medicare's own. ⚠️ Because commercial coverage policy is plan- and contract-specific rather than governed by a single national rule, this build did not attempt to verify a universal commercial-payer position and none should be assumed — pull the specific payer's medical policy for routine foot care and diabetic foot care before assuming a Medicare-style CO-96 or CO-50 pattern applies on a non-Medicare claim. Treating every payer's routine-foot-care denial as if it followed Medicare's exact rule is a common source of appeals filed on the wrong theory.

Pro tip

Before writing any podiatry appeal, ask one question first: does the chart actually document a qualifying condition, or is it genuinely routine care that denied correctly? That single check sorts almost every denial into "correction" (the condition exists, a modifier or code was missing), "appeal" (the condition exists, the payer's system missed it), or "closed" (no qualifying condition, ABN status decides who eats the cost). Skipping straight to writing an appeal letter without that check is the most common wasted-effort pattern in podiatry denial management.

GA and GZ: how an ABN changes the entire denial-and-appeal calculus

A genuinely non-covered service isn't automatically a lost claim for the practice — it's a question of who was told in advance. Medicare's two ABN-related modifiers point to opposite outcomes for the exact same denied claim.

Modifier GA — ABN signed, denial expected
  • The patient signed a valid Advance Beneficiary Notice before the service, acknowledging Medicare is likely to deny it as not reasonable and necessary. Billing with modifier GA tells the payer that notice is on file. When the expected denial comes back, the patient — not the payer, and not a provider write-off — is financially responsible. There's no appeal to write, because the outcome was disclosed and accepted in advance.
Modifier GZ — no ABN, denial expected
  • The service was billed anyway, without an ABN, when the practice had reason to expect Medicare would deny it as not reasonable and necessary. The claim denies, the practice cannot bill the patient for it, and there typically isn't a winning appeal on the record as submitted — the absence of the ABN is itself the problem, not something a stronger clinical argument fixes after the fact.

The practical implication: for any visit where staff genuinely aren't sure a qualifying systemic condition will hold up — a borderline class finding, a patient whose neuropathy documentation is thin — getting a signed ABN before the visit is cheaper than gambling on a CO-50 or CO-96 denial with no ABN behind it. It converts an unrecoverable write-off into a properly disclosed patient-responsibility charge.

Worked reconsideration: CO-50 denied, but the qualifying condition was documented

The single most common wrongly-denied CO-50 pattern in podiatry isn't a genuine coverage dispute — it's a claim where the physician documented a real Class B or Class C finding at the visit, but the Q8 or Q9 modifier never made it onto the claim that went out. That's a correction request, not a formal appeal, and it moves faster when it's framed that way. A skeleton for that letter:

Frequently asked questions

Does Medicare ever pay for routine foot care, or is it always denied?

Routine foot care is statutorily excluded under Section 1862(a)(13) of the Social Security Act, but Medicare carves out an exception when a documented systemic condition — such as diabetes with peripheral neuropathy or peripheral vascular disease — makes professional care medically necessary rather than cosmetic. That exception only pays when the chart supports a qualifying class finding and the claim carries the matching Q7, Q8, or Q9 modifier. Without both, the same CPT code that would be covered for one patient is routine, excluded care for another.

What's the real difference between a CO-50 and a CO-96 denial on a foot care claim?

CO-96 means the payer's system flagged the service as categorically non-covered — no exception exists on the claim as submitted, full stop. CO-50 means the payer looked for medical necessity and didn't find it on this specific claim, which usually means the qualifying condition, the class finding, or the Q-modifier didn't make it onto the claim even though the chart supports it. That distinction decides the entire response: a CO-96 on genuinely routine care is a write-off, while a CO-50 is worth checking the chart before assuming the same thing.

Do we need an ABN for every foot care service that might not be covered?

Only for services the practice has reason to believe Medicare won't pay, and it changes what happens next. An Advance Beneficiary Notice signed before the visit, billed with modifier GA, shifts financial responsibility to the patient once the expected denial comes back — there's no appeal to write because the outcome was anticipated and disclosed. Billing genuinely routine foot care with modifier GZ, meaning no ABN was obtained, means the practice cannot bill the patient either; the contractual write-off is likely to stand and there typically isn't a winning appeal on the record as submitted.

How often can we bill nail debridement without triggering a frequency denial?

Most Medicare Administrative Contractors pay covered nail debridement (CPT 11720/11721) no more often than once every 60 days per the applicable LCD for debridement of mycotic nails, and some cap it at six sessions per beneficiary per 12 months. Billing before that interval has elapsed, even with a valid qualifying condition and modifier on file, typically denies as a frequency edit rather than a medical-necessity denial — confirm the date of the last paid debridement before scheduling the next one, not after the claim comes back.
