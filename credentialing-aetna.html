Aetna credentialing: CAQH-driven, Availity-managed, network-gated.

Aetna runs a relatively standardised national credentialing process built on CAQH data and administered largely through Availity. The friction is rarely the paperwork; it is network availability, which is a commercial decision Aetna makes independently of whether your credentials are impeccable.

Key takeaways

  • CAQH is the credentialing source. Aetna pulls from ProView, so profile completeness determines application progress.
  • Network status is a business decision. A closed panel can decline a fully qualified provider, and that is not appealable on credentials.
  • Commercial and Medicare Advantage are separate. Being in-network on one Aetna product line doesn't guarantee the other.
  • Recredentialing runs on a cycle. Missing it can suspend participation, and reinstatement is slower than renewal.
  • Contracting is separate from credentialing. Both must complete before a provider is genuinely participating.

How Aetna credentialing works

Aetna credentialing follows the pattern common to large national commercial payers: the practitioner maintains a CAQH ProView profile, authorises Aetna to access it, and submits a participation request through Availity or Aetna's provider channels. Aetna performs primary source verification — confirming licensure directly with the state board, education and training directly with the institution, board certification directly with the certifying board, and checking the practitioner against the OIG exclusion list and the National Practitioner Data Bank rather than taking any of it on the applicant's word — and takes the file through a credentialing committee that meets on a recurring schedule rather than reviewing files individually as they arrive. Because the data comes from CAQH, the quality of that profile largely determines how smoothly the process runs; a verifier who hits a gap in the profile has to stop and request it rather than proceeding, which is what turns a routine file into a stalled one.

The decisive variable is usually network status. Aetna maintains panels by specialty and geography, and where a panel is closed it can decline to contract regardless of qualifications. That is a commercial decision rather than a credentialing outcome, and it is addressed with an access argument rather than by resubmitting documents. Practices that understand this early spend their effort on the right thing: demonstrating a service gap, unusual hours, language capability or an underserved location, rather than repeatedly polishing a CAQH profile that was never the actual obstacle.

Whether the applicant is a solo practitioner or a physician joining an existing group also changes what gets submitted. A solo practitioner applies under their own tax ID and practice location, and the credentialing file and the contract both attach to that individual. A physician joining a practice that already holds an Aetna contract is typically added under the group's existing agreement rather than negotiating a fresh one, but the individual still has to clear credentialing on their own record before Aetna will add them to the group's roster and load them into the directory at that location — credentialing is always personal to the practitioner, even when the contract itself is not. A brand-new group with no prior Aetna relationship has to establish both the group contract and the individual credentialing files essentially at the same time, which is where timelines tend to run longest because neither piece can fully finish without the other.

CAQH ProView is not just a data source Aetna references occasionally — it functions as the application itself. Aetna's credentialing team pulls directly from the attested profile, so an incomplete section, an unexplained work-history gap, or a lapsed attestation stalls the file before anyone at Aetna reviews the clinical content underneath it. The profile has to carry a complete, chronological work history with gaps of any real length explained in writing, current malpractice history including any settled or pending claims, and current copies of licensure, DEA registration and board certification — and because CAQH attestation itself expires periodically, a profile that was complete at initial submission can go stale mid-review if the practice isn't tracking the re-attestation date independently of the Aetna file. For select states, Aetna also publishes its own state-specific credentialing-request form as an additional or alternative intake path alongside the CAQH-based route, which is worth checking for rather than assuming the profile is the whole story. Full walkthrough of how the CAQH-based intake actually works, what the application-management team handles versus general Provider Services, and the state-form pattern, is in our CAQH and Aetna's provider credentialing process guide.

Commercial versus Medicare Advantage participation

Aetna runs Provider Services as two functionally separate lines depending on product: 1-888-632-3862 for commercial and most non-Medicare business, 1-800-624-0756 for HMO and Medicare Advantage plans. That split exists because Aetna's Medicare Advantage network operates under its own CMS network-adequacy requirements and, in many markets, a materially different panel than the commercial network — so being credentialed and contracted for Aetna's commercial products does not automatically mean a practice is participating in Aetna Medicare Advantage, or vice versa.

Practices that assume one credentialing file covers everything sometimes only discover the gap when an Aetna Medicare Advantage claim pays out of network, months after the practice believed it was fully in-network with Aetna. This is worse than a denial, because nothing about the initial commercial approval flags the omission — the practice keeps operating on the wrong assumption until a specific claim surfaces it, usually after several visits have already been scheduled under the wrong assumption. The same caution applies to Aetna's dual-eligible special needs plans and any other Medicare Advantage product variant Aetna underwrites regionally: a directory listing under commercial Aetna is not evidence of participation in a specific Medicare Advantage plan, and the only reliable confirmation is a direct check against that plan's own network file or a call to the Medicare Advantage line. Full detail on what's shared between the two product lines, what has to be confirmed separately, and how to verify Medicare Advantage participation before it becomes a claims problem, is in our Aetna Medicare Advantage versus commercial credentialing guide.

Timeline expectations and effective dates

There is no single published number for how long an Aetna application takes, and practices that plan around an assumed date rather than a confirmed one tend to get burned by it. What is predictable is the sequence: CAQH attestation has to be current before Aetna will meaningfully progress the file, the network application (and any state-specific credentialing form) layers on top of that, and the effective date is only set once verification and contracting have both completed — credentialing approval alone does not make a provider billable as in-network.

Practices that let a new hire start seeing Aetna patients on the assumption paperwork will simply catch up routinely find those encounters processing out of network or getting written off entirely. The credentialing committee cadence adds its own lag on top of verification time: even a file with zero outstanding items has to wait for the next scheduled committee date rather than being approved the moment the last document clears, which is why two files completed a week apart can still reach committee at the same time, or a file finished a day after a committee cutoff can lose weeks waiting for the next one. The realistic sequencing — what to submit first, what can run in parallel, and where applications commonly stall waiting on nothing more than a phone call to confirm status — is in our Aetna enrollment timeline and effective dates guide.

Step by step: how to do it

  1. Complete and attest CAQH ProViewPopulate every section, explain work history gaps, upload current documents and authorise Aetna specifically.
  2. Confirm network availabilityEstablish whether the panel is open for your specialty and location before investing in a full application.
  3. Submit the participation requestApply through Availity or Aetna's provider onboarding channel with practice details, locations and the products sought. Check whether your state has its own credentialing-request form as an additional intake path.
  4. Complete credentialing verificationAetna verifies licensure, education, board certification, malpractice history and sanctions, then takes the file to committee.
  5. Review and execute the contractConfirm the fee schedule, the products and networks included — commercial, HMO, Medicare Advantage — and the effective date before signing.
  6. Load identifiers and confirm the directoryRecord Aetna-specific provider identifiers, verify directory accuracy and release held claims from the effective date.
  7. Diarise recredentialingRecredentialing typically runs on a multi-year cycle tied to CAQH re-attestation; missing it can suspend participation without warning.

Availity's role doesn't end once the contract is executed. Once a provider is loaded, ongoing provider data maintenance — a new practice location, a name change, adding a specialty, updating a group's roster when a physician leaves — is generally handled through Availity rather than by reopening the credentialing file each time, though a genuinely new practice location for an existing provider can still trigger its own credentialing check depending on distance and specialty. Practices that only ever log into Availity for eligibility checks and claim status are often unaware the same portal is where provider maintenance requests belong, and end up calling Provider Services for changes that would move faster submitted directly through the portal.

Delegated credentialing for larger groups

Groups large enough to run their own NCQA-compliant credentialing program can apply for delegated credentialing, under which Aetna formally delegates initial verification and recredentialing decisions to the group's own credentialing committee rather than running each practitioner through Aetna's process individually. It is not a shortcut available to any practice that simply wants to move faster — Aetna requires a pre-delegation audit of the group's credentialing policies, primary source verification procedures and a sample of existing credentialing files before granting delegation, and it runs periodic file audits afterward, plus ongoing roster reporting as practitioners join and leave the group, to confirm the group is still meeting NCQA standards on an ongoing basis rather than just at the point delegation was granted.

For anything short of a large multi-specialty group or health system with dedicated credentialing staff, standard individual credentialing through CAQH remains the right path; delegation adds an audit and ongoing compliance burden — policy documentation, file sampling, roster accuracy — that isn't worth carrying for a handful of providers, and a failed pre-delegation audit costs more time than simply running individual applications would have. What the pre-delegation audit actually checks, how the periodic file audit works, and roughly the volume where delegation starts to make sense, is in our delegated credentialing with Aetna guide.

Where the denial is closer to a genuine access or committee decision, or a stalled file needs escalation past general Provider Services and toward the credentialing team directly, there is a structured path for that too, and knowing which of those two categories a given denial falls into is most of the work in resolving it efficiently. Pro tip

Ask about panel status before you start the application, not after months of silence. A closed panel is a commercial decision that no amount of documentation will change, and knowing it upfront lets you redirect effort into an access argument or a single-case agreement rather than waiting on a process that was never going to approve. When you do submit a reconsideration, put the access argument in writing with specifics — wait times at existing in-network providers, a named subspecialty gap, hours the panel doesn't currently cover — rather than a general request to reconsider, since a vague request is easy for a reviewer to set aside.

Recredentialing: staying in network

Aetna recredentials participating providers on a cycle, commonly around every three years, consistent with NCQA accreditation standards rather than an Aetna-specific schedule. The trigger on your end is CAQH: recredentialing runs off the same attested profile as initial credentialing, so a lapsed attestation doesn't sit quietly — it can stall the recredentialing file the same way it would stall a new application, only this time the provider is already generating claims that depend on staying in network.

Recredentialing isn't purely a paperwork refresh, either. Along with re-verifying licensure, certification and insurance, Aetna's review at this stage also looks back at anything accumulated since initial credentialing — new malpractice activity, sanctions, or member complaints — so a clean initial file doesn't guarantee a clean recredentialing cycle if something material happened in the interim and wasn't proactively disclosed. Practices sometimes treat recredentialing as a formality precisely because the initial credentialing was uneventful, and that assumption is exactly what causes a preventable notice to sit unopened until it becomes a deadline problem instead of a routine renewal.

Miss the cycle deadline entirely and participation can be suspended, at which point claims process out of network until it's resolved, and reinstatement is routinely slower than the recredentialing itself would have been, in part because a suspended file sometimes has to be treated closer to a new application than a renewal. The full cycle, what triggers the review, and what actually happens to claims and network status if it lapses, is in our Aetna recredentialing cycle guide.

Multi-location practices and directory accuracy

A practice with more than one office has to treat each location as its own data point in Aetna's system, not an afterthought to the original application. Every location where a credentialed provider will actually see Aetna patients needs to be reflected in the file, and a location added after the fact — a satellite office opened a year into the contract, a provider who starts splitting time between two sites — needs its own update rather than an assumption that the original approval automatically covers it. This matters beyond internal recordkeeping because it drives the public provider directory, and an inaccurate directory listing (wrong address, a provider shown at a location they no longer see patients at, a closed panel still shown as accepting new patients) creates real patient-facing problems and, increasingly, payer compliance exposure of its own given regulatory attention on directory accuracy.

The practical habit worth building is a standing quarterly check of your own directory listing against reality, rather than waiting for a patient complaint or a compliance audit to surface a stale entry. It's a small task compared to the credentialing process itself, but it's one of the only steps in this whole workflow that stays entirely within the practice's control rather than depending on Aetna's timeline.

Do and don't

Do
  • Confirm panel availability before investing in a full application.
  • Keep CAQH complete, current and authorised to Aetna.
  • Frame reconsideration requests around patient access, not credentials.
  • Confirm Medicare Advantage participation separately if your patients include Aetna MA plans.
  • Update every practice location in the file as the group grows, not just the original one.
  • Diarise recredentialing well ahead of the cycle date.
  • Verify the directory listing on a standing schedule, not only right after approval.
Don't
  • Don't assume strong credentials override a closed panel.
  • Don't treat credentialing approval as participation.
  • Don't assume commercial participation includes Aetna Medicare Advantage networks.
  • Don't let CAQH attestation lapse mid-application or between recredentialing cycles.
  • Don't submit a W-9 that differs from the billing entity.
  • Don't leave a new practice location off the file and assume the original approval covers it.
  • Don't miss recredentialing; suspension is harder to undo than renewal.

Frequently asked questions

What do we do when Aetna says the panel is closed?

Treat it as a commercial conversation. Submit a reconsideration built on access rather than qualifications: an underserved location, extended or weekend hours, a subspecialty the network lacks, language capability, or a referral relationship that currently sends patients out of network. Where patients need care in the meantime, single-case agreements can cover individual episodes, though those are negotiated per patient and per episode rather than a standing substitute for network participation. Panels reopen periodically as Aetna's network needs shift, so keep the request live rather than abandoning it after one rejection.

How long does Aetna credentialing take?

For a clean application with a complete CAQH profile and an open panel, plan on a few months from submission to executed contract, with committee scheduling being a common source of variability. Incomplete CAQH data, unexplained history gaps or expired documents extend it considerably. Where the panel is closed, the timeline is indefinite because the constraint is not the process.

Does credentialing put us in network automatically?

No. Credentialing verifies the practitioner; the contract establishes network participation, products and rates. Both must complete before you can bill as participating. Practices sometimes begin seeing Aetna patients on credentialing approval and find claims paying at out-of-network rates, which creates both a revenue shortfall and a patient balance problem that is difficult to resolve after the fact.

What happens if we miss recredentialing?

Participation can be suspended, after which claims process out of network until the matter is resolved, and reinstatement generally takes longer than the recredentialing would have. Aetna issues notice in advance, but those notices are easy to lose in a busy practice. Track the cycle date centrally with a named owner and a reminder well ahead of the deadline.

Does credentialing with Aetna's commercial plans also cover Medicare Advantage?

Not automatically. Aetna operates commercial and Medicare Advantage as functionally separate product lines with separate Provider Services numbers, and Medicare Advantage network adequacy and panel decisions run independently of the commercial network. A practice credentialed and contracted for Aetna commercial products should confirm Medicare Advantage participation separately rather than assume it, especially before scheduling MA patients — a directory listing under one product line is not proof of participation in the other, and the surest confirmation is a direct call to the relevant Provider Services line or a written check with the credentialing team.

Is delegated credentialing something our practice should pursue?

Only if the group is large enough to run its own NCQA-compliant credentialing program with dedicated staff. Delegation shifts initial verification and recredentialing decisions to the group's own committee, but Aetna requires a pre-delegation audit of policies, procedures and a sample of existing files before granting delegation, and it runs periodic file audits and ongoing roster reporting afterward, which is real ongoing compliance overhead rather than a one-time approval. For a handful of providers, standard individual credentialing through CAQH is faster to set up, cheaper to maintain, and doesn't carry the risk of failing a pre-delegation audit after investing in preparing for one.

Do you manage recredentialing as well as initial applications?

Yes. Recredentialing lapses are among the most costly and most preventable credentialing failures, because the provider is already generating revenue when participation is suspended. We track cycle dates across all payers, keep CAQH attested continuously, and complete recredentialing ahead of deadlines rather than in response to a suspension notice.

Want Aetna credentialing handled end to end?

We prepare, submit and chase applications on a schedule, keep CAQH attested, and tell you the week each provider becomes billable.
