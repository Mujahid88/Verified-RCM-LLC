Our complete practice licensing guide

Forming the practice entity: LLC, PLLC, PC and the EIN that everything else depends on.

A medical practice can't apply for a state license "as" a business, open a bank account under a business name, or show up on a payer's credentialing application until a legal entity exists on paper. That entity, and the EIN attached to it, is the foundation every other piece of licensing and enrollment references. Get the entity type wrong for your state's ownership rules and you can end up refiling months into the process — after the state license application, the NPI, and the bank account already reference the entity that now has to change.

Reviewed by Dana Whitfield, JD · Healthcare Regulatory Counsel · Part of our practice licensing guide

Key takeaways

  • A medical practice usually can't use a plain LLC. Most states require a professional entity — a PLLC, PC, or professional corporation — built specifically for licensed practitioners.
  • Corporate practice of medicine rules restrict who can own the entity in many states, and the restriction can be strict enough to block a non-physician co-founder or investor entirely.
  • The EIN is free and fast through the IRS, but it can't be applied for until the entity itself is registered with the state.
  • Everything downstream references the entity and EIN — the bank account, the organizational NPI, payer credentialing, and often the state licensing paperwork itself.

Why a medical practice can't just form an LLC

A standard LLC is built for a general small business, and most states won't let a medical practice use one. Instead, states that license professionals typically require a professional entity — commonly a PLLC (professional limited liability company), a PC (professional corporation), or in some states a professional association — formed under a specific statute that applies to licensed occupations rather than businesses generally. The mechanics of liability protection and pass-through taxation are broadly similar to a standard LLC, but a professional entity usually has to demonstrate that its owners hold the relevant professional license before the state will register it, and it stays subject to that state's professional-conduct and ownership rules for as long as it operates.

Which label applies — PLLC, PC, professional association, or something else — is entirely a state-by-state question, and so is which professional entity type a given state requires for a medical practice specifically versus other licensed professions. This is not a step to guess at from a template used in a different state; confirm the correct entity type with the state's business filing agency or a healthcare attorney licensed there before filing.

Corporate practice of medicine: who's allowed to own the entity

Under what's broadly called the corporate practice of medicine doctrine, many states restrict ownership of a medical practice to licensed physicians, or in some cases to other specific categories of licensed practitioners. The underlying concern is that a non-licensed owner shouldn't be in a position to direct clinical judgment, so states that enforce this doctrine limit who can hold an equity stake in the entity that employs the physicians and bills for their care.

How strict this gets varies significantly by state. Some states enforce it tightly, with narrow exceptions. Others allow more flexible structures — management services organizations, friendly-PC arrangements, or other layered structures that separate clinical ownership from business operations — but those structures carry their own compliance requirements and are not something to set up without counsel familiar with that state's specific case law and regulatory guidance. A structure that's routine in one state can be a real problem in another, and this is one of the few areas of practice licensing where a generic answer is actively risky to rely on.

Do
  • Confirm the required entity type and ownership rules with the state's filing agency or a healthcare attorney before drafting anything.
  • Get every owner's license status confirmed and documented before filing formation paperwork.
  • Ask specifically about non-physician ownership if any investor or co-founder isn't a licensed physician.
Don't
  • Don't reuse a generic LLC template built for a non-licensed business.
  • Don't assume a structure that worked in one state is compliant in another.
  • Don't bring on a non-physician owner before confirming that state's corporate practice of medicine position.

The EIN: free, fast, and gated on the entity existing first

An Employer Identification Number is a free federal tax ID issued by the IRS, and the online application is typically one of the fastest steps in the entire licensing sequence once it's actually eligible to be filed. The catch is sequencing: the EIN application asks for the legal entity's name and formation details, so it can't be filed correctly until the state has registered the entity. Practices that try to get ahead of the process by applying for an EIN before the entity is formed either get rejected or end up with an EIN tied to the wrong legal name, which then has to be corrected with the IRS later — an avoidable delay stacked on top of every other one.

Once the entity is registered and the EIN is issued, both become reference points for nearly everything that follows: the business bank account, the organizational (Type 2) NPI application, payer credentialing and enrollment paperwork, and in many states, the practice-level registration that state medical boards require alongside the individual provider's license.

What the entity and EIN unlock downstream

Steps that depend on the entity and EIN already existing.
Downstream stepWhy it needs the entity/EIN first

None of these steps are technically difficult on their own. What makes entity formation the true first step in the sequence is that skipping ahead on any of them — opening a bank account under a placeholder name, applying for an NPI before the entity is finalized — tends to create a mismatch that has to be corrected later, usually right when the practice is trying to move fast on something else.

Step by step: forming the entity correctly

  1. Confirm the required entity type for the state and specialtyPLLC, PC, or another professional-entity label — confirm with the state's business filing agency or counsel, not a generic template.
  2. Confirm ownership eligibility under the state's corporate practice of medicine rulesEvery owner's license status should be settled before the formation documents are drafted.
  3. File the formation documents with the stateArticles of organization or incorporation, filed with the secretary of state or equivalent agency.
  4. Apply for the EIN once the entity is registeredFree through the IRS; usually fast, but only once the entity's legal name and formation details are final.
  5. Open the business bank accountRequires the formation documents and EIN; this is also usually the point where payer and vendor contracts start referencing the entity.
  6. Move into parallel licensing stepsState medical board licensure for each provider, NPI registration, and any practice-level state registration can now reference a finalized entity and EIN.
Pro tip

Don't apply for the EIN, open the bank account, or file the organizational NPI application under a placeholder or "doing business as" name while the formal entity paperwork is still pending. Every mismatch created early gets corrected the hard way later, usually while something more time-sensitive is also waiting on it.

Do and don't

Do
  • Confirm the correct professional entity type with the state before filing.
  • Settle ownership eligibility under corporate practice of medicine rules first.
  • Apply for the EIN only after the entity is registered and its legal name is final.
  • Use the same legal entity name consistently across every downstream application.
Don't
  • Don't default to a standard LLC without confirming the state requires a professional entity instead.
  • Don't bring on a non-physician owner without confirming the state's ownership rules first.
  • Don't apply for the EIN or NPI under a name that isn't yet the entity's final legal name.

Frequently asked questions

Can anyone own a medical practice?

No. Most states restrict ownership of a medical practice to licensed physicians (or, depending on the state, other licensed practitioners) under what's called the corporate practice of medicine doctrine. How strict that restriction is varies significantly by state — some allow limited non-physician ownership under specific structures, others are very restrictive. This is exactly the kind of question to confirm with a healthcare attorney licensed in the state where the practice will operate, before choosing an entity structure or bringing on a non-physician investor.

Do we need the EIN before we can apply for a state medical license?

The individual provider's state medical license application generally runs on the person, not the entity, so it can often move in parallel with entity formation. But almost everything else downstream — opening a business bank account, applying for the organizational NPI, enrolling with payers, and in many states registering the practice itself — expects the entity and its EIN to already exist. Forming the entity and getting the EIN early keeps it from becoming the bottleneck later.

Is an LLC the same as a PLLC?

No. A PLLC (professional limited liability company) is a variant that most states require for licensed professionals, including physicians, instead of a standard LLC. The practical mechanics — liability protection, pass-through taxation — are similar, but a PLLC typically requires proof that its owners hold the relevant professional license, and it's subject to the state's corporate practice of medicine rules in a way a generic LLC formed for a non-licensed business isn't. Whether your state uses PLLC, PC, or another professional-entity label specifically is a state-by-state question.

Confirm before you rely on this. Licensing fees, compact membership, training requirements and application processes change, and change by state. The process information on this page reflects standard industry practice as of August 2026 and is provided for general education — verify current requirements directly with the relevant state board, the DEA, or CMS before relying on it.
