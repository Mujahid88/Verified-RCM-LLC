Our complete sleep medicine guide

Sleep medicine claim denials and appeals: wrong code set, CPAP compliance.

Sleep medicine denials cluster around a small set of repeat offenders: a home sleep test billed with the wrong code set for the payer, a titration study that never proves the patient still needs the device, a split night billed as two full studies instead of one, and a CPAP rental line missing the modifier that certifies compliance. Almost none of these need a true medical-necessity appeal — they need the claim corrected against the rule that actually governs it. This guide covers the six denials that account for most sleep lab and DME write-offs, the appeal or correction argument for each, and a worked resubmission letter for the most common one: the CPT-versus-G-code mismatch on a home sleep apnea test.

Reviewed by Phares A., MD · Physiotherapist, CPC (AAPC) · Part of our sleep medicine billing and coding guide

Key takeaways

  • Medicare doesn't accept the CPT codes for a home sleep apnea test at all. DME MACs require the HCPCS G-code that matches the monitor type — G0398, G0399, or G0400 — and CPT 95800/95801/95806 on a Medicare claim comes back CO-16 with remark N56, not a coverage denial.
  • A split-night study bills as 95811 alone, never 95810 plus 95811. The two codes are a mutually exclusive NCCI pair for the same date of service, and the second code denies CO-97 as included in the first.
  • The KX modifier on a CPAP rental claim is a documentation certification, not a formality. From the fourth month of therapy on, it certifies a compliance download on file showing PAP use ≥4 hours/night on ≥70% of nights across a consecutive 30-day window — missing that data behind the modifier is the denial, not the modifier itself.
  • Repeat sleep studies need a stated clinical reason, not just a new order. Substantial weight change, an insufficient clinical response, symptom recurrence after an initial good result, or re-qualification after a failed PAP trial are the accepted triggers — "repeat study" alone reads as a frequency violation.

Why sleep medicine denials are mostly rulebook mismatches, not coverage fights

Sleep medicine sits across three billing systems at once, and most of its denials come from applying the wrong one rather than from a payer genuinely disputing medical necessity. A facility-based polysomnogram or titration study bills as a professional/technical service under the medical benefit. A home sleep apnea test (HSAT) bills under the same benefit but uses a completely different code set depending on the payer — CPT for most commercial plans, HCPCS G-codes for Medicare DME MACs. And the CPAP device itself, once it goes home with the patient, moves into the durable medical equipment (DME) benefit, with its own capped-rental billing cycle, its own modifier rules, and its own compliance-documentation clock that runs independently of anything the sleep lab already submitted.

That three-way split is exactly where denials concentrate. A biller who's used to submitting CPT codes for a commercial HSAT sends the same code set to a Medicare DME MAC and gets an automatic rejection that looks like a coding error but is really a payer-identification error. A sleep lab that documents a split-night study correctly still loses the second code to a bundling edit if it bills both halves separately. A DME supplier with a fully compliant patient still gets denied if the KX modifier goes out before the compliance download is pulled and filed. None of these are medical-necessity disputes in the usual sense — they're claims built against the wrong rule, which means most of them get fixed by resubmission or a short, specific letter rather than a lengthy appeal.

The six denials that account for most sleep medicine write-offs

The table below lists each denial with its actual CARC, why it fires, the argument that gets it overturned or corrected, and the upstream fix that keeps it from recurring.

Common sleep medicine and CPAP claim denials, their CARC codes, and how each one resolves.
DenialWhy it firesAppeal / correction argumentPrevention
CO-16 (with remark N56) — wrong code set for a home sleep test CPT 95800, 95801, or 95806 submitted to a Medicare DME MAC instead of the required G-code, or a G-code sent to a commercial payer that only recognizes CPT for HSAT. Not an appeal — a corrected resubmission. The test performed and its documentation are unchanged; only the code was wrong for that payer's code set. Confirm the payer type before coding: Medicare DME MAC always gets G0398/G0399/G0400 by channel count, never the CPT equivalents. Build the code choice into the scheduling or intake step, not billing.
CO-50 — diagnostic sleep study, no qualifying indication The study was ordered and billed without a documented qualifying sign, symptom, or comorbidity on the chart, or without the matching ICD-10 code linked on the claim. If the indication was documented but not coded correctly, appeal with the chart note showing the qualifying finding (witnessed apneas, excessive daytime sleepiness, treatment-resistant hypertension, a high STOP-BANG score) and the corrected diagnosis pointer. Build the qualifying indication into the order itself, not just the encounter note, and confirm the ICD-10 code on the claim traces directly to that documented finding.
CO-50 — CPAP continued without compliance documentation A continued-coverage claim (month 4 onward) went out without objective adherence evidence on file — the payer's PAP policy treats ongoing coverage as conditional on proof of use, not just a working device. If the compliance download exists but wasn't submitted, appeal with the download showing ≥4 hours/night on ≥70% of nights during the required 30-day window, dated within the day-31-to-day-91 assessment period. Pull and file the compliance download before the fourth month's claim goes out, not after a denial forces a look-back.
CO-97 — full diagnostic and full titration billed separately for a split night 95810 and 95811 both submitted for the same date of service instead of 95811 alone; the codes are a mutually exclusive NCCI procedure-to-procedure pair because 95811 already includes the diagnostic portion. Correction, not appeal, when it really was one continuous night: drop 95810 and rebill 95811 alone for the full study. A true appeal applies only if the diagnostic and titration portions happened on two separate calendar nights — then the record has to show that split explicitly. Confirm at coding time whether the sleep tech's note describes one continuous overnight recording (95811 only) or two distinct nights (95810 for night one, 95811 for night two) before either code goes on a claim.
CO-151 — repeat sleep study exceeds frequency A second polysomnogram or additional HSAT was billed without documentation tying it to an accepted reason for repeating the test — the payer reads the claim as exceeding the number of studies its policy allows without justification. Appeal with the specific clinical trigger documented in the chart: substantial weight gain or loss since the last study, an insufficient clinical response to the current therapy, symptom recurrence after a prior good response, or re-qualification following a failed PAP trial requiring a repeat facility-based study. State the repeat-study reason in the order itself in the payer's own terms, not just "repeat sleep study" — the reviewer is checking for one of a short list of accepted triggers.
CO-4 — KX modifier missing on continued CPAP rental A continued-coverage rental claim (month 4 and beyond) was submitted without the KX modifier the payer's PAP policy requires once both initial and continued coverage criteria must be certified. Correction if the compliance criteria were actually met: add KX and resubmit with the supporting download in the file. Not correctable if the compliance data doesn't support it — billing KX without the evidence behind it isn't a documentation gap to fix, it's a claim that shouldn't go out that way. Build a claim-scrubber rule that blocks a month-4-or-later PAP rental line from submitting without both the KX modifier and a compliance download dated inside the required window.
Pro tip

Read the CARC before deciding how to fight it. A CO-16/N56 denial and a CO-50 denial look similar on an aging report — both show up as unpaid sleep study claims — but one needs a corrected code on a resubmission and the other needs a clinical appeal with chart documentation. Routing a code-set mismatch into the appeals queue instead of the same-day correction queue is one of the most common ways sleep lab AR ages past the point it should.

The HSAT code-set mismatch, in detail

This is the single highest-volume sleep medicine denial because it isn't a judgment call — it's a lookup table problem that gets skipped under volume. Medicare DME MACs classify a home sleep apnea test by the number of channels the monitor records, and assign a HCPCS G-code accordingly instead of recognizing the CPT unattended-study codes most commercial payers use.

Home sleep apnea test code sets: commercial CPT versus Medicare HCPCS G-code, by monitor type.
Monitor typeCommercial CPT (payer-dependent)Medicare HCPCS G-code
Type II (7+ channels, includes EEG)95800 — unattended, heart rate, oxygen saturation, respiratory analysis, sleep timeG0398
Type III (4+ channels, no EEG)95806 — unattended, heart rate, oxygen saturation, respiratory airflow and effortG0399 — the most commonly billed HSAT code under Medicare
Type IV (3+ channels, minimum airflow/oximetry)95801 — unattended, heart rate, oxygen saturation, respiratory analysis, no sleep timeG0400

The practical rule: confirm whether the claim is going to a Medicare DME MAC before the code is chosen, not after a denial. Some commercial payers also accept G-codes and some Medicare Advantage plans process CPT correctly through their own claims systems, so this isn't a universal Medicare-versus-commercial split — it's a per-payer setting that has to be confirmed and stored against that payer in the practice management system, the same way a payer's timely-filing limit or claim-form preference would be.

Split-night billing: one code, one continuous study

One continuous overnight recording
  • Diagnostic portion followed by CPAP or bi-level titration in the same overnight session bills as 95811 alone. The code's own definition already includes the diagnostic portion, so there's nothing left for 95810 to describe on that date of service.
Two separate calendar nights
  • A diagnostic study on one date of service and a titration on a later date bills as 95810 for the first night and 95811 for the second — but the chart has to show them as genuinely distinct sessions, not one recording split across midnight or a technician's shorthand for a single continuous study.

Sleep labs generally follow the American Academy of Sleep Medicine's split-night criteria when deciding whether to convert a diagnostic study into a same-night titration: an apnea-hypopnea index of at least 40 confirmed over a minimum of two hours of diagnostic recording, or an AHI of 20–40 supported by clinical judgment. ⚠️ That AASM threshold is a clinical protocol standard, not a universal CMS billing rule — check the specific split-night language in your MAC's polysomnography LCD and any commercial payer's sleep-study medical policy before treating it as the coding trigger, since a payer can require its own documentation of the conversion decision independent of the AASM criteria.

A worked resubmission: HSAT denied for the wrong code set

Because a CPT-versus-G-code mismatch usually means the test and its documentation were correct all along, this isn't framed as a clinical appeal — it's a corrected-claim cover letter that tells the payer exactly what changed and why the original service still stands.

Skeleton: corrected-claim letter for a home sleep apnea test denied CO-16/N56 for the wrong code set.
SectionWhat goes in it
HeaderPatient name/DOB (or internal identifier), member ID, original claim number, date of service, original denial code and remark code as they appear on the remittance (CO-16 / N56).
Opening line"This is a corrected claim submission for the home sleep apnea test performed on [date]. The original claim was denied under CARC CO-16 / RARC N56 for an invalid procedure code. The test and its supporting documentation are unchanged; only the procedure code has been corrected to match [payer]'s required code set."
What changedState the original code billed (e.g., CPT 95806) and the corrected code now submitted (e.g., HCPCS G0399), and identify the monitor type/channel count that determines the correct G-code.
Supporting attachmentThe original sleep study order with the qualifying indication, the technician's or interpreting physician's report confirming the monitor type used, and the corrected CMS-1500 or claim form.
ClosingRequest reprocessing of the corrected claim under the original date of service and ask for confirmation of the corrected code's acceptance in writing, with a named contact and callback number for questions.

Keep this letter short. A reviewer processing a corrected claim is looking to confirm the code now matches their system's expected set, not to re-adjudicate medical necessity that was never in question — a letter padded with clinical justification for a denial that was never about clinical justification slows the reprocessing down rather than speeding it up.

Frequently asked questions

Why does Medicare deny a home sleep apnea test billed with CPT code 95806?

Because Medicare DME MACs don't recognize CPT 95806, 95800, or 95801 for a home sleep apnea test at all — they require the equivalent HCPCS G-code instead: G0398 for a Type II monitor (7 or more channels), G0399 for a Type III monitor (4 or more channels, the most commonly billed code and the Medicare equivalent of CPT 95806), or G0400 for a Type IV monitor (3 or more channels). Submitting the CPT code to a Medicare DME MAC typically comes back CO-16 with remark code N56, meaning the procedure code isn't valid for the service billed. Since the test itself and its documentation are usually fine, the fix is a corrected resubmission with the matching G-code, not a medical-necessity appeal.

What documentation prevents a CO-50 medical-necessity denial on a diagnostic sleep study?

The order has to show the qualifying signs, symptoms, and comorbidities your payer's coverage policy lists — typically habitual loud snoring, witnessed apneas or gasping, excessive daytime sleepiness, or a comorbidity such as treatment-resistant hypertension, atrial fibrillation, or a high STOP-BANG or Epworth score — documented before the test is ordered, with the corresponding ICD-10 code linked on the claim. A study ordered on a chart that only says "rule out sleep apnea" without a documented indication is the single most common way this denial happens, because the payer has nothing on the claim tying the test to a covered indication.

Can a full diagnostic sleep study and a full CPAP titration be billed as two separate codes for the same night?

No. When a diagnostic study and a CPAP titration happen on the same night, the correct code is 95811 alone — it's defined to include the diagnostic portion, and 95810 and 95811 are a mutually exclusive NCCI procedure-to-procedure edit pair for the same date of service. Billing both codes for one split night gets the second one denied CO-97 as included in the payment for the other. The appeal, when it's actually correct to split the studies into two separate visits, is proving in the record that the diagnostic and titration portions happened on two distinct calendar nights, not one continuous recording.

When is the KX modifier required on a continued CPAP rental claim, and what does it certify?

The KX modifier certifies that the documentation in the supplier's file supports the coverage criteria in the payer's PAP policy for that billing period. For the first through third months of therapy, it certifies the initial coverage criteria were met. From the fourth month's claim onward, it certifies both the initial criteria and the continued-coverage criteria — objective evidence, usually a compliance download, showing PAP use of 4 or more hours per night on at least 70% of nights during a consecutive 30-day period assessed between day 31 and day 91 of therapy. Appending KX without that compliance data on file, or omitting it when it's required, is what triggers the denial; the fix is pulling the download before the claim goes out, not after.
