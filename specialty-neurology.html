Neurology billing and coding: the complete 2026 guide.

Neurology denies at roughly 18% by industry benchmarking, against 5–10% for most other specialties, because it stacks dense same-day testing bundles, a high-cost injectable gated behind diary documentation, MAC coverage policy that turns over on a rolling basis rather than once a year, and diagnosis coding where the specific code — not the category — decides coverage. This guide covers the full revenue cycle: what actually changed in late 2025 and early 2026 (with the retired-vs-current article and LCD numbers nobody else tracks), every core procedure family, and the denial-to-appeal pairing most competitor guides treat as an afterthought.

Reviewed by Phares A., MD · Physiotherapist, CPC (AAPC) · Updated August 2026

Key takeaways

  • The G35 header is dead for billing, and it has real replacements. Unspecified "multiple sclerosis" no longer clears HIPAA transactions — you need phenotype plus, for progressive forms, activity status: G35.A, G35.B0/B1/B2, G35.C0/C1/C2, or G35.D as a genuine placeholder.
  • Article and LCD numbers changed under your feet in late 2025 and early 2026. Noridian retired its NCS/EMG article A54992 and replaced it same-day with A54969. Five MACs retired their Botulinum Toxin LCDs 2/21/2026 and replaced them 2/22/2026 — one under a renamed contractor (NGS is now listed as Wellpoint Federal), one (WPS) with a six-week coverage gap before its replacement took effect.
  • The EMG/NCS bundling trap isn't the add-on pairing itself — it's the standalone codes. 95885-95887 are designed to be billed with 95907-95913. The denial shows up when 95860-95864 (standalone EMG) is stacked on top of that combination for the same limb.
  • Denial-to-appeal pairing is where neurology guides consistently go thin. A CARC code and a root cause aren't an appeal strategy. Every denial in this guide is paired with the specific argument, the citation to use, and the upstream fix.

Neurology billing at a glance

Four structural problems drive most of neurology's denial volume: dense same-day bundling across EMG, NCS, and EEG; a chronic-migraine injectable with a documentation bar most practices under-build; diagnosis specificity that Medicare LCDs and commercial policy use as a hard coverage gate; and MAC-specific coverage articles that get renumbered and retired far more often than most billers check for. Here's the shape of it before the detail.

None of these four is exotic. What makes neurology expensive to bill is that a single patient — a chronic migraine workup that includes an EMG for a separate complaint, say — can touch three or four of them on one encounter, and each one has its own coverage logic, its own MAC-specific policy, and its own documentation bar.

What changed recently

Three things changed between late 2025 and early 2026 that matter operationally, not as trivia. All three were verified directly against live sources for this build, and all three are the kind of change that breaks silently — a scrubber rule or order set that still cites the old number keeps running, it just stops being current.

The FY2026 G35 multiple sclerosis restructuring. We confirmed this live against the current ICD-10-CM code set: the bare G35 header ("Multiple sclerosis") is a category heading only and does not clear HIPAA transactions — it never did, but the restructuring makes the point unavoidable because there's no longer a plausible "close enough" specific code sitting one level down. Billing now requires phenotype, and for progressive disease, activity status:

Noridian's EMG/NCS article split. Noridian Healthcare Solutions retired article A54992 ("Billing and Coding: Nerve Conduction Studies and Electromyography") on 10/23/2025 and published A54969, under the identical title, effective the same day. If your documentation template, payer policy library, or claims scrubber still cites A54992, the citation itself is now wrong even if nothing about the underlying coverage criteria changed — and on audit, a reference to a retired article number reads as evidence your compliance references aren't current, which is exactly the kind of finding that turns a routine chart pull into a broader review.

The Botulinum Toxin LCD retirement wave. Five MACs retired their Botulinum Toxin LCDs effective 2/21/2026 and replaced them 2/22/2026, verified live against the CMS Coverage Database:

The practical fallout across all three changes: any order set, scrubber rule, or documentation template that cites a retired article or LCD number is technically citing dead policy, even where the clinical criteria carried over unchanged. Audit your references against the tables above before your next EMG, NCS, or Botox claim goes out, not after a denial or an audit letter forces the question.

E/M coding for neurology

Established-patient office visits (99202–99215) are billed on either total time or medical decision making, whichever the note more fully supports — not both stacked together. What makes neurology's MDM genuinely higher-complexity, and worth documenting as such, is multiple chronic neurologic conditions managed concurrently, independent interpretation of imaging or neurophysiologic testing (EEG, EMG, NCS) the physician reads personally rather than relying on someone else's report, and high-risk medication management — AEDs with narrow therapeutic windows, disease-modifying MS therapies with monitoring requirements, or anticoagulation in a stroke patient. A 99215-level visit that only documents "follow-up, stable" reads as a 99213 on audit regardless of how complex the actual decision-making was; the complexity has to be visible in the note, not just present in the physician's head.

Two coding paths get missed constantly. Prolonged services beyond the time threshold for the highest-level E/M code have separate codes depending on payer: 99417 for commercial payers that follow CPT's prolonged-services structure, and G2212 for Medicare, which uses its own code and its own time threshold rather than CPT's. Billing 99417 on a Medicare claim, or vice versa, is a straightforward crosswalk error that denies on code-payer mismatch. Second, a dementia or cognitive-decline workup billed as a standard E/M routinely undercodes the actual work; 99483, the cognitive assessment and care planning code, covers the standardized cognitive testing, functional assessment, safety evaluation, and care plan documentation a real dementia workup involves, and reimburses at a materially higher level than a comparable E/M — it's the single most commonly missed higher-value code in general neurology practice.

Modifier 25 belongs on the E/M when a significant, separately identifiable evaluation happens the same day as a minor procedure or a diagnostic test — not routinely, and never as a way to guarantee both claims pay. The trap that costs neurology practices real money: billing an E/M same-day as an EMG/NCS or EEG without documentation that the visit involved decision-making genuinely separate from ordering and reviewing the test itself. If the E/M note is essentially "patient here for EMG, will proceed with testing," that's not a modifier-25 visit — it's part of the testing encounter. The full time-vs-MDM decision tree and the specific EMG/EEG-same-day modifier-25 pattern are in our neurology modifiers guide.

EMG and nerve conduction studies

This is neurology's highest-volume diagnostic family and its densest bundling risk, because the same limb can legitimately be billed several different ways depending on what was actually studied.

Core EMG/NCS code structure and the bundling logic that governs it.
CodeServiceBilling logic
95907–95913Nerve conduction studies, tiered by number of studies performed (roughly 1–2 studies through 13 or more)Primary code for the NCS component; select the tier by actual study count, not by convention
95885Needle EMG, limited study, each extremityAdd-on to a same-day NCS code — not separately payable alone
95886Needle EMG, complete study, each extremityAdd-on to a same-day NCS code; requires at least five muscles studied, spanning three nerves or four spinal levels, to support "complete" over "limited"
95887Needle EMG, non-extremity muscles (e.g., cranial nerve or thoracic paraspinal)Add-on to a same-day NCS code, for muscles outside the standard extremity groups
95860–95864Standalone needle EMG, by extremity count, without a same-day NCSUsed only when no NCS was performed the same date; billing this alongside 95885–95887 for the same limb is the bundling trap below

The add-on relationship between 95885–95887 and 95907–95913 is intentional, not a bundling edit to route around — CMS built these codes to be reported together, and an add-on code billed without a payable primary code on the same claim denies outright. The genuine trap sits one layer over: billing a standalone EMG code (95860–95864) for a limb that was also billed under the NCS-plus-add-on combination the same date. That pattern usually traces to an ordering or workflow error — two separate orders for what should have been one combined study — rather than a coding decision, and it's the pairing most likely to trigger a CO-97 bundling denial or draw audit attention if it recurs.

Documentation has to name the actual nerves and muscles studied — "bilateral upper extremity NCS" in a note doesn't support the tier or the add-on level billed; the report needs to list which specific nerves (median, ulnar, radial, etc.) and which specific muscles were tested, because that's what a payer or auditor checks the billed tier against. Unit-count logic for 95886 follows directly from the five-muscle, three-nerve-or-four-spinal-level threshold above: fall short of that and the study supports 95885 (limited), not 95886 (complete), regardless of what the order said. And the retired-vs-current article trap from the section above lands squarely here — if your EMG/NCS documentation policy or scrubber still references Noridian's A54992, it's citing a dead article; the current one is A54969, same title, effective 10/23/2025. EEG coding: routine, ambulatory, and video monitoring

EEG billing splits along two axes that get confused constantly: how the recording was obtained (routine office EEG, ambulatory take-home monitoring, or inpatient/outpatient video-EEG), and who owns the equipment versus who interprets the tracing.

  • 1Routine EEG (roughly the 95812–95816 family). Distinguished by recording duration and state — awake, drowsy, and asleep recordings are documented and coded differently, and the report has to state which states were actually captured, not just that "a routine EEG was performed." A study ordered to capture sleep-activated abnormalities that never actually records sleep doesn't support the sleep-inclusive code, however the order was written.
  • 2Ambulatory EEG. The take-home setup and application is billed once per monitoring period, not per day of recording — billing the setup code repeatedly across a single multi-day ambulatory study is a frequency error that denies or, worse, goes uncaught and simply overbills the payer.
  • 3Video / long-term monitoring (roughly the 95700–95726 family). Tiered by monitoring duration and complexity rather than a single flat code; inpatient epilepsy-monitoring-unit admissions and outpatient ambulatory video-EEG use different codes within this range, and duration has to be documented precisely enough to support the tier billed.

The global-versus-component split works the same way it does for EMG and NCS: bill globally only when your practice owns the recording equipment, employs the technologist, and your physician performs the interpretation. Bill 26 (professional component) when your physician interprets a tracing recorded on equipment your practice doesn't own — common for hospital-based epilepsy-monitoring-unit reads — and TC (technical component) when your equipment and staff produced the recording but the interpretation happened elsewhere. Billing globally on a hospital-recorded study creates the same duplicate-claim conflict covered in the modifiers section below.

Prior authorization is where video-EEG denials concentrate. Most commercial payers and a growing share of Medicare Advantage plans require authorization once continuous monitoring extends past roughly 24 to 48 hours, and some gate any inpatient long-term monitoring admission regardless of length. The documentation that actually clears review is seizure semiology and frequency detail, prior antiepileptic drug trials with outcomes rather than a bare medication list, and the specific clinical question the monitoring is meant to resolve — pre-surgical localization, distinguishing epileptic from non-epileptic events, or classifying an ambiguous seizure type. Requesting authorization with that detail in hand before the admission, rather than reconstructing it after a CO-197 denial, is the difference between a routine approval and a peer-to-peer fight. Full code-by-code detail and the complete component-split logic are in our EEG billing and coding guide.

Botox for chronic migraine

OnabotulinumtoxinA for chronic migraine bills as HCPCS J0585 (the drug) alongside CPT 64615 (chemodenervation of muscles innervated by the facial, trigeminal, cervical spinal, and accessory nerves for chronic migraine) — and it should never be billed with 64612, the standard facial chemodenervation code used for non-migraine indications like blepharospasm or hemifacial spasm. Reporting both codes for what is clinically one injection session is an unbundling error, not two separately payable services, and it's an easy pattern for a payer's claims-edit system to catch.

Medical necessity turns on the chronic migraine definition: headache on 15 or more days per month for at least three months, with at least 8 of those days meeting migraine criteria or responding to a triptan. That threshold has to be documented, not asserted — a headache diary or calendar, ideally covering at least a full month immediately before the treatment request, is what most MAC LCDs and commercial policies actually check for. A note that says "patient reports frequent migraines" without day-count detail is the single most common reason a well-indicated Botox claim still denies as not medically necessary.

Injection technique follows the PREEMPT protocol from the FDA labeling: a fixed-site, fixed-dose regimen delivering a minimum of 155 units across 31 sites spanning seven head-and-neck muscle groups, with an optional "follow-the-pain" extension of up to 40 additional units across up to 8 additional sites (frontalis, temporalis, occipitalis, cervical paraspinal, and trapezius) for a maximum of 195 units across 39 sites. The operative note needs to reflect the actual sites and total dose delivered, matched against the units of J0585 billed — a documented 155-unit, 31-site injection billed against a claim for 195 units is a units-versus-documentation mismatch that a payer's claims-edit system, or an auditor, will catch immediately.

Pro tip

OnabotulinumtoxinA's FDA labeling for chronic migraine does not require a documented failed oral preventive before treatment — that requirement, where it appears, is a payer policy overlay, not an FDA condition. If a prior-auth denial cites "no failed oral preventive" as the reason and your payer's own published policy doesn't actually require one for this indication, that's an appealable point worth checking before assuming the denial is correct.

Full dosing documentation templates, the diary format payers actually accept, and MAC-by-MAC coverage detail beyond the retirement wave above are in our Botox for chronic migraine billing guide.

Neurology's medical necessity runs on diagnosis specificity more than almost any category of billing outside cardiology, because the specific code — not the disease category — decides what a payer's coverage policy will pay for. Four categories carry most of the volume, and all of the codes below were verified live against the FY2026 ICD-10-CM code set.

Modifiers

Neurology's modifier logic centers on three decisions: whether the E/M is genuinely separate from same-day testing, whether two same-day procedures are genuinely distinct, and who owns the equipment behind an interpreted study.

26 / TC
  • 26 — professional component only; physician interprets an EEG or EMG/NCS tracing recorded on equipment the practice doesn't own. Default for hospital-based EEG reads and off-site EMG interpretation.
  • TC — technical component only; equipment, supplies, and technologist time, billed by whoever owns the equipment regardless of who reads the study.
59 / XS
  • Distinct procedural service — a genuinely separate anatomic site, structure, or session, not the same study reported twice or a workflow artifact from a duplicate order.
  • XS (separate structure) is usually the right choice in neurology over generic 59, since most defensible same-day unbundling here is anatomic — a genuinely separate limb or nerve territory.
95 (telehealth)
  • Synchronous audio-video telehealth for follow-up visits — medication management, stable chronic-condition checks — where the payer's telehealth policy covers the code billed.
  • Not appropriate for anything requiring hands-on exam findings (new focal deficit, gait assessment) the visit note claims to have captured.
25 on a testing-only visit
  • Modifier 25 requires a significant, separately identifiable E/M — not routine attachment to guarantee both the visit and a same-day EMG, NCS, or EEG both pay. A note that's essentially "here for testing, proceeding with testing" doesn't support it, and this is the single most common modifier error the specialty's audits flag.

The component-split error costs the same way it does in every specialty that has one: billing 26 and TC together (the global fee) for a study recorded on equipment the practice doesn't own creates a conflicting claim once the facility bills its own technical component for the same date and patient — easy for a payer to catch, because the two claims contradict each other on their face. The complete modifier reference, including the telehealth POS/modifier pairing by payer type, is in our neurology modifiers guide.

NCCI edits, MUEs, and bundling

Two CMS-published values decide whether a bundled or capped neurology code is billable at all, and both are pair- and code-specific — never a guess, and never something to infer from "we've always billed it that way."

The NCCI modifier indicator attached to a Column 1/Column 2 code pair:

  • 0Never bypassable. No modifier overrides it, however well the record supports separateness.
  • 1Bypassable with documentation. An NCCI-associated modifier (59 or the specific X-modifier) can override the edit, but only where the record shows the second service was genuinely distinct — separate site, session, or structure.
  • 9Edit deleted. The pair no longer applies.

The MUE Adjudication Indicator (MAI) attached to a per-code, per-day unit cap:

  • 1Claim-line edit. Units above the cap deny that line; a genuinely repeated study can sometimes be split across lines with the correct modifier and documentation.
  • 2Absolute, date-of-service edit. CMS treats exceeding it as clinically implausible. No appeal path exists — confirm the MAI before staff spend time on one.
  • 3Appealable, date-of-service edit. A real path exists with documentation the excess units reflect genuinely distinct, medically necessary sessions.

Where these show up most in neurology: the 95885–95887 EMG add-on codes exist specifically to be reported with the 95907–95913 NCS tier, so that pairing is not an edit to route around — it's the intended billing structure. The pairing that actually needs a modifier-indicator check is 95860–95864 (standalone EMG) against the combined NCS-plus-add-on billing for the same limb, same date; whether that combination is bypassable, and under what documentation, is pair-specific and needs to be confirmed in the CMS NCCI PTP Edits Lookup Tool rather than assumed. Same-day EEG and EMG on a patient being worked up for both seizure and neuropathic symptoms, and same-day Botox injection with an E/M, are the other two combinations most likely to trigger a Column 1/Column 2 edit or draw a modifier-25 audit flag.

⚠️ On specificity: this build could not open CMS's primary NCCI PTP edit file or MUE file directly to confirm the exact modifier-indicator and MAI values for the EMG/NCS pairs above — CMS's coverage-database and NCCI file pages returned an access error to every automated fetch attempt made while researching this page, consistent with what we've found building this content for other specialties. Treat the structural framework above (which pairing is the actual risk, and why) as confirmed; verify the specific indicator or MAI value for any pair in the CMS NCCI PTP Edits Lookup Tool before building it into a scrubber rule, because these values change quarterly and the lookup tool is the only source that's always current. The complete bundling reference, organized by procedure family, is in our neurology NCCI edits and MUE limits guide.

Conclusion and next step

Neurology's ~18% denial rate isn't one problem, it's four compounding ones: coding references that go stale faster than most billers check (the G35 restructuring, the retired Noridian article, the Botox LCD wave), same-day bundling that looks simple until the standalone-versus-add-on distinction gets missed, diagnosis specificity that decides coverage outright, and a denial-to-appeal process that too many practices run generically instead of by CARC-specific argument. Each one is fixable on its own. Together, they're the difference between a neurology practice that's leaving five to ten points of collectible revenue on the table every month and one that isn't.

Frequently asked questions

Why is the unspecified G35 code no longer valid for multiple sclerosis claims?

The FY2026 ICD-10-CM restructuring split multiple sclerosis into phenotype-specific codes, and the bare G35 header ("Multiple sclerosis") is a category heading only — it was never billable and still is not. You now have to code the phenotype and, for progressive forms, activity status: G35.A for relapsing-remitting, G35.B0/B1/B2 for primary progressive (unspecified, active, or non-active), G35.C0/C1/C2 for secondary progressive by the same activity split, or G35.D if the phenotype genuinely can't be determined yet. G35.D is billable, but it should be a placeholder while workup continues, not a permanent habit — payers increasingly expect the phenotype-specific code once the diagnosis is established.

What changed in Noridian's EMG and nerve conduction study coverage article?

Noridian retired article A54992 (Billing and Coding: Nerve Conduction Studies and Electromyography) on 10/23/2025 and replaced it the same day with A54969 under the identical title. If a scrubber rule, order set, or documentation template still cites A54992, it's referencing a dead article — the citation itself becomes a compliance problem even if the underlying coverage criteria carried over unchanged, because an auditor checking your documentation against a retired article number can't confirm you're following current policy.

Why did Botulinum Toxin LCDs get replaced in February 2026?

Five MACs retired their Botulinum Toxin LCDs effective 2/21/2026 — WPS (L34635), National Government Services (L33646), and CGS (L33949) — and replaced them the next day, 2/22/2026, with renumbered LCDs: Palmetto L39836, CGS L39857, Noridian L35172 and L35170, and a new L39832 now published under Wellpoint Federal rather than NGS. Wellpoint Federal shares NGS's contract numbers in CMS's system, which reflects NGS's ownership transition — if your team is still searching the coverage database for "National Government Services" botulinum toxin coverage, the current LCD for that jurisdiction now lists under Wellpoint Federal. WPS's replacement, L39909, didn't take effect until 4/2/2026, roughly six weeks after L34635 retired — confirm what your MAC's operative LCD was for any Botox claim with a date of service in that window.

Can we bill 95885-95887 with 95907-95913 the same day?

Yes — that's the intended pairing. 95885 (limited extremity EMG), 95886 (complete extremity EMG), and 95887 (non-extremity EMG) are add-on codes that exist specifically to be reported with a same-day nerve conduction study from the 95907-95913 tier; they aren't separately payable on their own and CMS will deny an add-on code billed without its primary code on the same claim. The edit to actually watch is billing 95860-95864 (standalone EMG without a same-day NCS) alongside the 95885-95887 add-ons for the same limb on the same date — that combination usually reflects a workflow or ordering error rather than two genuinely distinct services, and it's the pairing most likely to draw a bundling denial or audit attention.

What's the diagnostic threshold for Botox coverage in chronic migraine?

The clinical definition of chronic migraine used to establish medical necessity is a headache on 15 or more days per month for at least three months, with at least 8 of those days meeting migraine or migraine-responsive-to-triptan criteria. That threshold has to be supported in the chart — typically with a headache diary or calendar covering at least one full month before the request — not just asserted in a note. Most payers also want at least one documented failed or contraindicated oral preventive, even though onabotulinumtoxinA's FDA labeling for chronic migraine doesn't itself require a failed-oral trial; that's a payer overlay, not an FDA requirement, and it's worth knowing the difference when you're pushing back on a denial.

Why do our EMG and NCS claims deny as bundled (CO-97)?

Almost always because the claim reports a standalone EMG code (95860-95864) alongside the tiered NCS-plus-EMG combination (95907-95913 with the 95885-95887 add-ons) for the same extremity, same date — the payer's system reads that as double-billing the EMG component. The fix starts with the order: if the study genuinely needs both NCS and needle EMG on a limb, it should be ordered and billed as the combined pathway, not stacked with a separate standalone EMG code. If two genuinely separate extremities were studied by design, the claim needs to state that distinctly rather than relying on the payer to infer it.

Do we need prior authorization for video EEG monitoring?

Most commercial payers and a growing share of Medicare Advantage plans require prior authorization once continuous video-EEG monitoring extends past roughly 24 to 48 hours, and some require it for any inpatient long-term monitoring admission regardless of duration. The documentation that actually gets it approved is seizure frequency and semiology detail, prior AED trials and their outcomes (not just a drug list), and the specific localization question the monitoring is meant to answer — pre-surgical workup, differentiating epileptic from non-epileptic events, or classifying an unclear seizure type. Requesting the authorization before the admission, with that detail already in hand, avoids the retrospective medical-necessity fight that shows up as a CO-197 denial after the stay.

Why is neurology's denial rate so much higher than other specialties?

Neurology runs roughly 18% average denial rates against 5-10% for most other specialties, by industry benchmarking, because it stacks several hard problems at once: diagnostic testing with dense same-day bundling rules (EMG/NCS, EEG), a high-cost injectable (Botox) that most payers gate behind prior authorization and diary documentation, MAC-specific coverage policy that changes on a rolling basis rather than once a year, and diagnosis coding — stroke, MS, epilepsy, migraine — where the specific code, not just the category, decides whether a study or procedure is covered at all. Each of those is manageable individually; the volume of claims that trip more than one at once is what pushes the aggregate rate up.
