Insurance eligibility and benefit verification: the cheapest denial is the one that never happens.

Eligibility failures are the largest single category of avoidable denial in outpatient billing, and they are also the easiest to prevent. Every one of them is decided before the patient is seen, which means the fix is operational rather than clerical, and it costs a fraction of what reworking the claim afterwards does. This guide covers the full picture: what eligibility actually confirms, how the check itself works under the hood, where coverage that looks active still fails, what happens when it does, and how to build the whole thing into a workflow that runs itself.

Verify via
Payer portal, clearinghouse, or direct EDI

Most practices route through a clearinghouse so one interface can check every payer, rather than logging into each payer's own portal separately.

Standard transaction
ANSI X12 270 (inquiry) / 271 (response)

The HIPAA-mandated electronic transaction set for eligibility inquiries. Every certified payer, clearinghouse, and practice management system speaks this format.

Typical turnaround
Real-time (interactive) or same-day (batch)

A real-time inquiry returns in seconds during scheduling or check-in. A batch run against the next day's full schedule returns within the processing window, not instantly.

Key takeaways

  • Eligibility is not the same as benefits. Active coverage tells you the policy exists; benefits tell you whether this service is payable.
  • Verify the day before, not at check-in. A problem found the night before can be solved by a phone call; the same problem at the desk cannot.
  • Coordination of benefits drives silent denials. Dual coverage that nobody re-asked about produces claims billed to the wrong primary.
  • Deductible position determines what you collect. Knowing it before the visit is the difference between collecting at the desk and chasing a statement.
  • A "successful" check just means the policy is active. The 271 response that confirms coverage exists is not the same thing as confirming a specific service will be paid.

Eligibility, benefits and authorisation are three different checks

Practices routinely conflate these, and the conflation is expensive. Eligibility confirms the patient has active coverage on the date of service under the plan presented. Benefits confirm whether the specific service is covered, at what level, subject to which deductible, copay and coinsurance, and whether frequency or visit limits apply. Authorisation confirms the payer has approved this particular service in advance.

A patient can be perfectly eligible and still generate a denied claim because the service is excluded, the annual limit is exhausted, or authorisation was never obtained. Front desks that check only active status feel they have done eligibility work, and the denials arrive weeks later looking like a billing problem rather than an intake one.

The three checks also sit on different owners in most practices, which is part of why they get conflated. Eligibility is usually a front-desk or scheduling function, run in bulk and largely automatable. Benefits verification often falls to whoever is fielding the phone call to the payer, because the 270/271 transaction that handles eligibility does not always return the granular, service-specific detail a benefits investigation needs. Authorisation is frequently owned by clinical staff or a dedicated prior-auth coordinator, because it requires clinical justification the front desk cannot supply. When ownership is split three ways and nobody is accountable for the handoff between them, gaps open at exactly the seams — a patient whose eligibility was confirmed but whose benefits were never checked, or whose benefits were checked but whose authorisation request was never filed.

What an eligibility check actually confirms — and what it doesn't

This is the gap that catches practices most often, because an eligibility response looks authoritative even when it's answering a narrower question than the one that matters. A 271 response tells you the payer found the member, that the policy is active on the date you asked about, and usually the effective and termination dates on file. Depending on the payer, it may also return a set of eligibility-and-benefit (EB) segments with copay, coinsurance and deductible figures for common service types. It does not, as a rule, tell you whether one specific CPT or HCPCS code for one specific planned service is covered, whether an annual visit limit has already been exhausted, or whether the plan requires prior authorization for that service. Those are benefits questions, and getting an answer to them usually requires either a richer benefits inquiry (a more specific 270 built with the correct service type code) or a phone call to the payer.

The practical risk is that a clean, active-coverage 271 response reads as reassurance and the office stops there. For routine, low-cost, broadly-covered services — an office visit, a basic lab panel — that's usually fine, because the odds of a coverage exclusion are low enough not to justify a benefits call every time. For anything non-routine, high-cost, or authorization-dependent, treating an active-eligibility response as confirmation that the service will be paid is exactly the mistake that produces a denial weeks later that looks, on the surface, like a coding or billing failure.

Run it as a batch, the night before

The highest-return configuration is a batch eligibility run against tomorrow's full schedule, executed automatically each evening, with exceptions surfaced to the front desk each morning. This turns eligibility from a per-patient task performed under time pressure at the desk into an exception report handled before the clinic opens.

The operational difference is decisive. A terminated policy discovered the night before allows a phone call, a coverage update and a claim that pays. The same discovery at check-in, with a waiting room and a provider running behind, produces a visit delivered on hope. Practices that make this single change usually see eligibility-related denials fall sharply within a month, without any change to coding or claims work.

The night-before cadence also changes who does the work and how much of it there is. Checking eligibility one patient at a time, at the desk, competes directly with checking patients in, answering the phone, and everything else a front desk handles during clinic hours — which is exactly why it gets skipped or rushed under pressure. A batch run happens against the whole next-day schedule at once, outside clinic hours, and produces a short list of genuine exceptions rather than a stack of individual lookups. The staff time that used to go into running dozens of routine checks that mostly come back clean goes instead into the handful that actually need a phone call. Practices that run Monday's batch on Friday evening, rather than Sunday night, also give themselves a full business day to resolve weekend-discovered problems before the clinic reopens, which matters more than it sounds for any practice that sees patients Monday morning.

Pro tip

Ask "has anything changed with your insurance?" at every visit, not just for new patients. Coverage changes with jobs, marriages, births and open enrolment, and patients almost never volunteer it because they do not think of it as your concern. That single scripted question at check-in prevents more coordination-of-benefits denials than any system configuration.

Coordination of benefits: how "primary" gets decided

When a patient has two active policies, the claim has to go to the right one first, and payers determine primacy using a set of standard rules rather than whichever card the patient happens to hand over. Coverage through an employer is generally primary over coverage held as a dependent on someone else's plan. For a dependent child covered under both parents' plans, industry practice widely follows the "birthday rule": the parent whose birthday (month and day, not year) falls earlier in the calendar year carries the primary plan for that child, regardless of which parent is older. Medicare has its own coordination rules relative to employer coverage that depend on employer size and the patient's reason for Medicare eligibility. None of these rules are visible from an eligibility check alone — the 271 response tells you a policy is active, not where it ranks against a patient's other coverage — so getting coordination of benefits right depends on asking the question directly and keeping the answer current in the patient's record, not on anything the eligibility transaction surfaces automatically.

The failure mode is quiet rather than dramatic. A claim goes to what your system has on file as primary, the payer that's now actually secondary processes it and pays nothing because it isn't primary anymore, and the claim comes back looking like a routine denial rather than a coordination problem — unless someone recognizes the pattern. Re-asking the COB question at every visit, not only the first one, is the single highest-leverage habit for keeping this from happening.

How the check actually happens: portals, clearinghouses, and the 270/271

Everything above describes what to check and when. What actually happens on the wire is a standardized electronic conversation. Your practice management system, or a staff member working directly in a portal, generates a 270 eligibility inquiry — patient name, date of birth, member ID, the payer, the rendering provider's NPI, and usually a service type code describing the general category of care being asked about. That inquiry goes out one of two ways: straight to the payer's own system, or through a clearinghouse that sits between your practice and every payer you work with.

A direct connection to a single payer's portal makes sense when that payer dominates your patient mix, or when you're troubleshooting a specific case and want the payer's own interface rather than a summarized pass-through. For everyone else, a clearinghouse is the more common setup, because it lets one login and one interface check coverage across Medicare, Medicaid, and every commercial payer a practice deals with, rather than maintaining a dozen separate portal credentials and re-keying the same patient information into each one.

The payer's system answers with a 271 — active or inactive, the dates that bracket coverage, and whatever benefit detail that payer chooses to include. A "successful" 271 means the transaction completed and the member was found; it does not by itself mean the visit is fully covered, which is exactly the distinction the section above walks through in more detail. The service type code submitted on the 270 also matters more than most offices realize: a generic code returns generic benefit detail, while a more specific one aimed at the actual planned service tends to return a more useful answer, when the payer supports that level of granularity at all. The mechanics of real-time versus batch processing, when a clearinghouse earns its cost over going direct to a payer, and what the service type code on your 270 actually controls, are covered in full in our guide to payer portals, clearinghouses, and the 270/271 transaction.

Coverage that looks active but isn't: the gaps a check has to catch

The uncomfortable reality is that an eligibility check can return a clean, active response and the claim can still deny for a coverage reason. This isn't the system lying to you; it's the system telling you the truth as of whenever its data was last synchronized, which is not always the truth as of today. A secondary payer that hasn't been notified of a coordination-of-benefits change, a termed policy sitting in a cache that hasn't refreshed, a plan that changed mid-year without the front desk being told, a newborn who exists on the subscriber's policy but hasn't been loaded into the payer's eligibility system yet — all of these produce a 271 that looks fine and a claim that doesn't pay.

None of these are eligibility-check failures in the sense of a broken transaction. They're timing and data-currency problems that a single automated check, run once, cannot catch on its own. Catching them requires knowing which scenarios recur often enough to build a specific verification step around — asking the COB question at every visit rather than only the first, re-checking anything with a plan-change flag, confirming dependent coverage separately for anything involving a newborn or a recently added family member. Take the newborn example on its own: a baby is typically covered under a parent's policy from birth, but most payers require the family to actually notify them and add the dependent before that coverage shows up in the eligibility system, sometimes weeks later. A practice that runs a standard eligibility check for that infant's first well-visit can get back an inactive or not-found response even though the coverage is, in every real sense, already in force — and the fix isn't a different eligibility transaction, it's knowing to ask the parent directly whether the newborn has been added to the policy yet.

The specific scenarios that create this kind of false confidence, what each one actually looks like on the claim, and the operational step that catches it before the visit, are laid out in our guide to coverage gap scenarios.

When eligibility fails anyway: the CARC codes and what they mean

When an eligibility problem does make it through to a submitted claim, it comes back as a denial carrying a specific Claim Adjustment Reason Code, or CARC. Two of them account for most eligibility-driven denials. CARC CO-27 means "expenses incurred after coverage terminated" — the service was delivered on a date after the policy had already ended. CARC CO-31 means "patient cannot be identified as our insured" — the payer has no record matching this patient to this policy at all, whether because of a data mismatch, a plan the patient was never actually enrolled in, or coverage that was never active in the first place.

The two CARC codes most commonly tied to eligibility failures.
CARCMeaningTypical root cause
CO-27Expenses incurred after coverage terminatedPolicy ended before the date of service; eligibility check ran against stale data or wasn't run at all
CO-31Patient cannot be identified as our insuredMember ID, name, or DOB mismatch; patient never enrolled under the plan presented; card outlived the policy

Both of these are structurally different from a coding denial, and that difference matters for how you respond. A coding denial usually means the service was covered but described or documented wrong on the claim — something correctable through a corrected claim or appeal. An eligibility denial usually means the service, as delivered on that date to that patient, genuinely wasn't covered. There's rarely a clinical or documentation argument that changes that fact after the visit has already happened, which is why eligibility denials are prevented rather than appealed. The full breakdown of both codes, how eligibility failures cascade from a missed check into a denied claim, and why the fix is almost never a successful appeal, is in our guide to eligibility-driven denials.

Building this into a workflow instead of a habit

Everything in this guide works only if it happens reliably, for every patient, without depending on any one person remembering to do it. That means a defined cadence — a batch run against tomorrow's full schedule every evening, a same-day re-check for anyone added or rescheduled after that batch ran, and a clear escalation path for whatever the batch flags, so an exception doesn't sit unresolved until the patient is already in the waiting room. It also means clear ownership: who reviews the exception report each morning, who makes the phone call when something fails, and who has the authority to reschedule or collect a deposit when coverage can't be confirmed in time.

Automation earns its keep on the repetitive, rules-based part of this — running the batch, flagging inactive or mismatched responses, routing exceptions to the right queue. It does not replace the judgment calls: deciding whether a flagged exception is worth delaying a visit over, handling the phone call when a payer's system disagrees with what the patient believes about their own coverage, or catching the coverage-gap scenarios above that a clean 271 response won't surface on its own. A well-built escalation path routes an inactive-coverage flag to the front desk for a same-day phone call, routes a coordination-of-benefits mismatch to whoever owns COB follow-up, and routes anything involving a high-cost or authorization-dependent service straight to the person who can actually delay or reschedule the visit if coverage can't be confirmed in time — rather than every exception landing in one shared queue where the urgent ones wait behind the routine ones.

The operational cadence that works in practice, how to split the work between automated batch checking and staff judgment, and where practices most often under- or over-invest in one versus the other, are covered in our guide to building an eligibility verification workflow.

Do and don't

Do
  • Run batch eligibility against tomorrow's schedule every evening.
  • Verify benefits, not just active status, for any non-routine or high-cost service.
  • Ask every patient at every visit whether their coverage has changed.
  • Record the deductible position so the desk knows what to collect.
  • Keep the payer response on file; it is your evidence in an appeal.
  • Route the routine, rules-based checking through automation and save staff time for the exceptions.
Don't
  • Don't treat active coverage as confirmation that the service is payable.
  • Don't rely on the card the patient presents; cards outlive policies.
  • Don't skip re-verification for established patients.
  • Don't deliver a high-cost service on a verbal assurance of coverage.
  • Don't leave the deductible unknown at the point of collection.
  • Don't treat a CO-27 or CO-31 denial as an appeal opportunity before confirming it isn't simply correct.

Frequently asked questions

How far in advance should we verify eligibility?

Run a batch against the next day's schedule each evening, which is recent enough to catch terminations and early enough to act on them. For high-cost or authorisation-dependent services, verify benefits at the point of scheduling as well, since those decisions need to be made before the appointment exists rather than the night before it happens.

What is the difference between eligibility and benefits verification?

Eligibility confirms the policy is active for that patient on that date. Benefits confirm whether the specific service is covered, what the patient owes, and whether limits or authorisation requirements apply. Eligibility is fast and automatable; benefits often require a more detailed enquiry. Checking only eligibility is the most common reason a practice believes it verified coverage and still receives a denial.

Why do coordination of benefits denials keep happening?

Because coverage changes and nobody asks. A patient who gains a spouse's plan, ages onto Medicare, or has a child added to two policies now has a primary payer that differs from your record, and they will not mention it. The claim goes to the old primary and denies. Asking about changes at every registration, rather than only at the first, resolves most of it.

Can we bill the patient when eligibility fails?

Sometimes, and it depends on why. Where coverage was genuinely terminated and the patient signed a financial responsibility agreement, the balance is usually theirs. Where the service required authorisation that was never obtained, most contracts prohibit billing the patient because the failure was administrative. This distinction is exactly why authorisation must be confirmed before delivery rather than discovered afterwards.

Do you run eligibility as part of your service?

Yes. We run automated batch verification against your upcoming schedule, surface exceptions to your front desk each morning with the specific issue identified, and verify benefits for services where coverage is the deciding factor. Because eligibility problems are the largest avoidable denial category, this is usually where an engagement produces measurable results first.

What is the 270/271 transaction?

The 270 is the standardized electronic eligibility inquiry a provider or clearinghouse sends to a payer; the 271 is the payer's response. Both are ANSI X12 transaction sets required under HIPAA, which is why every certified payer, clearinghouse and practice management system can exchange them regardless of who built the software on either end. A 271 confirming active coverage means the transaction found the member and the policy is active on that date — it does not by itself confirm that a specific planned service is covered.

What do CARC codes CO-27 and CO-31 mean on a denial?

CO-27 means expenses were incurred after coverage had already terminated, which usually points to a policy end date that either wasn't caught by the eligibility check or wasn't checked at all. CO-31 means the payer cannot identify the patient as their insured, typically from a member ID, name or date-of-birth mismatch, or coverage that was never active under the plan presented. Both are eligibility-driven denials rather than coding denials, and both are almost always addressed through prevention — catching the problem before the visit — rather than through an appeal after the fact.
