RTM data that counts, and the FDA device question every program has to answer.

Two questions decide whether an RTM claim survives an audit before anyone reads a single note. Is the data the right kind, meaning musculoskeletal signs, symptoms and function tied to the treatment plan? And is the product that collects it the kind of product Medicare pays for, meaning one that meets the FDA definition of a medical device? This page takes both in turn.

Key takeaways

  • Medicare pays RTM for data from a product that meets the FDA definition of a medical device. That requirement came with the codes in 2022 and it is the first thing an auditor checks.
  • Not every app qualifies. FDA guidance lists food logs, general wellness tracking and simple tools that only record and share data as not devices.
  • Alerts to clinicians for active monitoring sit at the regulated end. They can require FDA clearance, so do not add them without a regulatory review.
  • Musculoskeletal data counts when it is tied to a documented diagnosis and plan: pain intensity, pain interference, function and adherence. Food-only days do not count toward the days of data.
  • Wording does not solve the device question. A good pain scale in a product that is not a device still fails the first test.

Which data counts for musculoskeletal RTM

CPT defines RTM data as signs, symptoms and functions of a therapeutic response, which may include subjective inputs reported by the patient. Every item has to relate to a documented musculoskeletal condition, usually an ICD-10 chapter M code as a recommended standard rather than a Medicare rule, and to the patient’s treatment plan.

Wording the pain questions

No CMS or CPT rule sets the wording. The 2026 RTM codes describe monitoring of therapy adherence and response, with device supply to support monitoring of the musculoskeletal system. A single generic question is weak support. Stronger options:

  • The PEG, three items covering pain intensity, interference with enjoyment of life and interference with general activity, derived from the Brief Pain Inventory and validated by Krebs and colleagues in two samples that included primary care patients with chronic pain. Ask it weekly.
  • One daily musculoskeletal pain interference item naming the site, which is what supports the day count.
  • The Brief Pain Inventory for the body map and interference items.
  • PROMIS Pain Interference and Physical Function short forms.
  • One or two items on the musculoskeletal treatment, for example a function measure or home exercise completed.

An interference question that names the musculoskeletal site is enough when the patient has a musculoskeletal diagnosis and plan. List the musculoskeletal diagnosis first on the claim as a best practice. For patients whose condition is not musculoskeletal, such as chronic pain syndrome, complex regional pain syndrome, neuralgia or migraine, the musculoskeletal device codes need a musculoskeletal diagnosis behind them, so use the underlying musculoskeletal condition when there is one and treat the others as secondary diagnoses.

The device question

Medicare adopted the requirement in the CY2022 Physician Fee Schedule: payment for RTM requires a product that meets the FDA definition of a medical device. The FDA’s guidance on mobile medical software sorts software functions into groups, and where a given app sits decides whether RTM billing is supportable. The guidance, last issued September 28, 2022, has three appendices that matter here.

How FDA guidance sorts software functions, with examples relevant to RTM.
GroupWhat the guidance saysExamples that matter for RTM
Appendix A: not medical devicesSoftware functions the FDA does not consider devicesGeneral wellness and dietary logging, including food diaries and calorie counters; tools that let people record and log measurements such as weight and share them with a clinician; simple tools that let patients with specific conditions record events or measurements and share them with their clinician as part of a disease-management plan
Appendix B: enforcement discretionFunctions that may meet the device definition, where the FDA does not currently enforce device requirementsMedication reminders to improve adherence, software that motivates patients to do prescribed home physical therapy exercises, and trending of vital signs or blood pressure data
Appendix C: focus of oversightFunctions the FDA actively regulatesSoftware used in active patient monitoring or to analyze patient-specific medical device data, which includes software that generates alerts to clinicians for active monitoring

The consequence for billing is a gradient. A plain diary that only records and shares data sits in the non-device group, and the guidance specifically calls out dietary logs. Software that prompts adherence to a prescribed treatment or trends the patient’s data sits in the enforcement discretion group, which is where most patient-facing RTM features land. Software that generates alerts for clinicians as part of active monitoring sits in the regulated group and can require FDA clearance. Where a product sits depends on what it actually does and what its maker says it is for.

The FDA also reissued its general wellness policy on January 6, 2026. Wellness products can show values, ranges, trends and summaries if they are solely for wellness and carry no clinical prompts. Products that measure or report physiologic values to monitor, screen, alert or manage a disease, or that use diagnostic thresholds or alerts that recommend clinical action, are outside the policy.

What this means for a billing practice. Medicare requires a product that meets the FDA device definition, and the FDA lists plain diaries and food logs as not devices. A product that is only a diary does not support RTM billing, however good the questions are. Make the vendor state the product’s intended use in writing and get a written regulatory opinion before billing.

What to ask the vendor, and what to keep

  • The intended use statement: monitoring musculoskeletal therapy adherence and response in patients with a diagnosed condition.
  • Whether the product is FDA cleared, authorized or registered, with the number, or the basis for the claim that it is a device without clearance.
  • Which features are on: adherence prompts, trending, and above all any alert sent to a clinician for active monitoring.
  • A written opinion from regulatory counsel that the feature set meets the device definition Medicare requires, and an updated opinion before any new clinician alert feature is switched on.
  • The privacy and security terms, including a signed business associate agreement.

Physicians may bill RTM outside a therapy plan of care when appropriate, but devices made specifically for therapy services should be under a therapy plan of care. Keep the intended use statement and the regulatory opinion with the program records, because they are the first documents an auditor will ask for.

Pro tip

If the vendor cannot give you a written intended use statement and a regulatory position on the product, stop before enrolling a patient. Every RTM claim you bill on that product carries the same risk, and you will not know the size of it until a payer asks.

Putting it into the order

At enrollment, the practitioner writes an RTM order that names the condition, the data collected and how it will guide treatment. List the musculoskeletal diagnosis first. Consent is separate from the CCM or PCM consent. The care plan names the measures (the PEG, a function measure, the daily interference item) so that each billed month has data that traces back to a goal. The monthly nurse review compares that data to the practitioner-set thresholds and records the days of data, which is what picks the device code.

Frequently asked questions

Does an RTM app have to be FDA cleared?

Medicare requires a product that meets the FDA definition of a medical device. That is not the same as FDA clearance: the FDA also lists categories of software where it uses enforcement discretion, meaning it does not currently enforce device requirements. What matters is that the product meets the definition, which is why a written regulatory opinion is the evidence to keep. Features that alert clinicians for active monitoring are the regulated end and may need clearance.

Can a food or symptom diary support RTM billing?

No. FDA guidance lists food logs and simple tools that only record and share data as not devices, and the RTM device codes only exist for respiratory, musculoskeletal and cognitive behavioral therapy monitoring. Food-only days do not count toward the days of data.

Which pain question should we use?

Neither CMS nor CPT requires specific wording. The PEG is a good weekly measure, and a daily item that names the musculoskeletal site and asks about pain interference supports the day count. The Brief Pain Inventory and PROMIS short forms are well established alternatives.

Can sleep or step data count?

Only when tied to a musculoskeletal goal. Pain interference with sleep counts. General sleep tracking does not. Steps count when they are part of a prescribed activity goal in the plan; plain step tracking is a wellness feature.

Does RTM require a musculoskeletal diagnosis?

The musculoskeletal device codes need data that relates to a musculoskeletal condition and treatment plan. A musculoskeletal ICD-10 code listed first is a recommended standard that makes the link obvious, not a Medicare rule.

Who should check whether our app is a device?

Regulatory counsel, with the vendor’s intended use statement and feature list in front of them. A billing team cannot settle it from the marketing page.

Confirm before you rely on this. This page is billing education, not regulatory or legal advice. Whether a particular product meets the FDA definition of a medical device is a regulatory question that depends on its intended use and features, and the right way to settle it is a written opinion from regulatory counsel. Verify current requirements with the FDA, your Medicare Administrative Contractor and your attorney.
