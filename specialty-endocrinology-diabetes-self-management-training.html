Diabetes self-management training billing: G0108, G0109, and where MNT fits.

DSMT is one of the few Medicare preventive benefits with a hard-coded hour budget, a benefit-specific referral rule, and a program-level accreditation requirement stacked on top of the usual coding rules, and a claim can be clean on CPT/HCPCS grounds and still deny because one of those three failed. This guide covers exactly how the 10 initial hours split between G0108 and G0109, who is allowed to write the referral and what it has to say, the annual follow-up allotment, and how Medical Nutrition Therapy layers on top without colliding with DSMT on the same date of service.

Key takeaways

  • The initial DSMT benefit is 10 hours, and only 1 of them is payable as individual training. The other 9 must run as group sessions (G0109) unless the referral documents one of three specific exceptions, and the whole benefit is once-in-a-lifetime, not annual.
  • The program has to be accredited, not just the biller. DSMT is only payable when furnished by a program that holds ADCES accreditation or ADA recognition, a requirement that sits on top of, not instead of, a valid physician or QHP referral.
  • MNT's referral rule is narrower than DSMT's. A nurse practitioner or physician assistant can refer a patient for DSMT, but Medicare requires a physician specifically to refer for MNT, the same NP or PA order that gets DSMT paid does not satisfy the MNT referral requirement.
  • DSMT and MNT can both run in the same benefit year, just never the same day. A newly diagnosed patient can use up to 10 DSMT hours plus 3 MNT hours, 13 hours total, but the two services can't be billed for the same date of service.

G0108 and G0109: the codes and the hour math

DSMT bills under two HCPCS Level II codes, both denominated in 30-minute units, and the code you choose depends entirely on who's in the room, not on the content being taught.

HCPCS codes for Medicare diabetes self-management training.
CodeDescriptorUnit
G0108Diabetes outpatient self-management training services, individual, per session30 minutes
G0109Diabetes outpatient self-management training services, group session (2 or more)30 minutes

The initial DSMT benefit is 10 hours, delivered within a continuous 12-month period that starts with the first training session, that window doesn't have to align with the calendar year, so a patient who starts in September has until the following September to use the full allotment, not until December 31. Of those 10 hours, Medicare's default split is one hour billable as individual training (two units of G0108) and the remaining nine hours as group training (18 units of G0109). That default isn't a suggestion; billing more than one hour of G0108 without documentation supporting an exception is exactly the kind of thing a MAC's post-payment review is built to catch.

Three conditions let the referring provider order some or all of the remaining nine hours as individual instead of group:

  • 1No group session is available. No group class exists within two months of the date the training was ordered.
  • 2The beneficiary has a documented barrier to group learning. Vision loss, hearing loss, a cognitive impairment, a language barrier, or a mobility limitation that the referring provider notes as interfering with group instruction.
  • 3The patient needs additional insulin training. The referring provider indicates the patient requires additional one-on-one instruction on insulin use that a group format can't accommodate.

None of these are self-certifying by the DSMT program, the referral itself has to name the condition. A program that decides on its own that a patient "seems like a better fit for individual sessions" and bills G0108 for hours 2 through 10 without that reasoning on the order is billing outside the benefit, regardless of how clinically reasonable the judgment was.

⚠️ Sources describe the initial 10-hour benefit as "once-in-a-lifetime" per beneficiary, distinct from the annual follow-up allotment described below. Unlike MNT, which has a published second-referral mechanism (HCPCS G0270/G0271) for a significant change in diagnosis or treatment regimen, this build could not independently confirm through a primary CMS source whether an equivalent path exists to reopen the initial DSMT benefit after a major change in a patient's diabetes management (for example, a new insulin start years after the initial 10 hours were used). Treat the annual 2-hour follow-up as the only confirmed mechanism for additional DSMT hours after year one, and confirm with the patient's MAC before billing anything beyond it.

The referral DSMT can't be billed without, and the accreditation rule stacked on top of it

DSMT has two gating requirements that sit above ordinary coding rules: a specific referral, and a specific kind of program. Miss either one and the hours themselves being medically reasonable doesn't save the claim.

Who can refer. The referral has to come from the physician or a qualified nonphysician practitioner, a nurse practitioner, physician assistant, or clinical nurse specialist, who is actively managing the beneficiary's diabetes. It can't come from a provider who isn't involved in that patient's diabetes care just because they happen to be available to sign an order.

What the referral has to specify. A DSMT referral that will survive review documents all of the following:

  • 1The beneficiary's diagnosis, with a valid, current ICD-10-CM code (for example E11.9, type 2 diabetes mellitus without complications, or a more specific complication code where one applies), not a narrative diagnosis alone.
  • 2That DSMT is needed, and the number of hours being ordered.
  • 3Whether the training is to be delivered as group or individual, and if individual, which of the three exception conditions applies.
  • 4The date of the order, the referring provider's signature (or valid e-signature), and their NPI.

The referring provider is also expected to maintain a plan of diabetes care in the patient's own medical record, separate from the referral itself, and the DSMT program is expected to retain the original referral, along with any documented change to it, in its own records. A verbal order that never gets reduced to a signed, dated referral is the single most common gap that shows up when a payer asks for records on a DSMT claim.

The program itself has to be accredited. This is easy to overlook because it isn't about the biller's credentials at all, it's about the DSMT program's status. Medicare only pays for DSMT furnished by a program that holds accreditation from the Association of Diabetes Care & Education Specialists (ADCES) or recognition from the American Diabetes Association (ADA). Both statuses have to be renewed periodically; a program billing on an accreditation that lapsed mid-year is billing for a service it's no longer qualified to furnish, independent of whether any individual session was clinically appropriate. Confirm current accreditation status before the first claim goes out, not after a payer asks for it.

Follow-up training: the annual allotment after year one

Once the initial 10-hour benefit is exhausted, DSMT doesn't end, it converts to a smaller annual allotment.

DSMT hour allotments: initial benefit versus annual follow-up.
Benefit periodTotal hoursIndividualGroup
Initial (once-in-a-lifetime, 12-month window from first session)10 hours1 hour (G0108) by default9 hours (G0109) by default
Follow-up (each subsequent calendar year)2 hoursBillable as G0108 or G0109Billable as G0108 or G0109

Follow-up hours run on a calendar-year basis, not a 12-month rolling window like the initial benefit, and they can be delivered as either individual or group depending on how the session is actually furnished, the strict 1-hour-individual cap that applies to the initial benefit doesn't carry over to follow-up training. Follow-up hours are also use-it-or-lose-it: unused hours from one calendar year don't roll into the next, and there's no mechanism to bank them. A patient who uses none of their 2 follow-up hours in 2026 doesn't get 4 hours available in 2027.

The referral requirement doesn't relax for follow-up training. Each year's follow-up hours need their own documented medical necessity tying back to the patient's diabetes management, even if it's a brief order rather than a full new referral packet, a DSMT program billing follow-up hours purely on the strength of the original initial-benefit referral, with nothing dated to the current calendar year, is exposed the same way an initial claim without a referral is.

DSMT and MNT together: two benefits, two referral rules, never the same day

Medical Nutrition Therapy is a separate Medicare Part B benefit that frequently travels alongside DSMT for the same diabetic patient, but it runs on its own codes, its own hour allotment, and, critically, its own referral rule that's narrower than DSMT's.

MNT codes relevant to diabetes and chronic kidney disease.
CodeDescriptorUnit
97802Medical nutrition therapy; initial assessment and intervention, individual, face-to-face with the patient15 minutes
97803Medical nutrition therapy; re-assessment and intervention, individual, face-to-face with the patient15 minutes
97804Medical nutrition therapy; group (2 or more individuals)30 minutes
G0270MNT reassessment and subsequent intervention(s) following a second referral in the same year for a change in diagnosis, medical condition, or treatment regimen, individual15 minutes
G0271Same as G0270, furnished as a group session30 minutes

MNT's hour allotment is smaller than DSMT's: 3 hours in the first year a beneficiary uses the benefit, and 2 hours in each subsequent calendar year, and unlike DSMT's fixed initial split, there's no mandated individual-versus-group ratio inside those hours. A physician can order additional hours mid-year using G0270 or G0271 if a documented change in diagnosis, medical condition, or treatment regimen creates new medical necessity, which gives MNT an explicit escalation path that DSMT's initial benefit doesn't have.

MNT is also diagnosis-restricted in a way DSMT isn't. Medicare covers MNT only for beneficiaries with diabetes, chronic kidney disease (non-dialysis), or a kidney transplant within the preceding 36 months, a diagnosis outside that list doesn't qualify for the Part B MNT benefit no matter how clinically appropriate nutrition counseling would be.

The referral gap is the trap worth building a workflow around. DSMT accepts an order from a physician or a qualified nonphysician practitioner. MNT does not extend that same latitude, Medicare requires a physician specifically to refer for MNT. A clinic that routes both referrals through its nurse practitioners because that's who orders DSMT will find every MNT claim vulnerable to a referral-requirement denial, because the NP's signature that's sufficient for G0108/G0109 doesn't satisfy the referral requirement behind 97802/97803/97804.

Both benefits can be used by the same patient in the same calendar year, a diabetes diagnosis alone doesn't force a choice between them. The one absolute rule is timing: DSMT and MNT cannot be billed for the same beneficiary on the same date of service, even when both are medically necessary in the same period of care. Scheduling has to put them on different calendar days; a same-day DSMT group session and MNT initial assessment is a denial waiting to happen no matter how well-documented each one is on its own.

Worked example: a newly diagnosed patient's first-year hour allocation

A 58-year-old patient is diagnosed with type 2 diabetes (E11.9) at a primary care visit. The physician documents the diagnosis, refers the patient to an ADCES-accredited DSMT program for the full 10-hour initial benefit as group training (no exception condition applies), and separately writes a physician-signed referral to a registered dietitian for MNT, citing the diabetes diagnosis. Both referrals are dated the same week but the sessions themselves are scheduled on different days throughout the following two months.

First-year hour and code breakdown for a newly diagnosed diabetic patient using both benefits in full.
ServiceCodeUnits billedHoursReferral on file
DSMT, individualG01082 units (30 min each)1 hourPhysician/QHP order naming DSMT, diagnosis, hours, individual/group split
DSMT, groupG010918 units (30 min each)9 hoursSame order as above
MNT, initial assessment978024 units (15 min each)1 hourPhysician-only referral naming diabetes diagnosis
MNT, reassessment978038 units (15 min each)2 hoursSame referral as above

That's 13 total hours across two benefits in the patient's first year, 10 DSMT hours plus 3 MNT hours, billed under two separate referrals, from two different types of qualified referring providers (a physician or QHP for DSMT, a physician only for MNT), on no fewer than however many distinct calendar days the sessions actually took to run. From year two forward, the same patient is eligible for up to 2 DSMT follow-up hours and up to 2 MNT follow-up hours annually, each needing its own current-year medical necessity, not a carryover of the original referral.

Keep the claim clean
  • Confirm the DSMT program's ADCES/ADA status and the referral's required elements, diagnosis, hours, individual/group, provider signature and NPI, before the first session is scheduled, not after a payer requests records.
  • Route every MNT referral through a physician specifically, even in a practice where NPs and PAs handle most diabetes management and DSMT referrals.
  • Track the initial 12-month DSMT window and the calendar-year MNT/follow-up windows separately, they don't reset on the same schedule.
Common denial triggers
  • Billing more than 1 hour of G0108 in the initial benefit without one of the three documented exceptions on the referral.
  • Billing DSMT or MNT past the annual hour allotment, or without a current-year order supporting the follow-up hours claimed.
  • Scheduling a DSMT session and an MNT session for the same beneficiary on the same date of service.
  • Billing through a DSMT program with lapsed ADCES accreditation or ADA recognition.

Frequently asked questions

How many hours of DSMT does Medicare cover, and how are they split between individual and group?

Medicare covers 10 hours of initial DSMT within a continuous 12-month period starting from the first training session. Of those 10 hours, only 1 hour is payable as individual training (G0108); the remaining 9 hours must be furnished as group sessions (G0109) unless one of three documented exceptions applies. After the initial benefit is used, beneficiaries qualify for up to 2 hours of follow-up training in each subsequent calendar year, billable as either individual or group depending on how it's delivered.

Can a patient get all 10 initial DSMT hours as individual training instead of group?

Only if the referring physician or qualified nonphysician practitioner documents one of three conditions: no group class is available within two months of the referral date, the beneficiary has a documented barrier to group learning such as vision loss, hearing loss, a cognitive impairment, a language barrier, or a mobility limitation, or the referring provider indicates the patient needs additional one-on-one insulin-training instruction. Without one of those three reasons on the referral, only the first hour is payable as individual training and the rest must run as group.

Who can refer a patient for DSMT, and can the same provider refer for MNT?

DSMT accepts a referral from the physician or a qualified nonphysician practitioner, a nurse practitioner, physician assistant, or clinical nurse specialist, who is managing the beneficiary's diabetes. Medical Nutrition Therapy is narrower: only a physician can refer for MNT. An NP or PA can order DSMT, but that same order does not satisfy the referral requirement for MNT, which needs its own physician-signed referral naming diabetes, chronic kidney disease, or a kidney transplant within the last 36 months as the qualifying diagnosis.

Can a Medicare patient receive both DSMT and MNT in the same year?

Yes. DSMT and MNT are separate benefits with separate hour allotments, up to 10 hours of DSMT and 3 hours of MNT in the first year, for up to 13 combined hours, and a beneficiary can use both in the same calendar year if each has its own qualifying referral. The one hard rule is that DSMT and MNT cannot be billed for the same beneficiary on the same date of service; the two need to be scheduled on different days even when both are medically necessary in the same period.
