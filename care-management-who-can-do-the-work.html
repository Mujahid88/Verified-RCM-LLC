Our complete care management guide

Who can do the work: Medicare’s clinical staff rule, then the state law that narrows it.

Medicare decides which minutes count. State law decides who is allowed to spend them. A task can satisfy Medicare’s definition of clinical staff work and still be outside what the person doing it may lawfully do, in which case the claim has a problem. This page starts with the federal rule and then uses Arizona and Texas as worked examples of how differently two states can treat the same role.

Key takeaways

  • Only clinical staff time counts. That means people allowed by law, regulation and facility policy to help furnish the service, working under the billing practitioner’s general supervision.
  • Staff may be employees or contractors today. The 2027 proposal would require RTM staff to be the billing practice’s direct employees. It is a proposal, not a rule.
  • Staff and patients must be in the United States, and staff cannot be excluded from federal health programs.
  • State law narrows who may do what. Interpreting clinical data and changing a treatment plan stay with licensed staff in Arizona and in Texas.
  • Our recommended staffing: a registered dietitian or nurse for care management calls, a licensed nurse for RTM data review and the live call, a medical assistant or coordinator for set-up and support, and only the practitioner for medical changes.

The federal rule: clinical staff under general supervision

  • Clinical staff means people who are allowed by law, regulation and facility policy to assist with the service. Time by non-clinical staff does not count toward the codes.
  • General supervision means the work is done under the practitioner’s overall direction and control. The practitioner does not need to be on site. Medicare’s incident-to rule says designated care management services can be furnished under general supervision, and CMS guidance on remote monitoring says auxiliary personnel may provide it under general supervision.
  • Employees or contractors. The incident-to rule defines auxiliary personnel as people under the practitioner’s supervision who are employees, leased employees or independent contractors, who are not excluded from federal health programs, and who meet state requirements to provide the service.
  • One biller. Only the practitioner who supervises the staff may bill incident-to. A referring or ordering doctor who does not supervise cannot.
  • Location. Staff and patients must be in the United States for the time to count.
  • State law applies. Services must be furnished in accordance with applicable state law, which is what ties the state scope-of-practice rules below to a Medicare claim.

Dietitians cannot bill these codes under their own NPI. They work as clinical staff under the billing practitioner. The 2027 proposal would also change contracted RTM staff: if finalized, payment would require clinical staff the billing practice employs, which would end RTM work by outside teams under Medicare.

Role by role

Registered dietitians

In its CCM guidance CMS names registered dietitians among the people who cannot bill the service themselves but can take part as clinical staff under general supervision when incident-to rules are met, within their scope and state law. We found no PCM-specific statement, and PCM follows the same incident-to rules. What the dietitian does has to be care management tied to the care plan: setting and reviewing goals that support the condition, education, checking adherence, solving barriers and coordinating with the practice. Writing detailed menus or recipes, or counseling on a condition that is not in the care plan, looks like medical nutrition therapy. That is a separate Medicare benefit limited to diabetes and kidney disease, billed by the dietitian under its own rules, and the regulation says it cannot be furnished incident to a practitioner’s services. Log the care management purpose, not the diet content.

On licensure, Texas licenses dietitians and protects the titles Licensed Dietitian and LD, and separately limits the title Registered Dietitian to people registered by the Commission on Dietetic Registration. As far as we could determine, Arizona does not license dietitians; licensure bills have been introduced in recent sessions, so confirm the current position. Whichever state applies, a dietitian may not diagnose, change medications or triage pain.

Nurses

Both Arizona and Texas are full members of the Nurse Licensure Compact, so a nurse with a valid multistate license can serve patients in either. Registered nursing in both states includes assessing health status and evaluating responses to care. An RN can review RTM data, make the live call, develop the nursing care plan and coordinate care. A licensed practical or vocational nurse contributes to the assessment and carries out care under RN or physician supervision. Medical treatment changes need the practitioner. Confirm the multistate status on the live compact map before relying on it.

Medical assistants

Arizona details that matter for remote care management. Arizona lists tasks a medical assistant may do without direct supervision, including taking vitals and history, billing and coding, scheduling, insurance verification, obtaining and communicating prior authorizations as documented and ordered, and communicating documented medical advice, documented interpretations of test results and documented orders. The same statute’s definition says a medical assistant does not diagnose or interpret, design or modify established treatment programs. Arizona’s definition of direct supervision means the practitioner is within the same room or office suite, and the texts we read do not address remote supervision, so check with counsel before relying on remote work for tasks that require direct supervision. Remote untrained staff should not use the title medical assistant; call them care coordinators or health coaches.

In Arizona osteopathic practices the rules are different and silent on remote work: the osteopathic medical assistant rules cover procedures under direct supervision and offer no physician-designed training path. The safest course in a DO practice is to have nurses make the patient calls, or to have an MD, PA or NP be the billing practitioner.

Texas details. A physician may delegate to a qualified and properly trained person any medical act that a reasonable and prudent physician would find within the scope of sound medical judgment to delegate, and the physician remains responsible. In the delegating physician’s opinion the act has to be one the delegate can properly and safely perform in its customary manner. If an NP or PA is the billing practitioner, have the collaborating physician sign the protocols. In our view interpreting clinical data and changing treatment are not tasks a reasonable and prudent physician would delegate to an unlicensed coach.

Coaches and coordinators

Neither state has a health coach licensing law. What coaches can do in both: education from approved materials, encouraging the patient to follow the plan, collecting self-reported data, scheduling, and escalating to a nurse or practitioner by protocol. They may not assess, triage, evaluate response to treatment or change the plan, and purely administrative minutes do not count toward the codes. The 2027 proposal includes health coaching codes 0591T to 0593T for certified coaches under direct supervision, which could become a billable path later.

For RTM management specifically, we recommend a licensed nurse for the data review and the live call. RTM management means reviewing data, changing the care plan and acting when the patient is not improving, and state law reserves that clinical judgment for licensed staff.

Delegation protocols

A delegation protocol is a written document, signed by the physician, that says which tasks staff may do, how, under what supervision, and what to do in an emergency. Texas rules set out its elements for standing orders and protocols: development and approval by the delegating physician, a written and signed document, the specific instructions, a notation of the level of supervision, plans for patient emergencies, an annual review signed by the physician, and a copy kept at the practice site. Texas rewrote its delegation rules in January 2025 and the specific subchapters override the general standards, so confirm what applies to the delegate and the act. We found nothing in the Texas chapter that addresses care management staff by name. Arizona does not require a protocol, but it is the practical way to show that staff pass on only documented advice and orders.

A model protocol outline

  • Title, practice, location, effective date and version.
  • Delegating practitioner: name, license number, signature and date, and alternates.
  • Delegates by name or role (RN, LVN or LPN, dietitian, medical assistant, coordinator, coach), the training required and the practitioner’s statement that they are qualified and properly trained; the vendor name if an outside team; US location; exclusion check.
  • Patients covered: CCM, PCM and RTM enrollees, with inclusion and exclusion criteria.
  • Tasks by role: scripted check-ins; symptom and questionnaire collection (PEG, PHQ-2); education from the approved library; RTM data review against practitioner-set thresholds; medication adherence reminders with no dose changes; care plan updates limited to proposals for practitioner approval; referral coordination; time logging.
  • Prohibited acts: diagnose, interpret beyond thresholds, change medications, change the treatment plan, give new medical advice, triage beyond the script.
  • Supervision: general supervision, the named supervising practitioner, how to reach them and the chart review schedule.
  • Emergencies: the red-flag list and 911 script, transfer to the practice, after-hours number and documentation within 24 hours.
  • Escalation thresholds and timeframes; documentation fields; quality checks (a monthly chart audit sample and a yearly competency check).
  • The annual review signature block, a statement that the original is kept at the practice site, and signature lines for the practitioner and each delegate.

Supervision evidence the practitioner should keep

CMS has said that when a practice provides little oversight and has no clinical integration with a third party, the service is not being furnished by the practice. The practitioner has to remain involved in ongoing oversight, management, collaboration and reassessment. Keep:

  • The care plan reviewed and approved by the practitioner, with any attached protocol (signature or EHR attestation recommended; Medicare does not require it for CCM).
  • Written protocols signed by the practitioner, reviewed yearly and kept at the practice where state law requires them.
  • Practitioner approval of every care plan change.
  • A short monthly practitioner review note per patient (recommended practice).
  • An escalation log that shows the practitioner was reachable and responded.
  • A staff roster: credentials, licence or compact status, US location and the date of the last exclusion check.
  • A practitioner-signed record of staff training.
  • Documentation that happens inside the practice’s own EHR.

If a practice has several practitioners billing under one group NPI, the rendering practitioner on the claim has to be the one who did the enrollment visit, owns the care plan and supervises that patient’s care management. One practitioner billing for every patient is allowed only if that practitioner actually does the initiating visit, consent and oversight for each of them, and the OIG has found CCM overpayments where more than one practitioner billed the same patient in the same month.

Frequently asked questions

Can a contracted team provide the staff time?

Today, yes: Medicare’s incident-to rule allows employees, leased employees and independent contractors as auxiliary personnel under the billing practitioner’s supervision. The 2027 proposed rule would require RTM and RPM clinical staff to be direct employees of the billing practice. That is a proposal, so plan for it without acting as if it is final.

Can a health coach do RTM management?

We recommend not. RTM management is reviewing data, changing the care plan and acting when the patient is not improving, and both Arizona and Texas reserve that judgment for licensed staff or the practitioner. Coaches can educate, encourage, collect self-reported data, schedule and escalate by protocol.

Do we need a delegation protocol in Arizona?

Arizona does not require one by statute or rule. A protocol with an approved advice and education library is still the practical way to prove that staff pass on only documented advice and orders.

Does an out-of-state nurse or dietitian need a local license?

It depends. A nurse with a valid multistate compact license can serve patients in compact states, including Arizona and Texas. A dietitian does not need a license where the state does not license the profession, but should not use a protected title such as Licensed Dietitian in Texas. Out-of-state clinicians may also face telehealth registration rules. Check each state with counsel.

Whose NPI goes on the claim?

The practitioner who supervised the staff and, where required, held the initiating visit. The group NPI is the billing provider and the rendering practitioner is the one who owns the care plan for that patient. A referring doctor who does not supervise cannot bill.

Do staff have to be employees of the practice for CCM and PCM?

Not today. They may be employees or contractors under general supervision. The 2027 proposal asks questions about supervision of contracted care management staff and could lead to limits in the future.

Confirm before you rely on this. This page summarizes what federal and state texts say; it is not legal advice. Texas statute text was checked against a mirror of the official source and should be re-checked on the official site, and state rules change. Have a healthcare attorney confirm scope-of-practice and delegation questions for your state before you assign work.
