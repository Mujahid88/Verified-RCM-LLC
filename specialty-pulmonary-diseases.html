Pulmonary Medicine Billing and Coding: The Complete 2026 Guide

Pulmonology denies at roughly 18% industry-wide — against a clean-claim target of 95% or higher — and prior authorization alone accounts for close to 30% of that volume, driven by CPAP, oxygen, and biologic asthma therapy. This guide covers the full revenue cycle: real NCCI indicator values instead of a generic "check your edits," MAC-specific sleep study and spirometry articles named by document number, ICD-10 specificity that actually drives coverage (COPD-asthma overlap, asthma severity-plus-exacerbation, sleep apnea subtypes), and denial-to-appeal pairing keyed to the exact reason a claim came back.

Reviewed by Phares A., MD · Physiotherapist, CPC (AAPC) · Updated August 2026

Key takeaways

  • 31622 is column-2 to 31623 and 31625 with a modifier indicator of 0. No modifier bypasses it — diagnostic bronchoscopy is inherent to a biopsy performed the same session, full stop. Competitor guides name the edit category and stop there; the indicator value is what actually decides whether an appeal is worth writing.
  • Five different MACs publish five separately dated polysomnography articles — CGS A57049, Noridian A57697, WPS A56903, First Coast A57496, and Palmetto A56995/L36593 — and "check your LCD" is meaningless without naming which one governs your practice location.
  • Medicare requires HCPCS G0398–G0400 for home sleep testing, never the CPT sleep-study codes. Submitting 95800, 95801, or 95806 to Medicare denies automatically regardless of clinical accuracy.
  • A CPAP compliance denial and a PA device-mismatch denial need entirely different appeals. One needs a 30-day, 70%-of-nights, 4-hour-per-night download; the other needs a corrected authorization, not a clinical argument. Conflating them wastes the appeal window on both.

Pulmonary medicine billing at a glance

Pulmonology stacks four structural problems that most other specialties face separately: a diagnostic-testing code family with no single "complete study" code, so every PFT component has to be reported and interpreted on its own; a sleep-testing world split between commercial CPT codes and Medicare-mandated HCPCS G-codes that aren't interchangeable; dense NCCI bundling around bronchoscopy where the modifier indicator — not just the existence of an edit — decides whether an appeal is possible; and prior authorization that gates nearly every high-cost service in the specialty, from CPAP to biologic asthma therapy. The pulmonology denial rate runs around 18% by industry reporting, well above the 5–10% median most specialties see, and prior authorization denials alone account for close to 30% of that volume — both figures are secondary-sourced (Medscape Physician Compensation Report data as aggregated by industry billing analysts), not a CMS-published statistic, so treat them as directional rather than a number to cite in a payer negotiation.

E/M coding and modifier 25 in the pulmonology visit

Established-patient office visits (99212–99215) are leveled on either total time or medical decision making under the 2021+ E/M guidelines — fully satisfying either pathway supports the code, and the two are never combined. In pulmonology, data complexity is where MDM leveling most often gets under-documented: ordering and independently reviewing a spirometry tracing, a chest CT, or an outside sleep study report each count toward the data-complexity element, but only when the note shows the physician actually reviewed and interpreted the data, not just referenced that it exists. A 99214 typically needs 30–39 minutes of total time (face-to-face plus same-day chart review, ordering, and documentation) or moderate MDM; 99215 needs 40–54 minutes or high MDM. Titrating a patient's home oxygen prescription, escalating a biologic asthma regimen, or managing an acute COPD exacerbation with a same-day medication change routinely supports 99215-level MDM even when the visit itself runs short — the note has to capture the risk and complexity that was actually present, not just the time spent.

Prolonged services add-on code 99417 (or 99418 in a facility setting) applies once total time on a 99215-level visit exceeds the code's time threshold by 15 minutes or more, billed in 15-minute increments beyond that threshold. It requires the base E/M to already be leveled on time, not MDM, and the prolonged time has to be face-to-face or non-face-to-face time on the same date, clearly separated from any other billed service.

Modifier 25 is the recurring flashpoint whenever an E/M is billed the same day as spirometry or another PFT component. The documentation test is whether the E/M represents a significant, separately identifiable service beyond the work inherent to ordering and overseeing the diagnostic test itself — a physician reviewing a new patient's overall respiratory complaint, examining the patient, and making a broader treatment decision (adjusting inhaler therapy, ordering imaging, discussing smoking cessation) clears that bar; a visit whose entire content is "patient came in for spirometry, physician reviewed the result" generally does not, because interpreting the study is already compensated through the PFT code itself. Payers that scrutinize modifier 25 heavily in pulmonology are looking specifically for a chief complaint, exam, and plan that stand on their own apart from the test order. The full time-vs-MDM decision tree and worked leveling examples for the specialty, plus the parallel rules for modifier 26/TC, 59, and 52, are covered in the dedicated modifiers section below.

Pulmonary function testing: spirometry, lung volumes, DLCO

There is no single "complete PFT" CPT code. Each component of a pulmonary function evaluation is billed separately, based on what was actually performed and interpreted, and the family breaks down as follows.

Full code-by-code detail, including which combinations are typically ordered together clinically and the documentation each interpretation needs, is in our PFT billing guide covering 94010, 94060, and the lung volume codes.

Technical/professional split. Like most diagnostic testing, PFT codes carry a technical component (equipment, supplies, technologist time) and a professional component (physician interpretation and report), split with modifiers TC and 26 respectively. Bill the code without a modifier only when the same entity owns the equipment, employs the technologist, and performs the interpretation. A hospital-based pulmonologist reading a PFT tracing acquired on hospital equipment bills 26 only; the hospital bills TC for the technical side. Before defaulting to a component split, check the code's PC/TC indicator in the Medicare Physician Fee Schedule — not every PFT code carries a split (some are professional-component-only or technical-only by definition), and billing 26 on a code that doesn't support a component split denies outright.

The interpretation-and-report requirement is the single most common PFT denial reason. A tracing without a signed physician interpretation addressing the pattern (obstructive, restrictive, mixed), severity grading, and comparison to any prior study on file is billed but not supportable — auditors and payers alike treat the interpretation as the service being paid for, not the raw data acquisition. This shows up as a CO-16 (missing information) denial when caught at claim submission, or as a post-payment recoupment when it's caught later. Build the interpretation requirement into your chart-closing workflow so a PFT claim can't go out the door without a completed, signed report attached.

For remote and home-based spirometry monitoring specifically, CGS Administrators publishes a dedicated article — A56808, Billing and Coding: Transtelephonic Spirometry, effective 10/02/2025 — covering the documentation and frequency requirements for patients whose spirometry is performed outside the office and transmitted for interpretation. If your practice does this kind of monitoring in CGS's jurisdiction, that article, not the standard in-office PFT rules, governs the claim.

Submitting a CPT HST code (95800/95801/95806) to Medicare triggers an automatic denial regardless of clinical accuracy — Medicare simply doesn't recognize those codes for home sleep testing and requires the HCPCS G-code substitution instead. This is one of the most preventable denial patterns in the specialty because it's entirely a code-selection issue, not a coverage dispute: the fix is a payer-aware charge rule, not an appeal. In-lab polysomnography requires the study to be performed at a facility-based sleep lab meeting accreditation standards; it is not billable for a study run in a patient's home, regardless of which monitor or channel count was used. Bronchoscopy and pleural procedure coding

The bronchoscopy family (31622–31654) covers everything from a basic diagnostic exam through EBUS, navigation bronchoscopy, thermoplasty, stent placement, and endobronchial valve placement, and it carries the densest NCCI bundling of any pulmonology code family.

Core bronchoscopy family and what each code adds over the base diagnostic exam.
CodeService
31622Diagnostic bronchoscopy, with or without cell washing (base code)
31623Bronchoscopy with brushing or protected brushings
31624Bronchoscopy with bronchoalveolar lavage
31625Bronchoscopy with endobronchial biopsy, single or multiple sites
31628Bronchoscopy with transbronchial lung biopsy, single lobe
31629Bronchoscopy with transbronchial needle aspiration, trachea/bronchi
31652/31653Bronchoscopy with EBUS-guided transbronchial sampling, one or two mediastinal/hilar lymph node stations, versus three or more
31654Bronchoscopy with EBUS, add-on for the peripheral, non-lymph-node component

The core NCCI relationship: 31622 is column-2 to 31623 and 31625, with a modifier indicator of 0. That means when a biopsy or brushing is performed at the same session as the diagnostic exam that necessarily preceded it, the diagnostic bronchoscopy (31622) is not separately payable — and because the indicator is 0, no modifier, however well-documented, changes that outcome. This is the exact indicator value that generic "bronchoscopy bundles with biopsy" guidance never states, and it's the difference between telling a coder not to bother appealing versus letting staff burn time on an unwinnable claim. Separately, secondary industry-billing sources describe 31622 as also inclusive with 31628, 31629, 31630, and 31631 under the same non-bypassable logic when performed at the same session — consistent with the general principle that a diagnostic bronchoscopy is bundled into any more extensive bronchoscopic procedure performed the same encounter.

31625/31652/31653 carry their own set of interactions: EBUS-guided sampling of lymph node stations (31652/31653) is billed once per session based on the total number of stations sampled (one or two vs. three or more), not once per station, and billing both codes for the same encounter is a duplicate rather than two distinct services. A modifier 59 or the specific X-modifier (most often XS, separate structure) is only supportable when the operative note documents that a second, genuinely distinct procedure targeted a separate, non-contiguous anatomic site — a different lobe, a different lung, or a structure outside the lymph node stations already captured by the EBUS codes — and even then, only where the underlying pair's modifier indicator is 1, not 0. Full bundling logic across the entire 31622–31654 range, plus the modifier decision tree for each pair, is in our bronchoscopy coding and NCCI bundling guide covering 31622 through 31654.

Pleural procedures: 32554 versus 32555. 32554 reports thoracentesis without imaging guidance; 32555 reports it with imaging guidance, and the guidance work — performing and interpreting the ultrasound — is bundled into 32555's value. Reporting 76942 (ultrasound guidance) separately alongside 32555 is a direct NCCI Policy Manual violation (Chapter 9's general rule that radiologic guidance integral to another procedure isn't separately reportable) and denies. The less obvious trap runs the other direction: billing 32554 without imaging, when current standard of care for thoracentesis is image-guided placement, can itself draw a medical-necessity denial or a payer inquiry, because performing the procedure blind is increasingly treated as below the expected standard outside a genuine emergency where imaging isn't available. Document the clinical reason whenever 32554 is billed instead of 32555.

Ventilator management and critical care time billing

Ventilator management codes are mutually exclusive on the same date of service and split by setting and visit sequence, not by complexity.

Ventilator management code family.
CodeSetting
94002Ventilator management, initial day, inpatient/observation hospital or nursing facility (initial encounter)
94003Ventilator management, subsequent day, same inpatient/observation setting
94004Ventilator management, nursing facility, per day (distinct from the initial hospital-day code)

Only one of these applies per date of service per patient — billing more than one for the same day is a duplicate, not two separately payable services covering different aspects of vent management.

When critical care supersedes vent management billing. If the physician's time and the patient's acuity meet critical care criteria — a critical illness or injury that acutely impairs one or more vital organ systems, with a high probability of imminent or life-threatening deterioration — 99291/99292 replaces 94002–94004 rather than stacking with it; ventilator management is considered part of the critical care service when critical care criteria are met and shouldn't be billed separately alongside it. The distinction is clinical, not a coder's choice: a stable, chronically ventilated nursing-facility patient supports 94004; a patient in acute respiratory failure requiring minute-to-minute physician management supports critical care instead.

Time must be spent by the physician or qualified practitioner exclusively devoted to that one critically ill patient — time spent on other patients, or activities that could be performed by staff, doesn't count, and the record needs start/stop times or a clearly stated total to support the units billed. Same-group, same-specialty limit: 99291 is billable once per patient per calendar day across physicians of the same group and specialty; a second physician in the same group and specialty covering the same patient later the same day reports additional time as 99292, not a second 99291. Different-specialty co-management is a genuine exception: a pulmonologist managing respiratory failure and a cardiologist separately managing acute heart failure on the same critically ill patient, same day, can each independently bill critical care for their own distinct organ-system management, provided each physician's documentation stands on its own and doesn't overlap in time or scope with the other's. Full worked scenarios, including the vent-management-versus-critical-care decision tree, are in our ventilator management and critical care time billing guide for pulmonology.

ICD-10-CM specificity for pulmonary diagnoses

Pulmonology's medical necessity turns on diagnosis specificity as much as cardiology's does, and three areas carry almost all of the volume: COPD, asthma, and sleep apnea subtype.

COPD and the COPD-asthma overlap rule

COPD family, verified live against the FY2026 ICD-10-CM code set.
CodeDescription
J44.0COPD with (acute) lower respiratory infection
J44.1COPD with (acute) exacerbation
J44.89Other specified COPD
J44.9COPD, unspecified — avoid as primary where a more specific option applies

COPD and asthma can and should be coded together when a patient's chart genuinely documents both conditions separately — this is the overlap pattern (sometimes referred to clinically as asthma-COPD overlap, consistent with GOLD 2026 guidance recognizing it as a distinct clinical phenotype). The coding mechanics support this: category J44 carries an Excludes2 note relative to J45, not an Excludes1 — and that distinction matters, because Excludes1 means the two conditions cannot be coded together under any circumstance, while Excludes2 means the excluded condition is not part of the category but the patient can have both, and both can be coded together when documented separately. In practice, that means a chart showing a documented COPD diagnosis and a documented, separately assessed asthma diagnosis supports billing both J44.x and the applicable J45.x code on the same claim, rather than forcing a single diagnosis to stand in for a genuinely mixed clinical picture.

Asthma severity-plus-exacerbation code structure

Asthma coding is not just J45.909 (unspecified asthma, uncomplicated) with the specifics filled in later — the code structure itself encodes both severity and current status, and both are extractable straight from a properly documented visit.

Asthma severity-and-status code structure, verified live against the FY2026 ICD-10-CM code set.
SeverityUncomplicatedWith (acute) exacerbationWith status asthmaticus
Mild intermittentJ45.20J45.21J45.22

Severity classification (intermittent vs. the three persistent tiers) reflects the patient's baseline disease control, not the acuity of today's visit — it should already be established in the chart from prior assessments and carried forward, not reassessed from scratch every encounter. Exacerbation and status asthmaticus, by contrast, describe the current encounter: an exacerbation is a worsening episode; status asthmaticus is a severe, treatment-resistant episode that fails to respond to usual bronchodilator therapy. Reporting unspecified severity (J45.9xx) when a severity classification is already documented elsewhere in the chart, or reporting "uncomplicated" when the visit note describes an active exacerbation, both trigger specificity denials against payer policies that require the severity-plus-status combination for coverage of advanced testing or biologic therapy. Full crosswalks and documentation templates for capturing severity and status correctly at the point of care are in our ICD-10 coding guide for COPD, asthma, and overlap syndrome.

Sleep apnea subtype differentiation

Sleep apnea subtype codes, verified live against the FY2026 ICD-10-CM code set.
CodeDescription
G47.33Obstructive sleep apnea (adult) (pediatric)
G47.31Primary central sleep apnea
G47.37Central sleep apnea in conditions classified elsewhere (e.g., opioid-induced or heart-failure-associated central apnea, coded with the underlying condition)
G47.30Sleep apnea, unspecified — avoid as primary where the study result supports a subtype

Subtype drives medical necessity for the test type itself, not just for treatment: obstructive sleep apnea is the pattern home sleep apnea testing is validated to detect, while suspected central or complex/mixed apnea (a patient with cardiac disease, opioid use, or a prior non-diagnostic HST) is the clinical scenario that typically requires in-lab, attended polysomnography rather than an unattended home test, because HST devices are far less reliable at distinguishing central events from obstructive ones. A pre-test diagnosis of unspecified sleep apnea (G47.30) supports ordering a study at all, but once results are in hand, the subtype code should follow the study finding — billing follow-up visits and any CPAP or oxygen therapy orders against the specific, confirmed subtype rather than carrying the unspecified code forward indefinitely.

Modifiers pulmonology billers actually use

Five modifiers cover nearly every pulmonology-specific scenario, and each maps to a different structural reason a claim needs one.

26 / TC
  • Component split for PFT and diagnostic imaging interpretation in shared facility settings — 26 for interpretation only, TC for equipment and technologist time.
  • Check the code's PC/TC indicator in the Medicare Physician Fee Schedule before appending either; not every code in the PFT family supports a split.
25
  • E/M billed same day as spirometry/PFT, only where documentation shows a significant, separately identifiable service beyond ordering and overseeing the test.
  • A visit whose entire content is reviewing the test result rarely clears this bar on its own.
59 / XE, XS, XP, XU
  • Bronchoscopy bundling exceptions — only where the underlying pair's modifier indicator is 1 (never 0), and the note documents a genuinely distinct site, structure, encounter, or practitioner.
  • XS (separate structure) covers most defensible bronchoscopy unbundling, since it's usually a separate lobe or lung.
52
  • Reduced services for a home sleep apnea test recorded under 6 hours but still clinically interpretable.
  • Applies to the commercial CPT HST codes (95800/95801/95806); Medicare's HCPCS G-codes follow Medicare's own frequency and duration documentation rather than modifier 52.

Modifier 76 (repeat procedure, same physician, same day) shows up in pulmonology mainly for a technically inadequate PFT or sleep study repeated the same day — the repeat has to be clinically justified and distinct from simply disliking the first result, with the note stating why the original study wasn't usable. The complete modifier reference for the specialty, including a decision tree for each modifier above and the E/M-and-modifier-25 interaction covered earlier, is in our pulmonology modifiers guide: 25, 26/TC, 59, and 52 explained.

NCCI edits, MUE limits, and bundling traps

This is where pulmonology billing gets decided, and it's the section competitor guides in this niche consistently skip past with a generic "check the NCCI edits" line. Two values control whether a bundled or capped code is billable at all.

The NCCI modifier indicator attached to a Column 1/Column 2 code pair:

  • 0Never bypassable. No modifier, however well-documented, overrides it. The second code in the pair simply isn't separately payable.
  • 1Bypassable with documentation. An NCCI-associated modifier (59 or the specific X-modifier) can override the edit, but only where the record shows the second service was genuinely distinct.
  • 9Edit deleted. The pair no longer applies.

MUE limits. Secondary billing sources consistently describe a Medically Unlikely Edit of 1 unit per date of service on 94726 (plethysmography for lung volumes) — consistent with the fact that a lung volume measurement by a given method is a single study per encounter, not a per-attempt billable unit. ⚠️ The current MUE value for 94726, and the corresponding value for 94729 (DLCO), could not be independently confirmed against CMS's primary MUE files during this build; 94729 functions as an add-on-style code reported alongside a base spirometry or lung-volume code rather than standalone, and its own unit cap should be confirmed in the CMS MUE Lookup Tool by procedure code before relying on a specific number operationally. Point your team to the CMS MUE Lookup Tool by name rather than a cached figure, since MUE tables update quarterly alongside the NCCI edit files.

Pro tip

Before writing any bronchoscopy bundling appeal, look up the modifier indicator for the specific pair first. If it comes back 0 — and 31622 against 31623/31625 always will — stop. There is no documentation, however strong, that reverses a 0 indicator; the staff time is better spent on the claims that are actually winnable.

Top pulmonology denials and how to appeal them

Denial-to-appeal pairing only works when the appeal argument is matched to the actual reason the claim came back — a bundling denial, a PA mismatch, and a compliance-data gap each need a completely different fix, and sending the wrong one wastes the appeal window.

Payer and coverage nuances beyond Medicare

Medicare Advantage plans frequently diverge from traditional Medicare's sleep-testing preference even while operating under the same underlying benefit: many MA plans push home sleep apnea testing more aggressively than traditional Medicare's coverage articles require, using it as the default first-line study and requiring documented HST failure or a specific comorbidity (significant cardiopulmonary disease, suspected central or complex apnea, chronic opioid use) before authorizing in-lab polysomnography. That's a utilization-management overlay on top of, not a replacement for, the underlying Medicare coverage criteria — the MAC's article still defines what's covered, but the MA plan's prior-auth process decides which test gets authorized first.

Commercial payers commonly layer sleep-lab accreditation requirements on top of what CMS's articles specify, sometimes requiring accreditation from a specific body (the American Academy of Sleep Medicine is the most commonly required) even where the governing MAC's article accepts a broader set of accrediting organizations, and sometimes requiring accreditation that CMS's coverage criteria don't address for HST at all. Treat a commercial payer's provider manual and medical policy as the controlling document for that payer's claims — a Medicare LCD is context, not the answer, for a non-Medicare claim.

Multi-state jurisdiction tracking. Because sleep testing coverage is local, a practice with locations spanning more than one MAC jurisdiction needs an explicit map from practice location to governing article, not a single "our LCD" reference used across every site. The practical workflow: list every practice and sleep-lab location, identify the MAC with Part B jurisdiction over each (CGS, Noridian, WPS, First Coast, and Palmetto GBA between them cover a large share of the country's pulmonology practices), pull that MAC's current polysomnography article number and effective date, and route order-set coverage checks and prior documentation requirements through the article matching the location where the study is actually performed or interpreted — not the location of the billing office or the patient's home address. Revisit the map whenever a MAC republishes its article, since effective dates and article numbers do change, as the Noridian A57697-for-A57698 transition on 09/11/2025 shows.

Prior authorization for CPAP, oxygen, and biologics

Prior authorization gates nearly every high-cost service in pulmonology, and it's the single largest driver of the specialty's elevated denial rate. Getting ahead of it means building the chart before the PA request goes out, not scrambling to backfill it after a denial.

CPAP (HCPCS E0601)

CPAP prior authorization typically follows a structured trial-then-compliance-audit pattern: an initial authorization covers a defined trial period (commonly 90 days), during which the patient uses the device and the supplier or practice collects usage data from the machine's onboard compliance tracking. Continuation of coverage past the trial period requires demonstrating adherence — the standard threshold is a minimum of 70% of nights with at least 4 hours of use, measured across a consecutive 30-day period that falls within the initial 90-day trial window. Falling short of that threshold, or failing to submit the compliance data at all, is one of the most common and most preventable denial patterns in the specialty; it isn't a clinical judgment call, it's a data-submission requirement.

Oxygen therapy

Home oxygen requires a Certificate of Medical Necessity (CMN) documenting the qualifying test result (arterial blood gas or pulse oximetry at rest, during exercise, or during sleep, per the payer's specific protocol), the prescribed flow rate and duration of use, and a diagnosis supporting chronic hypoxemia. The CMN has to be completed and signed before the equipment is dispensed in most payer workflows, and re-certification at defined intervals is common for long-term oxygen therapy — missing a re-certification deadline is a frequent, entirely avoidable cause of a therapy that was previously covered suddenly denying.

Biologic asthma therapy

Biologic therapy for severe eosinophilic or allergic asthma (targeting IL-5, IL-4/IL-13, IgE, or related pathways depending on the specific agent) carries prior authorization requirements that stack with specialty pharmacy's own intake process: the payer's PA typically requires documented severe persistent asthma (tying directly back to the J45.5x severity coding covered earlier), a specific eosinophil count or IgE level threshold depending on the agent, a defined trial-and-failure history on standard controller therapy, and exacerbation frequency over a lookback period. Specialty pharmacy then runs its own separate benefits-verification and PA process before dispensing, which means the same clinical documentation often has to satisfy two distinct authorization workflows rather than one.

What has to be in the chart before the PA request goes out

For CPAP: a comprehensive sleep evaluation note, the diagnostic study result with subtype (G47.33/G47.31/G47.37), and a plan explicitly ordering CPAP as treatment. For oxygen: the qualifying test result at the correct testing condition (rest/exercise/sleep) and a diagnosis supporting chronic hypoxemia. For biologics: severity-coded asthma diagnosis (J45.5x), the specific biomarker threshold the agent's coverage policy requires, and a documented trial-and-failure history on standard therapy. Every one of these is something the payer will ask for eventually — assembling it before submission, rather than after a denial, is the entire difference between a clean PA and a 30% denial rate.

Full CMN documentation requirements, the 90-day CPAP compliance audit workflow with sample tracking templates, and the biologic PA stacking sequence with specialty pharmacy are in our CPAP and oxygen therapy prior authorization guide: avoiding the 30% denial rate.

Frequently asked questions

Why do our PFT claims deny even though the test itself was performed correctly?

The single most common PFT denial has nothing to do with the test equipment or the technician's work — it's a missing or incomplete physician interpretation and report. Every PFT code, whether it's spirometry, lung volumes, or DLCO, requires a signed interpretive statement addressing the pattern (obstructive, restrictive, mixed), severity, and comparison to prior studies where available. A tracing sitting in the chart without that narrative is billed but not supportable on audit, and it denies as CO-16 (missing information) or gets recouped later even if it initially paid.

Can we bill 94010 and 94060 for the same patient on the same day?

No. 94060 already includes a baseline spirometry maneuver plus a second set of maneuvers after bronchodilator administration, so it supersedes 94010 rather than stacking with it. If the physician ordered a pre/post bronchodilator study, bill 94060 alone. Billing 94010 in addition to 94060 for the same encounter is a duplicate service, not two separate tests, and payers bundle it accordingly.

Why does Medicare deny our home sleep apnea test claims when we bill the CPT sleep study codes?

Medicare requires HCPCS Level II codes — G0398, G0399, or G0400, selected by the type of portable monitor used — for every home sleep apnea test, regardless of how the device manufacturer or the sleep lab's own documentation describes it. Submitting the commercial-payer CPT codes 95800, 95801, or 95806 to Medicare triggers an automatic denial because those codes aren't recognized for HST on a Medicare claim; only traditional in-lab polysomnography uses the CPT 95808/95810/95811 family with Medicare.

What's the difference between a bundling denial and a prior-authorization device-mismatch denial on a CPAP claim?

They need completely different fixes. A bundling denial (CO-97) means a component of the CPAP claim was billed separately when it should have been included in another code — the fix is a coding correction. A PA device-mismatch denial means the equipment actually dispensed doesn't match what the prior authorization approved — wrong HCPCS code, wrong feature set, or a supplier substitution the payer never authorized. That's not something a clinical appeal fixes; it needs a corrected or amended authorization from the ordering physician's office before the claim can be resubmitted.

Do we need modifier 59 or an X-modifier when billing bronchoscopy with biopsy?

It depends entirely on which pair of codes you're billing and that pair's NCCI modifier indicator. Diagnostic bronchoscopy (31622) billed with a biopsy code like 31623 or 31625 carries a modifier indicator of 0 — no modifier overrides that edit, because the diagnostic exam is inherent to the biopsy procedure and 31622 simply isn't separately payable that day. A modifier only has a chance of working on a pair whose indicator is 1, and only when the operative note documents genuinely distinct anatomic sites, such as separate, non-contiguous lobes.

Why does an unspecified COPD or asthma code trigger a denial when the diagnosis seems obvious from the chart?

Medicare LCDs and commercial coverage policies for advanced pulmonary testing and biologic therapy publish the specific diagnosis codes that support medical necessity, and J44.9 or J45.909 frequently aren't on that list even when the clinical picture is unambiguous. The fix is documentation specificity at the point of care: severity classification for asthma (mild intermittent through severe persistent), exacerbation status, and for COPD, whether an acute lower respiratory infection or acute exacerbation is present. An unspecified code isn't wrong, but it's the version of the diagnosis least likely to clear a coverage edit.

How many minutes of critical care time do we need before we can bill both 99291 and 99292?

99291 covers the first 30 to 74 minutes of cumulative critical care time in a calendar day; under 30 minutes doesn't support 99291 at all and should be billed as an appropriate E/M service instead. 99292 is only added once total time reaches 75 minutes, and one unit of 99292 covers the range from 75 through 104 minutes. Past 104 minutes, add one more unit of 99292 for each additional 30-minute increment. Time has to be physician or qualified practitioner time devoted exclusively to that one critically ill patient, documented with start and stop times or total minutes.

Which MAC's sleep study coverage article applies to our practice if we see patients in more than one state?

The article that governs a claim is determined by the Medicare Administrative Contractor with jurisdiction over the rendering provider's practice location for that date of service, not the patient's home state or the billing address. A multi-state pulmonology group needs to map every practice location to its MAC — CGS, Noridian, WPS, First Coast, and Palmetto GBA each publish their own current polysomnography billing-and-coding article — and route claims and prior documentation checks through the correct article for the site where the study was actually performed or interpreted.
