Occupational therapy billing and coding: the complete 2026 guide.

OT billing runs on four gates that decide whether a claim pays at all: timed-unit rounding under the 8-minute rule, the OTA payment-reduction modifier, diagnosis specificity tied to a functional deficit rather than just a condition, and an annual dollar threshold that flips claims from silent to KX-required overnight. Most published OT guides restate the same generalities on all four. This one names the specific MAC articles, the exact edge case in the assistant-modifier standard that generates most OTA disputes, and the RTM code that every other guide gets wrong.

Reviewed by Phares A., MD · Physiotherapist, CPC (AAPC) · Updated August 2026

Key takeaways

  • The KX modifier threshold rose to $2,480 for 2026 — from $2,410 in 2025 — and the $3,000 targeted medical review threshold above it is unchanged.
  • Only four of the five RTM codes are OT-billable. 98976 is respiratory-system device supply only, not an OT code — competitor content routinely lists all five as available to OT and that's simply wrong.
  • The CQ/CO 10% de minimis standard has an edge case almost nobody explains: it doesn't govern the final 15-minute unit of a multi-unit day when the OT/OTA split straddles the 8-minute midpoint — and that's exactly the scenario behind most OTA modifier disputes.
  • Functional limitation reporting G-codes ended January 1, 2019. If your templates still reference them, that's stale guidance, not a live requirement.

Occupational therapy billing at a glance

Four structural problems drive most of OT's denial and underpayment volume: timed-unit rounding under the 8-minute rule, the OTA payment-reduction modifier and its edge cases, diagnosis specificity tied to the functional deficit rather than the diagnosis alone, and the annual KX/targeted-review threshold gate that changes every calendar year. Here's the shape of it before the detail.

What changed in 2026

Nothing in 2026 restructured OT coding the way lower-extremity revascularization was restructured in cardiology — OT's 2026 changes are dollar thresholds and policy extensions, not new or deleted codes. Four of them matter operationally.

2026 changes affecting occupational therapy billing, verified where a primary source was reachable.
Area20252026
KX modifier threshold (OT)$2,410$2,480 — tracked separately from the combined PT/SLP threshold, which is also $2,480 for 2026
Targeted medical review threshold$3,000$3,000, unchanged — holds through 2028
Telehealth flexibility for OTExtended through prior deadlineExtended through December 31, 2027, with retroactive coverage for the brief lapse around the funding deadline
PFS efficiency adjustmentN/ANew −2.5% cut to intra-service time and work RVUs for non-time-based codes, recalculated every three years — CMS states time-based codes are generally exempt

The efficiency adjustment is the one item on this list we can't give you a clean answer on. ⚠️ CMS's stated exemption covers time-based codes broadly (E/M, care management, behavioral health, telehealth-list services), but OT's evaluation codes (97165–97168) are flat per-encounter codes, not per-15-minute timed units, and public reporting on the final rule doesn't specify their treatment explicitly. Don't assume evaluation reimbursement is exempt until your MAC or fee schedule posting confirms it — check the CMS Physician Fee Schedule Look-Up Tool by name for your locality before budgeting around either outcome.

Evaluation and re-evaluation coding: 97165–97168 by complexity tier

Evaluation codes are OT's version of E/M leveling — complexity tier, not time, decides the code, and each one is billed once per encounter regardless of how long the evaluation actually ran. Our dedicated occupational therapy evaluation codes guide covers the full documentation-to-code mapping; here's the structure.

OT evaluation complexity tiers.
CodeComplexityPerformance deficitsTypical time
97165Low complexity1–3 performance deficits, stable/predictable clinical presentation~30 minutes
97166Moderate complexity3–5 performance deficits, evolving presentation~45 minutes
97167High complexity5+ performance deficits, unstable/unpredictable presentation~60 minutes
97168Re-evaluationRequires a significant clinical change, not a routine progress checkTime not tier-defined

97168 is where re-evaluation billing goes wrong most often: it requires a documented, significant change in the patient's functional status, a new diagnosis, or a clinical finding that alters the plan of care — not simply "time for the periodic progress note." A payer reviewing a 97168 claim is checking for that clinical trigger specifically, and its absence in the note is grounds for a straightforward medical-necessity denial. Documentation defensibility for any of the four codes rests on three elements tying directly to the tier billed: the client's occupational profile and history, the objective performance assessment identifying each deficit counted toward the tier, and the clinical decision-making that set the plan of care. A 97167 note that lists three deficits, not five, doesn't support the code it was billed under, independent of how much time the visit actually took.

Core procedure family: timed treatment codes and the 8-minute rule

This is OT's highest-volume billing category and the one with the least room for rounding error. Five codes carry most of the timed-treatment volume:

Core timed treatment codes, each billed in 15-minute units.
CodeService
97110Therapeutic exercise — strength, endurance, range of motion, flexibility
97112Neuromuscular re-education of movement, balance, coordination, posture
97530Therapeutic activities — dynamic activities to improve functional performance
97535Self-care/home management training (ADL training), including adaptive equipment use
97140Manual therapy techniques — mobilization/manipulation, manual lymphatic drainage, manual traction

Every one of these is a 15-minute timed code, and units are set by total minutes of skilled, one-on-one timed service delivered, not by minutes rounded up per code. The standard 8-minute-rule unit table:

8-minute rule: total timed minutes to billable units.
Total timed minutesBillable units
< 8 minutes0 units — not separately billable
8–22 minutes1 unit
23–37 minutes2 units
38–52 minutes3 units
53–67 minutes4 units
68–82 minutes5 units
83–97 minutes6 units

Minutes across multiple different timed codes on the same date are added together, then divided by 15 to determine total units, before those units are distributed across the specific codes performed — you don't apply the table separately to each code's individual minutes. A session with 20 minutes of 97110 and 15 minutes of 97530 totals 35 minutes, which is 2 units under the table, split across the two codes based on which had more minutes documented.

97140 and 97530 carry a known NCCI pairing that requires a 59 or XS modifier when both are genuinely performed as distinct services in the same session — manual therapy on one body region and therapeutic activity addressing a different functional task, each independently time-documented. Billing both without the modifier, or appending the modifier without the documentation to support two genuinely separate services, are both denial and audit triggers respectively. Worked scenarios for every unit-count edge case, including split OT/OTA sessions, are in our dedicated 8-minute rule guide for occupational therapy.

Core procedure family: cognitive intervention, self-care, and orthotic/assistive tech training

97129 (cognitive intervention, first 15 minutes) and its add-on 97130 (each additional 15 minutes) replaced the old, deleted 97127 code and are billed per-15-minute like the codes above, but as a self-contained pair rather than folded into the general timed-code family — use 97129/97130 specifically for cognitive-function interventions such as attention, memory, and problem-solving retraining, not for physical performance deficits better described by 97110 or 97530.

Orthotic and prosthetic training splits on initial-versus-subsequent status, and that distinction is a common denial trigger:

Orthotic and prosthetic training codes.
CodeServiceWhen to use it
97760Orthotic management and training, initial encounter, each 15 minutesFirst fitting and training on a new orthotic device
97761Prosthetic training, initial encounter, each 15 minutesFirst fitting and training on a new prosthetic device
97763Orthotic/prosthetic management, subsequent encounter, each 15 minutesAny follow-up encounter after the initial fitting — adjustment, refinement, continued training

Billing 97760 or 97761 on a follow-up visit, or 97763 on the true first fitting, is a straightforward code-selection error and denies as such once a payer cross-references the patient's visit history against the device. When a custom orthotic is fabricated as part of the encounter, the supply itself is billed separately under the appropriate L-code (HCPCS Level II) alongside 97760 — the training time has to independently clear the 8-minute threshold to be billed at all, separate from whatever time went into fabrication or fitting mechanics that aren't training.

Core procedure family: remote therapeutic monitoring and telehealth

RTM is where the most competitor content gets OT scope wrong. Of the five RTM CPT codes, only four are OT-billable:

RTM codes actually usable by occupational therapy. 98976 is intentionally omitted — see below.
CodeServiceKey rule
98975Initial set-up and patient education on RTM device useBilled once per episode of care, not monthly; requires the device to have collected at least 16 days of data within a 30-day period before the associated monitoring codes are billable
98977Device supply with scheduled recording/alert transmission, musculoskeletal system, each 30 days16 of 30 days of data required, same as 98975/98976
98980RTM treatment management, first 20 minutes of clinical time per calendar monthRequires at least one interactive communication with the patient/caregiver during the month; billed once per month regardless of modality count
98981RTM treatment management, each additional 20 minutesAdd-on to 98980; billed once per each additional complete 20-minute increment within the same month

98976 does not belong on this list, and no OT claim should carry it. 98976 is device supply for respiratory system monitoring exclusively — it is not an alternate or general-purpose RTM code, and OT scope doesn't include respiratory system monitoring. Several widely-circulated OT billing guides list all five RTM codes as available to occupational therapists; that's incorrect, and billing 98976 on an OT claim is a code-validity denial waiting to happen, not a gray area. Telehealth delivery for the interactive-communication component of 98980/98981 is covered by the 2026 extension through December 31, 2027 discussed above, subject to the modality and originating-site rules in effect for OT telehealth specifically.

ICD-10 specificity and crosswalks

OT medical necessity increasingly hinges on whether the diagnosis code supports the functional deficit driving treatment, not just whether it names a condition. Payers and state Medicaid programs are tightening this in particular for the evaluation tier billed — an unspecified code can support 97165, but the deficit count that justifies 97166 or 97167 needs a diagnosis that actually says something about laterality, dominance, or severity. Modifiers

OT modifier logic centers on who furnished the service, whether the service is habilitative or rehabilitative, and whether the annual dollar threshold has been crossed — a genuinely different structure from cardiology's component-split logic, but just as unforgiving of a missed one.

GO
  • Identifies every line as furnished under an OT plan of care. Required on every OT claim line, no exceptions — not situational the way most modifiers are.
  • Reported as the single most common OT-specific denial driver when missing; almost always a charge-capture defaults problem, not a training gap.
CO (assistant-furnished)
  • CO is the OTA equivalent of PT's CQ modifier — applied when an occupational therapy assistant furnishes the service, subject to the same 10% de minimis standard.
  • Under the standard, a unit gets CO when at least 10% of its minutes were furnished by the OTA; the unit then reimburses at 85% of the physician fee schedule rate.
96 / 97
  • 96 (habilitative) versus 97 (rehabilitative) — required by many commercial and Medicaid plans to distinguish services building a new skill from services restoring a lost one.
  • Check payer policy per plan; the two are not interchangeable and the wrong one can trigger a benefit-category mismatch even when the underlying service was correct.
CO on the final unit by default
  • The 10% de minimis standard does not automatically apply to the final 15-minute unit of a multi-unit treatment day when the OT/OTA time split straddles the 8-minute midpoint of that unit — treat the final partial unit's split on its own terms rather than assuming the same 10% rule mechanically carries over. This exact scenario generates most OTA modifier disputes we see, and it's the edge case most guides skip entirely.

Modifier 59 and the X-modifiers (XE, XS, XP, XU) override an NCCI bundling edit where the pair's modifier indicator allows it — most commonly needed on the 97140/97530 pairing discussed above. KX attests that services beyond the $2,480 threshold are medically necessary and documented; appending it without the supporting documentation is functionally a false attestation, not a formality. The complete modifier reference, including the full OT/OTA time-split worked example, is in our occupational therapy modifiers guide.

NCCI edits, MUEs, and bundling

OT bundling follows the same CMS framework as every other specialty — a modifier indicator per Column 1/Column 2 code pair, and an MUE Adjudication Indicator per code-level unit cap — but the values themselves are OT-specific and change quarterly.

The NCCI modifier indicator:

  • 0Never bypassable. No modifier overrides it regardless of documentation.
  • 1Bypassable with documentation. An NCCI-associated modifier (59 or the specific X-modifier) can override the edit only where the record shows the second service was genuinely distinct.
  • 9Edit deleted. No longer applies.

The MUE Adjudication Indicator (MAI):

  • 1Claim-line edit. Units above the cap deny that line; a genuinely repeated service can sometimes be split across lines with documentation.
  • 2Absolute, date-of-service edit. No appeal path exists — confirm the MAI before staff spend time on an appeal.
  • 3Appealable, date-of-service edit. A real path exists with documentation the excess units were distinct and necessary.

Where these show up most in OT: 97140 and 97530 carry a widely-reported bundling relationship requiring 59/XS when both are billed the same day, and evaluation codes billed alongside 97530 or the general therapeutic-activity family have historically been treated as non-separately-payable same-date in industry billing guidance. ⚠️ This build could not open CMS's primary NCCI PTP edit file directly to confirm the current indicator value for either pair (CMS's site returned access errors to automated fetch attempts made while researching this page), so treat both as reported-but-unconfirmed and verify the current pair-specific value in the CMS NCCI PTP Edits Lookup Tool before building either into a scrubber rule. The same caveat applies to any other single indicator or MAI value cited on this page. Full bundling reference by procedure family is in our NCCI edits and MUE limits guide for occupational therapy.

Medicare Advantage is where OT prior authorization is expanding fastest. UnitedHealthcare's outpatient therapy prior-authorization program, already live in most states since September 2024, extended to Medicare Advantage individual and group retiree plan members in Arizona and California starting February 1, 2026. That expansion pattern is worth watching regardless of which MA plans your practice sees today, because it has moved state by state rather than launching nationally. A 2026 HHS Office of Inspector General report found that among the largest Medicare Advantage organizations, appealed denials for inpatient rehabilitation facility stays were overturned 43% of the time on average (ranging 14%–86% by plan), and appealed skilled nursing facility denials were overturned 95% of the time — findings specific to IRF/SNF post-acute care rather than outpatient OT directly, but a strong signal that MA denials in the broader rehab category are frequently wrong on the first pass and worth appealing rather than writing off. Full detail on which OT services trigger MA prior auth and how to build the appeal case is in our prior authorization and Medicare Advantage guide for occupational therapy.

Prior authorization and medical necessity documentation

Traditional Medicare doesn't require prior authorization for standard outpatient OT visits, but three scenarios trigger it most often across Medicare Advantage and commercial plans: extended plans of care beyond the initial authorized visit count, RTM device supply as a standalone authorization line, and intensive pediatric protocols billed at high frequency. Confirm the specific plan's authorized visit count before treatment runs past it, not after a denial arrives citing an authorization gap that was preventable.

Across MACs and MA plans, three documentation elements are checked most consistently, and their absence is named directly in denial-pattern research as a recurring driver: measurable functional goals stated in terms a reviewer can verify against progress notes, a clearly documented baseline functional status to measure change against, and an explicit statement of why the service required a skilled therapist rather than unskilled assistance or a home exercise program alone. A plan of care that says "continue OT to improve function" without naming the specific skilled component is functionally under-documented even when the underlying treatment was entirely appropriate. Full detail on plan-specific prior-auth triggers and documentation templates that hold up on review are in our prior authorization and Medicare Advantage guide, cross-referenced above.

Frequently asked questions

What's the actual KX modifier threshold for occupational therapy in 2026?

$2,480, up from $2,410 in 2025. It's a separate threshold from the combined physical therapy/speech-language pathology threshold, which is also $2,480 for 2026 but tracked independently. Once a beneficiary's cumulative OT charges for the calendar year cross $2,480, every subsequent claim needs the KX modifier attesting that continued therapy is medically necessary and documented, or it denies automatically. The targeted medical review threshold sits above that, unchanged at $3,000.

Do we still need to report G-codes for functional limitation reporting?

No. CMS eliminated the functional limitation reporting program, including the G-codes and their severity modifiers, effective January 1, 2019. If a workflow, template, or scrubber rule in your practice still references G-codes or FLR severity modifiers, it's coding to a requirement that hasn't existed in years and should be removed rather than maintained.

Can occupational therapists bill all five RTM codes?

No, and this is one of the most commonly repeated errors in OT billing content. OTs can bill 98975 (initial setup and education), 98977 (device supply, musculoskeletal system), 98980, and 98981 (treatment management, first and each additional 20 minutes). 98976 covers device supply for respiratory system monitoring only and is not an OT code under any circumstance. Billing 98976 for an OT episode of care is a code-validity error, not a documentation one.

What's the difference between the CQ and CO modifiers?

CQ marks a PTA's services under physical therapy; CO is the parallel modifier for an occupational therapy assistant, or OTA. Both trigger the same payment consequence under the 2022 de minimis policy: when at least 10% of a timed unit's minutes were furnished by the assistant, that unit gets the modifier and is reimbursed at 85% of the physician fee schedule rate. Missing the CO modifier on assistant-furnished units, or applying it when the therapist actually met the time threshold alone, are both common and both audit-visible because time logs usually show exactly who did what.

Why do our OT evaluations keep denying for missing the GO modifier?

GO identifies every line as furnished under an OT plan of care, and its absence is consistently reported as the single most common OT-specific denial driver, ahead of even medical necessity denials. Unlike some modifiers that only matter in specific scenarios, GO belongs on every OT claim line without exception. The fix is almost always a charge-capture or EHR template default, not a training issue — if the modifier isn't hard-coded into the OT charge set, it gets missed on high-volume days.

Does an unspecified diagnosis code ever work for an OT evaluation claim?

Sometimes, but it's increasingly the weaker choice. Payers and state Medicaid programs are leaning harder on deficit-specific codes — laterality, dominance, and the functional impact itself — to support the complexity tier billed for an evaluation, not just to describe the underlying condition. G81.90 (unspecified hemiplegia) will often process, but I69.351 (hemiplegia following cerebral infarction, right dominant side) is far more defensible if the claim is ever reviewed, and it's the same amount of chart work to select the specific code at the point of evaluation.

Does Medicare require prior authorization for outpatient OT?

Traditional Medicare doesn't require prior authorization for standard outpatient OT visits, but Medicare Advantage plans increasingly do, and that gap is widening in 2026. UnitedHealthcare's outpatient therapy prior-auth program, already active in most states, expanded to Medicare Advantage members in Arizona and California starting February 1, 2026. Extended plans of care beyond the initial authorized visit count are the most common trigger, so confirm the specific MA plan's rules before treatment runs past whatever visit count was initially authorized, not after the denial arrives.

How do we know if an NCCI edit between two OT codes can be bypassed with a modifier?

The pair's modifier indicator decides it, the same as in every other specialty: 0 means no modifier overrides the edit under any circumstance, 1 means an NCCI-associated modifier (59 or the specific X-modifier) can bypass it if the documentation genuinely supports a distinct service, and 9 means the edit no longer applies. Look the specific pair up in the CMS NCCI Procedure-to-Procedure Edits Lookup Tool before appending a modifier to clear an edit, because indicator values are pair-specific and revised quarterly.
