Our complete care management guide

The eight procedures, the month-end close and the chart audit that keep a care management program billable.

A care management program is a monthly cycle with the same failure points every month: a missing benefits check, a note written from memory, a minute counted twice, an RTM month with no live call. The fix is procedure and a second pair of eyes. This page lays out eight standard operating procedures, the close that turns them into claims, and a chart audit scorecard that catches what slips through.

Key takeaways

  • Eight procedures cover the whole month: benefits check, enrollment visit, onboarding call, monthly care management call, messaging, nurse RTM review, escalation and month-end close.
  • Every procedure ends with what must be in the chart. If it is not documented, it did not happen for billing purposes.
  • Close the month within five business days while the staff who did the work remember it.
  • Audit a sample of charts every month against a scorecard with critical items. Any critical failure holds the claim until it is corrected.
  • These are recommended practice. Medicare does not require these procedures, but each one is built to meet a Medicare rule or to produce evidence of it.

SOP 1: benefits check

Who: billing staff. When: before the enrollment visit.

  • Original Medicare: check eligibility only (Part B active, no Medicare Advantage plan). Run a full benefits check for every other plan, Medicare Advantage included.
  • Check eligibility in the payer portal, and call if the portal does not show the codes.
  • Ask about every code the program may use: 99426, 99427, 99490, 99439, 98975, 98977, 98985, 98979, 98980 and 98981.
  • Apply the payer rules from the payer page, for example no RTM for payers that exclude it and no CCM for plans that do not pay it separately.
  • Send the result to the billing practitioner before the visit.

Document: payer, date, codes covered, prior authorization, patient cost share, reference number, staff name.

SOP 2: enrollment visit

Who: the billing practitioner (physician, nurse practitioner or physician assistant). When: an in-person visit, before any billed month.

  • Confirm the patient is an existing patient of the practice (recommended for RTM).
  • Choose CCM or PCM using the decision rule in CCM vs PCM and record the reason.
  • Explain the service and obtain consent using the script in the enrollment page.
  • For RTM: confirm a musculoskeletal diagnosis and write the order (the condition, the data collected and how it guides treatment).
  • Approve the starting care plan goals, or ask the care team to draft them for approval within seven days.
  • Assign the patient to this practitioner as the billing and supervising practitioner.

Document: a visit note naming the program discussed, consent (date, method, who obtained it), diagnoses with the qualifying one first, the RTM order and the path chosen.

SOP 3: onboarding call, month one

Who: a registered dietitian or nurse for the care management call; a medical assistant or coordinator for app set-up. When: within 14 days of enrollment.

  • Before the call, review the chart and build the draft care plan. Time spent before consent and the initiating visit does not count.
  • App set-up and teaching: show the patient how to log musculoskeletal symptoms. This supports 98975, which has no time minimum.
  • Walk through the care plan goals: pain, function, sleep impact, activity and any nutrition goal linked to the pain plan.
  • Agree on barriers, next steps and the check-in schedule.
  • Keep detailed menu or recipe planning to a separate, uncounted segment.
  • Route any medication or treatment question to the practitioner (SOP 7).

Document: the call note with counted and uncounted segments in minutes, the care plan updated in the EHR, and any protocol PDF attached with a short summary.

SOP 4: monthly care management call

Who: a dietitian or nurse. When: once a month, from month two.

  • Review the app data and last month’s goals before the call.
  • On the call: symptoms, progress on each goal, barriers, medication adherence (no changes) and coordination needs.
  • Update the care plan and send changes to the practitioner for approval.
  • Log minutes by segment and count only care management minutes.

Document: the call note, the care plan update and the minutes counted.

SOP 5: in-app messaging

Who: clinical staff only, within state rules. When: as needed.

  • Answer clinical messages within one business day.
  • Log the real minutes spent reading and writing each clinical message. Automated messages count as zero.
  • Each billed CCM or PCM month needs at least one two-way exchange or a call, which is our recommended standard. If there is no patient reply by day 20, call the patient.
  • Never give new medical advice by message; pass on only practitioner-documented advice.

Document: the message thread saved to the chart, or the time log entry with date, staff, minutes and topic.

SOP 6: nurse RTM review and live call

Who: a registered nurse or licensed practical or vocational nurse. When: data review weekly; live call at least once a month.

  • Review the musculoskeletal data against practitioner-set thresholds and record the days of data.
  • Make the live phone or video call, with about five minutes or more of clinical content as our recommended standard: discuss results, adherence and response.
  • Escalate to the practitioner when thresholds are crossed (SOP 7).
  • Log total RTM management minutes (data review, call and treatment-related messages), kept separate from CCM or PCM minutes.

Document: the data reviewed (dates and values), call date and length, what was discussed, any escalation and total minutes.

SOP 7: escalation and red flags

Who: all staff. When: immediately when triggered.

SOP 8: month-end close

Who: the care team lead and the practice biller; the practitioner attests. When: the first five business days of the next month.

  1. Total each patient’s minutes by code family (CCM, PCM, RTM management) and the RTM days of data.
  2. Choose codes with the selection table below, and drop any month without patient contact (CCM and PCM) or without a live call (RTM management).
  3. Check payer rules and conflicting codes.
  4. The practitioner reviews the patient list and attests to oversight for the month (recommended practice).
  5. Send the billing file below. Our convention is the last day of the service month as the date of service; CMS also allows the date the time threshold is met. RTM device codes use the last day of the 30-day period.

Document: the monthly ledger per patient and the practitioner attestation.

The monthly billing file

If an outside team does the work, it should send the practice biller one file per month, one row per patient per code, by the fifth business day, and the biller enters the charges in the practice management system. The time logs and care plan updates go into the patient chart.

The monthly chart audit

The audit is the control that catches what the procedures miss. Sample a share of enrolled patients each month, score each chart against the items below, and track the results over time. A reasonable starting rule: review at least 10% of patients enrolled that month with a floor of five charts, and always include every patient billed 90 or more PCM minutes, every patient whose minutes changed sharply from the prior month and every patient billed for the first time.

Score each item pass, fail or not applicable. Track two numbers: the overall pass rate, with a target of 95% or better, and the number of critical failures, with a target of zero. A critical failure on a chart that has not yet been billed means correct it first or do not bill that month. A critical failure on a claim already paid is a question for your compliance officer or healthcare attorney, who can tell you what the overpayment report-and-return rules require.

What to do with the results

  • Same-day fixes: a missing signature or attestation, an unfilled bracket, a log entry missing a credential.
  • Staff coaching: a pattern in one person’s notes, such as rounded minutes or uncounted segments missing.
  • Process changes: if the same item fails across many charts, change the template or the SOP, not the people.
  • Report: a one-page monthly summary to the practitioner and the compliance officer with pass rate, critical failures and corrective actions.

Frequently asked questions

Do we need all eight procedures?

Medicare does not require any of them, but each one exists to meet a Medicare rule or to leave evidence of it. A small practice can combine them into a one-page checklist per patient, as long as the benefits check, consent, time log, oversight and month-end reconciliation all happen and are documented.

Who should do the chart audit?

Someone who did not do the work in the chart: the compliance officer, the practice manager, or an outside reviewer. The point of the audit is independence.

How big a sample is enough?

At least 10% of enrolled patients each month with a minimum of five charts, plus every high-risk chart (90 or more PCM minutes, a sharp change in minutes, a first billed month). Increase the sample when the failure rate is high and reduce it only after several clean months.

Can the month-end close wait until we have time?

Close it within five business days. The longer the delay, the more likely minutes are estimated from memory, which is the finding auditors look for first.

What is the date of service for a monthly code?

Our convention is the last day of the service month. CMS also allows the date the time threshold is met. RTM device codes use the last day of the 30-day period. Use one convention consistently across the program.

Does an outside team need a separate billing file?

If an outside team does the work, a standard monthly file keeps their documentation and your claims aligned. The practice biller still enters the charges, and the time logs and care plan updates go into the practice’s own EHR.

Confirm before you rely on this. The SOPs and scorecard on this page are recommended practice, not Medicare requirements, and should be reviewed by your compliance officer or healthcare attorney before adoption. Medicare rules and payer policies change; verify current requirements before relying on this page.
