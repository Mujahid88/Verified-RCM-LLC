Care management billing: getting CCM, PCM and RTM paid without the audit risk.

Chronic care management, principal care management and remote therapeutic monitoring pay a practice for work done between visits. They also draw more audit attention than almost any other monthly service, because the rules on who can bill, who can do the work and which minutes count are easy to get wrong. This guide covers all three the way a billing team has to work them: patient by patient, month by month, code by code.

Key takeaways

  • Default to PCM for a specialist. Principal care management is built for one serious condition. Use chronic care management only when the patient has two or more chronic conditions and no other practitioner is billing it.
  • RTM is the riskiest of the three. Medicare pays it only for data from a product that meets the FDA definition of a medical device, and the device codes cover respiratory, musculoskeletal and cognitive behavioral therapy monitoring only.
  • Only clinical staff time counts, and only after consent. Each minute counts toward one code, never two, and time is never rounded up.
  • The billing practitioner has to stay involved every month. CMS says a service with little oversight and no clinical integration is not being furnished by the practice.
  • Cost sharing applies. Medicare patients owe the Part B deductible and 20% coinsurance on each billed code, and consent has to say so. Never describe the program as covered by insurance.
  • Payers disagree. Some exclude RTM outright, some do not pay CCM at all, and some have payment policies that cut across all three. Check benefits before you enroll.
  • The 2027 proposed rule could reshape RTM. It would require RTM staff to be the practice’s own employees and add an initiating visit. As of November 3, 2026 it is still a proposal, and it applies to RTM and RPM, not to CCM or PCM.
Principal care management
One serious condition

Designed for specialists. Clinical staff bill 99426 and 99427; the practitioner’s own time bills as 99424 and 99425.

Chronic care management
Two or more conditions

Only one practitioner can bill CCM for a patient in a calendar month. Clinical staff bill 99490 and 99439.

Remote therapeutic monitoring
Muscle and joint data

Billed next to CCM or PCM with separate time. Set-up 98975, device days 98977 and 98985, management 98979, 98980 and 98981.

What the three services are, and why specialists should care

All three services pay for care that happens outside the exam room. A nurse or dietitian reviews the patient’s plan, calls to check on progress, answers messages, coordinates with other providers and keeps the practitioner informed. The practitioner stays responsible for the plan and for every medical decision. Medicare pays a monthly amount once the time and documentation thresholds are met.

For a specialty practice, this matters for two reasons. First, the patients who need the most between-visit work, such as chronic pain, complex spine disease, rheumatologic and neurologic conditions, are the ones whose outcomes depend on it. Second, the revenue is recurring and predictable when the workflow is sound, which makes it one of the few service lines where the billing work and the clinical work reinforce each other.

A related code family, advanced primary care management (G0556 to G0558), bundles care management for primary care practices without minute counting. Some payers address it by name, and it is outside this guide. The rest of this guide takes each row above apart, and the detail pages linked in each section go much deeper than this overview.

Which service fits which patient

The choice is made by the billing practitioner at the enrollment visit, and the reason belongs in the visit note. A rule that works in practice:

  1. If the patient has two or more chronic conditions, no other practitioner bills CCM for them, and the payer pays CCM, use CCM.
  2. Otherwise, if the patient has one serious, complex condition, use PCM. For a specialist, this is the default.
  3. Add RTM only when the payer covers it and the patient has a musculoskeletal condition and treatment plan that the monitoring supports.

Two checks save most of the rework. Ask the patient and check claims history for another practitioner billing CCM, because the OIG has found overpayments where more than one practitioner billed the same patient in the same month. And confirm that the patient was seen by the billing practitioner, because the audit attention on remote services is concentrated on patients with no history at the practice. The full comparison, including the combinations Medicare allows, is in CCM vs PCM: choosing the right service.

The codes and the clock

Every care management code is built on minutes in a calendar month. The first code in each family needs a set number of minutes, and add-on codes need a further full block each. A minute is never rounded up: 19 minutes is not 20.

Minutes add up across separate activities and different days, from any clinical staff member working under the practitioner. Fifteen minutes of care plan revision, five minutes of messaging and ten minutes of coordination on three different days is thirty minutes. RTM is the riskiest of the three

Remote therapeutic monitoring pays well and audits badly. Three rules decide whether a claim holds up.

The product must meet the FDA definition of a medical device. Medicare adopted that requirement when it created the RTM codes. Food logs, plain symptom diaries and general wellness tracking do not qualify, and a written regulatory opinion on the product is the evidence a billing practice needs. RTM data that counts and the FDA device question walks through what the FDA guidance says and which data supports a musculoskeletal claim.

The data has to relate to the treatment. For a pain practice, that means pain intensity, pain interference, function and adherence to the prescribed plan, tied to a documented musculoskeletal diagnosis. Nutrition-only days do not count toward the days of data.

The management codes need a live call. Chat does not substitute, however long the thread. And exercise-based monitoring has its own rule about the therapy plan of care and the GP modifier. All of it is in remote therapeutic monitoring billing, 98975 to 98985.

Who may do the work

Medicare counts only the time of clinical staff, meaning people allowed by law, regulation and facility policy to assist with the service, working under the billing practitioner’s general supervision. In its CCM guidance CMS names registered dietitians among them, and the same incident-to rules apply to PCM, though we found no PCM-specific statement. Staff may be employees or contracted. Patients and staff must be in the United States.

State law then narrows who may do what. Arizona and Texas, for example, treat medical assistants and unlicensed coordinators very differently: Texas allows delegation of a wide range of tasks under a physician-signed written protocol that is reviewed every year, while Arizona does not require a protocol but limits unlicensed staff to tasks that need no medical judgment. Interpreting clinical data and changing a treatment plan stay with licensed staff in both. The staffing table, the delegation rules and a model protocol outline are in who can do care management work.

Enrollment and consent

The initiating visit is the only face-to-face requirement. For CCM it is required for new patients and for patients not seen by the billing practitioner within the past year, and a qualifying office visit (99212 to 99215), an annual wellness visit or a Welcome to Medicare visit works, as long as the program is discussed at that visit. A practice can enroll the patient at the follow-up visit it already bills; no second visit or second E/M is needed. Consent, spoken or written, has to be documented before the first billed month, and it has to cover availability, cost sharing, the one-practitioner limit for CCM, and the right to stop at any time. Enrolling patients in CCM and PCM has the consent script and the visit note wording.

Documentation that survives an audit

Medicare sets no required template for the care plan or the time log. What matters is that the record shows, for this patient, why the service is needed and what was actually done each month. Auditors look for patient-specific symptoms, thresholds and actions, and they flag notes that read like a program description or that are cloned across patients.

  • A care plan with the problem list, measurable goals (a named measure, a baseline, a target and a date), symptom and medication management, planned interventions and who does each, coordination and a review schedule.
  • A time log with one line per activity: date, staff and credential, code family, minutes counted, what was done, which condition it addressed, and whether it was a live call.
  • A provider review of the care plan and of every change, with a short monthly note per patient as recommended practice.

Templates for the provider enrollment notes, a measurable-goals worked example and the call note are in care management documentation.

Payers: Medicare first, then everything else

Original Medicare does not require prior authorization for any of these codes, and they are not in the WISeR prior authorization model. Everything else needs a benefits check before enrollment, Medicare Advantage included, because plans differ and a plan that follows Medicare on one code may restrict another. Published policies today include Cigna excluding remote therapeutic monitoring for every indication, UnitedHealthcare commercial listing most RTM codes as eligible but not 98979, HealthSpring and Oscar declining to pay CCM separately, and Aetna tightening its remote monitoring policy in 2026, which is why Aetna needs written confirmation before any RTM claim. The table, with the policy each statement rests on and the benefits call script, is in payer rules for CCM, PCM and RTM.

The 2027 proposals, and what to do before the final rule

CMS published its proposed 2027 Physician Fee Schedule rule in July 2026. For remote monitoring, it proposes to pay RTM and remote physiologic monitoring only when the services are furnished by clinical staff the billing practice directly employs, to limit RTM to established patients, to require a separately reportable initiating visit where the service is discussed for both RTM and remote physiologic monitoring, and to change how the set-up, device and management codes are valued, with a request for comment on replacing the 17 remote monitoring codes with four new monthly codes. The employed-staff proposal is limited to RPM and RTM; it does not change the rules for contracted CCM and PCM staff. The rule also asks questions about supervision of care management staff and proposes health coaching codes (0591T to 0593T) for certified coaches. As of November 3, 2026 we could find no final rule; last year’s was published November 5.

Plan for the proposals without acting on them as if they were final. Add RTM discussion to the enrollment visit note now, because it costs nothing and fits the proposal. Review contracts with any outside team that does your RTM work. Keep contracted-staff RTM revenue out of next year’s budget until the rule is final, and treat any 2027 payment figures as provisional because the proposed conversion factor is lower than 2026’s. We will update this guide when it is published.

Where audits look

The OIG has audited both chronic care management and remote monitoring, and the same themes repeat. Patients with no history at the practice. More than one practitioner billing the same service for the same patient in a month. Staff time that does not match the notes. Routine waiver of copays, which can violate the beneficiary inducement law. Outside vendors paid per patient or as a share of collections. And, for programs that involve a dietitian, nutrition counseling logged as care management when Medicare treats nutrition therapy as a separate benefit limited to diabetes and kidney disease. Care management compliance covers each with what to do about it.

A monthly workflow, step by step

  1. Pick the right patientAn existing patient of the billing practitioner with a qualifying condition. Check for another practitioner billing CCM. Exclude patients on payers that do not cover the service.
  2. Check benefitsFor every patient except Original Medicare. Ask about each code, prior authorization, frequency or device limits and cost sharing, and record the reference number.
  3. Enroll at the office visitThe billing practitioner discusses the service, documents consent and chooses CCM or PCM with the reason. For RTM, a separate consent and an order stating the condition and what the data will guide.
  4. Build the care planMeasurable goals, symptom and medication management, interventions, coordination and a review date. The practitioner approves it and the patient gets a copy.
  5. Do and log the workCalls, clinically meaningful messages, plan updates and coordination, each logged with minutes and credentials. RTM data reviewed weekly and a live call each month.
  6. SupervisePractitioner review of plan changes, an escalation path that works, and written protocols where state law requires them.
  7. Close the monthTotal minutes by code family, apply the code selection table, drop months without patient contact or without the RTM live call, check payer rules and conflicting codes, and have the practitioner attest to oversight.
Pro tip

Close the month in the first five business days of the next one, while the staff who did the work still remember it. A month closed late is where minutes get rounded up and notes get written from memory, which is exactly what an audit finds.

Do and don’t

Do
  • Enroll existing patients at an in-person visit and document consent first.
  • Check for another practitioner billing CCM before you choose CCM.
  • Log every minute with date, staff, credential and activity.
  • Use licensed nurses for RTM data review and the live call.
  • Make the care plan patient-specific, with named measures and target dates.
  • Run a benefits check for every patient except Original Medicare.
Don't
  • Don’t round minutes up or count the same minute toward two codes.
  • Don’t count non-clinical staff time, automated messages or time before consent.
  • Don’t bill RTM for food or general wellness data.
  • Don’t describe the program as covered by insurance or waive cost sharing routinely.
  • Don’t clone notes across patients.
  • Don’t let unlicensed staff interpret data or change the plan.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between CCM and PCM?

Chronic care management is for patients with two or more chronic conditions and covers a comprehensive care plan; only one practitioner can bill it per patient per month. Principal care management is for one serious, high-risk condition and was designed for specialists, so a specialist can bill PCM even when another practitioner bills CCM for the same patient. The same practitioner cannot bill both for one patient in the same month.

Can we bill RTM together with CCM or PCM in the same month?

Yes. Medicare allows RTM with CCM or PCM as long as the time is separate: a minute logged toward CCM or PCM cannot also count toward an RTM management code. RTM cannot be billed in the same period as remote physiologic monitoring, and only one practitioner can bill RTM device codes in a 30-day period.

Does a dietitian count as clinical staff?

In its CCM guidance, CMS names registered dietitians among the clinical staff who can take part under general supervision when incident-to rules are met. What they log has to be care management work tied to the care plan, such as setting goals, checking adherence, solving barriers and coordinating. Detailed meal planning looks like medical nutrition therapy, which Medicare covers separately for diabetes and kidney disease only, and it should not be logged as care management.

Does the patient have to come in for a visit before enrollment?

For CCM, an initiating visit is required for new patients and for patients the billing practitioner has not seen in the past year. For PCM, the same applies and the visit repeats after one year. A regular office visit that the practice already bills qualifies if the program is discussed there, so a separate enrollment visit is not needed.

Is a monthly phone call required for CCM or PCM?

No. Medicare does not require a monthly call for CCM or PCM, and care plan review with secure messaging can support the codes. Our recommended practice is to bill only months with at least one documented patient contact, either a call or a two-way message exchange. RTM management is different: it requires at least one live phone or video interaction each month.

Which payers should we not bill?

Per their own published policies, Cigna excludes remote therapeutic monitoring for every indication, and HealthSpring (Medicare Advantage) and Oscar do not pay CCM separately. Aetna’s remote monitoring policy changed in 2026, so get written confirmation of RTM, CCM and PCM benefits for each Aetna patient before enrolling. Medicaid is outside this guide and should be excluded unless your state program covers it. Always run a benefits check before enrollment.

How much do these services pay?

Medicare’s 2026 national amounts range from roughly $22 to $144 per code depending on the service. A typical first month for a patient on PCM plus RTM comes to about $195 and later months $119 to $146, before the patient’s 20% share. Amounts vary by locality and payer; the detail page has the table.

What happens if CMS finalizes the 2027 proposals?

If the final rule keeps the proposal, RTM staff would have to be the billing practice’s direct employees, which would end contracted-staff RTM, and RTM would require an established patient and an initiating visit where the service is discussed. Payment for the set-up and device codes would fall. Practices using outside teams for RTM should review contracts and plan for the change.

Do you run care management programs for practices?

We support practices with benefits checks, enrollment workflows, time-log and documentation templates, month-end close and claim submission, working inside the practice’s own EHR. The practitioner remains the billing provider and supervises the work.
