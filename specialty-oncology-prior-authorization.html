Oncology prior authorization: drug regimens, imaging, and treatment pathways.

No specialty carries a heavier prior-authorization load than oncology, because almost every lever a payer has — step therapy, clinical pathways, imaging necessity, radiation modality review — gets pulled on the same patient inside a single course of treatment. This guide covers how drug-level prior auth and step therapy actually work against the NCCN Compendium, how payer pathways programs change the calculus, what gets a PET scan or advanced radiation therapy approved, how to win a peer-to-peer review, and exactly what CMS-0057-F does and does not change about the clock.

Reviewed by Phares A., MD · Physiotherapist, CPC (AAPC) · Part of our oncology billing and coding guide

Key takeaways

  • Almost every infused or injected cancer drug requires prior authorization from commercial and Medicare Advantage payers, and most payers review the whole regimen against NCCN Guidelines rather than clearing each drug in isolation.
  • Clinical pathways programs are a real, separate lane from ordinary step therapy. Vendors and payer-run programs (AIM/Anthem Cancer Care Quality Program, New Century Health, eviCore, Value Pathways powered by NCCN) pre-clear NCCN-concordant regimens fast; stepping off the pathway routes the request into slower individual review.
  • CMS-0057-F's 7-day/72-hour clock explicitly excludes drug prior authorizations — and it binds only Medicare Advantage, Medicaid/CHIP FFS and managed care, and ACA exchange plans, not traditional Medicare or most employer-sponsored commercial coverage.
  • PET denials are almost always a framing problem, not a medical-necessity one — the request has to name the management decision the scan will change, not just confirm a diagnosis already on the chart.

Why oncology carries the heaviest prior-authorization load in medicine

Every other specialty in this series deals with one or two prior-authorization triggers — a biologic, an imaging study, a procedure. Oncology stacks all of them onto the same patient inside a single episode of care: the drug regimen, the staging and restaging imaging around it, and, for a large share of solid tumors, radiation therapy on top. Nearly every physician-administered chemotherapy agent, targeted small molecule, and immunotherapy checkpoint inhibitor sits on a commercial or Medicare Advantage prior-authorization list, and payers increasingly review the entire planned regimen — drug, dose, cycle length, and expected duration — as one request rather than authorizing agents individually.

Step therapy in oncology works the same way it does elsewhere — try the covered, usually lower-cost regimen first and document why it failed or wasn't tolerated before moving to the next line — but the reviewer's real question is narrower: does the requested regimen match a category 1, 2A, or 2B recommendation in the NCCN Clinical Practice Guidelines for that cancer type, stage, and line of therapy. A regimen that matches loses most of its step-therapy friction regardless of cost; a regimen that departs from NCCN-listed options, even for a clinically sound reason, draws the slowest, most document-heavy review in the system.

The NCCN Compendium and payer pathways: two different systems for the same question

Two NCCN-branded mechanisms shape oncology prior authorization, and they answer different questions. Confusing them is the most common documentation mistake in an oncology PA submission.

  • 1The NCCN Drugs & Biologics Compendium settles off-label medical necessity. Under the 1993 Omnibus Budget Reconciliation Act, Medicare is required to cover off-label anticancer drug uses that appear in an approved compendium. CMS recognizes the NCCN Drugs & Biologics Compendium for this purpose: an indication the Compendium lists as "Recommended" is treated as a medically accepted indication for coverage, while one listed "Not Recommended" is not. This is the citation that makes an off-label regimen billable at all, separate from whether it gets pre-approved quickly.
  • 2Payer clinical pathways programs settle how fast the authorization moves. Pathways are vendor- or payer-run programs — the Anthem/AIM Cancer Care Quality Program, New Century Health, eviCore's oncology pathways, and Value Pathways powered by NCCN among them — that pre-define a menu of regimens matching NCCN category 1, 2A, or 2B recommendations or an FDA-approved indication. A regimen entered as pathway-concordant can clear authorization in minutes through an EHR-integrated tool instead of routing to a multi-day individual medical-necessity review.

The practical effect: ordering a pathway-listed regimen for a common presentation is usually the fastest path through prior authorization available anywhere in oncology billing. Choosing a clinically justified but off-pathway regimen — a reasonable and common scenario given comorbidities, prior toxicity, or biomarker nuance — is not wrong, but it forecasts a slower review and should be submitted with the same documentation a peer-to-peer call would need: the specific reason the pathway option isn't appropriate for this patient, not just the alternative regimen's name.

Pro tip

Before submitting a regimen, check whether the payer runs a named pathways program (it's usually disclosed in the oncology section of the provider portal or medical policy). If it does, cite the specific pathway name and regimen ID in the submission, not just "per NCCN guidelines" — a generic NCCN citation still routes to standard review, while a matched pathway ID is what triggers the fast-track queue.

Imaging prior authorization: what actually gets a PET scan approved

PET and PET/CT for oncology staging, restaging, and treatment-response assessment commonly requires prior authorization from commercial payers and Medicare Advantage plans; traditional fee-for-service Medicare does not require prior authorization for PET, though coverage still runs against National Coverage Determination 220.6.17's clinical criteria. The single fact that decides most PET authorization outcomes is whether the request shows the scan will change management — not simply confirm what's already known.

PET/CT scenarios in oncology and the medical-necessity framing that typically supports authorization. Confirm against the specific payer's current imaging policy and, for traditional Medicare, NCD 220.6.17 and the local MAC's coverage article.
Clinical scenarioWhat the request needs to show
Initial stagingPET result will determine the stage assigned and, with it, the treatment approach (e.g., ruling in or out distant metastasis that would change a curative-intent plan to a palliative one)
Restaging after treatment completionAssessing for residual disease or determining extent of a known recurrence — not a routine surveillance scan without a specific clinical trigger
Suspected recurrenceSigns, symptoms, or a rising tumor marker that raise clinical suspicion, distinguishing the request from surveillance imaging
Treatment-strategy / subsequent-therapy monitoringThe scan is guiding a specific decision about continuing, changing, or stopping the current antitumor strategy, framed as a management decision rather than routine follow-up

A request that reads "patient has [cancer type], please authorize PET for staging" without naming the decision the result will resolve is the most common reason an oncology imaging authorization is denied or sent back for more information. The fix is almost always the same: state explicitly what changes in the treatment plan depending on the result.

Radiation therapy prior authorization: the modality decides the burden

Standard fractionated external-beam radiation therapy for common indications frequently moves through with routine or no prior authorization at many payers, because it's a well-established, lower-cost modality. Advanced and higher-cost modalities are a different story.

Peer-to-peer review: what actually turns a denial around

A peer-to-peer review puts the ordering oncologist on the phone with the payer's medical director, and it succeeds or fails on specificity. Radiation oncology data illustrates the stakes: surveyed radiation oncologists report a total time burden of roughly 92 to 95 minutes per prior-authorization event when a peer-to-peer discussion is required, and one academic-center study found roughly 85% of services under review at radiotherapy centers were ultimately approved — meaning the process usually delays rather than denies, which makes the speed of the peer-to-peer call itself the thing worth optimizing.

What wins the call
  • Naming the NCCN category (1, 2A, or 2B) and version date for the exact regimen requested, not a general reference to "NCCN guidelines."
  • Listing each prior line of therapy by drug, dates, and the specific reason it stopped — progression, intolerance, or contraindication.
  • Citing the biomarker result (HER2, PD-L1, MSI-H/dMMR, EGFR, and similar) with the date and assay when the requested drug's indication depends on it.
What wastes the call
  • Restating the diagnosis and general treatment rationale the medical director already has in the file.
  • Referring to "failed prior therapy" as a category instead of naming the drug, the dates, and the objective evidence of failure.
  • Treating the call as a formality rather than preparing the same level of detail the original written submission needed.

What CMS-0057-F actually changes — and what it doesn't

The CMS Interoperability and Prior Authorization Final Rule (CMS-0057-F) is frequently cited as a fix for oncology's prior-authorization burden. Its scope is real but narrower than the shorthand version suggests.

CMS-0057-F prior authorization decision timeframes and scope (CMS fact sheet, January 17, 2024).
ElementWhat the rule requires
Payers bound by the ruleMedicare Advantage organizations, state Medicaid and CHIP fee-for-service programs, Medicaid managed care plans, CHIP managed care entities, and ACA exchange Qualified Health Plan issuers on the Federally Facilitated Exchanges
Standard decision timeframe7 calendar days
Expedited (urgent) decision timeframe72 hours
Scope exclusion that matters most for oncologyThe rule's decision-timeframe requirement does not apply to prior authorization decisions for drugs
Compliance date for the decision-timeframe and denial-reason requirementsJanuary 1, 2026
Not covered by this rule at allTraditional fee-for-service Medicare; most employer-sponsored commercial plans; QHP issuers are excluded specifically from the decision-timeframe requirement

That drug exclusion is the detail that matters most for an oncology billing operation: the majority of what makes oncology prior authorization slow — the chemotherapy regimen, the targeted therapy, the checkpoint inhibitor — is a drug authorization, and CMS-0057-F's federal 7-day/72-hour clock does not bind it, even at a Medicare Advantage plan the rule otherwise covers. Where the rule does help oncology directly is on the non-drug side of the episode: PET and advanced imaging authorizations and, at the payer types the rule covers, some radiation therapy authorizations fall under "items and services" and do get the 7-day/72-hour deadline starting January 1, 2026. The rule also requires a specific, stated reason for any denial (excluding drug denials) beginning in 2026, which is useful documentation for an appeal regardless of how fast the decision arrived.

From order to infusion: a worked prior authorization timeline

A typical, non-emergent regimen moves through several checkpoints before the first infusion, and each one has its own clock.

  • 1Regimen ordered and staged for submission. The oncologist selects the regimen; staff check it against the payer's pathway list and prior-authorization requirements before submitting, attaching staging, pathology, and any required biomarker results up front to avoid a first-pass information request.
  • 2Prior authorization submitted. Submission channel (payer portal, fax, or a pathways vendor's EHR-integrated tool) affects speed as much as the clinical content does — a pathway-matched regimen submitted through an integrated tool can return a decision in minutes; the same regimen faxed to a general utilization-management queue does not.
  • 3Typical turnaround. Reported experience varies widely by payer and pathway status: the AMA's 2024 physician survey found an average prior-authorization wait of about 3 days across all specialties, with 31% of physicians reporting waits beyond a week; ASCO-reported surveys of oncology and radiation oncology practices found delays exceeding one business day in a large share of cases, and more than half of radiation oncology delays running five days or longer. A pathway-concordant drug regimen is typically at the fast end of that range; an off-pathway regimen, a step-therapy exception request, or a radiation modality requiring medical-director review is typically at the slow end.
  • 4If treatment can't wait: the expedited pathway. When a delay would seriously jeopardize the patient's life, health, or ability to regain maximum function, an expedited (urgent) request applies. At payers bound by CMS-0057-F, an expedited non-drug request must be decided within 72 hours; Medicare Advantage plans separately owe a 72-hour decision on standard Part B drug determinations under 42 CFR § 422.568, a deadline that cannot be extended even for missing records. Outside those specific frameworks, "expedited" review timing is set by the individual payer's own policy — confirm it rather than assuming a uniform federal floor applies.
  • 5Denial and peer-to-peer, if needed. A denial should arrive with a specific reason (required from impacted payers beginning 2026 for non-drug items). The peer-to-peer call, prepared with the NCCN category, prior-line detail, and biomarker documentation described above, is typically the fastest route to overturning a denial without a full formal appeal.

Frequently asked questions

Do all chemotherapy and biologic cancer drugs require prior authorization?

Most, but not all. Nearly every infused or injected chemotherapy agent, targeted therapy, and immunotherapy on a commercial or Medicare Advantage formulary carries a prior-authorization requirement, and payers commonly review the entire regimen at once rather than drug by drug. A small number of long-established generic cytotoxic agents used within a widely accepted NCCN-recommended regimen sometimes bypass individual review, but the safest operating assumption for a new regimen is that authorization is required until the payer's own prior-authorization list confirms otherwise.

Does the CMS-0057-F prior authorization rule speed up oncology drug approvals?

Not directly. CMS-0057-F requires Medicare Advantage plans, Medicaid and CHIP fee-for-service and managed care programs, and ACA exchange qualified health plans to decide standard prior authorization requests within 7 calendar days and expedited requests within 72 hours, starting January 1, 2026 — but the rule explicitly excludes prior authorization decisions for drugs from that timeline requirement. It also does not bind traditional fee-for-service Medicare or most employer-sponsored commercial plans. A chemotherapy or biologic drug authorization can still run on whatever timeline the specific payer's own policy sets, even at a plan CMS-0057-F otherwise covers.

What's the difference between a payer's oncology clinical pathways program and ordinary step therapy?

Step therapy requires trying a specified lower-cost or preferred drug first and documenting failure or intolerance before a subsequent drug is approved. A clinical pathways program instead pre-screens a defined menu of regimens — built from NCCN Guidelines categories 1, 2A, or 2B and FDA-approved indications — and grants faster, often near-automatic authorization to any regimen that matches the pathway. Ordering a pathway-concordant regimen up front can avoid a step-therapy sequence entirely; stepping outside the pathway usually triggers the slower, individually reviewed authorization route instead.

Why does a PET scan get denied even when the patient clearly has cancer?

Because most payers, following Medicare's own coverage framework, don't authorize a PET scan simply to confirm that a known cancer exists — the request has to show the result will change what happens next: whether it changes the stage assigned, whether it changes the treatment plan between two clinically reasonable options, or whether it's answering a specific question conventional CT or MRI already left open. A request that states a diagnosis and asks for staging without naming the management decision the scan will resolve is the single most common reason an oncology imaging authorization gets sent back or denied outright.
