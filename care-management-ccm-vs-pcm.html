CCM or PCM: how to choose the right care management service for each patient.

Chronic care management and principal care management look alike on a claim and are very different in an audit. Choosing the wrong one, or choosing both, is the most common avoidable error in care management billing. The choice comes down to the patient’s conditions, who else is billing, and what the billing practitioner can document.

Key takeaways

  • CCM is for two or more conditions; PCM is for one. CCM needs conditions expected to last at least 12 months. PCM needs one condition expected to last at least 3 months that is high risk and complex to manage.
  • Only one practitioner can bill CCM per patient per month. A specialist can still bill PCM for a condition they manage even when a primary care practitioner bills CCM.
  • The same practitioner cannot bill CCM and PCM for the same patient in the same month. Pick one and document why.
  • For a specialist, PCM is the default. It is the service CMS designed for them, and it avoids the one-biller-per-month conflict that makes CCM fragile.
  • The decision belongs to the billing practitioner and goes in the visit note with the reason.

What each service requires

Both services pay for clinical staff and practitioner time spent on a patient between visits, and both need a care plan, consent and general supervision. They differ in who qualifies and in what the care plan has to cover.

Eligibility and structure, side by side.
Chronic care management (CCM)Principal care management (PCM)

The complexity test is the part of PCM that practices skip. A patient with a single stable condition does not qualify just because the condition is chronic. The note has to say why this patient is at risk and why the management is complex, with facts specific to the patient: medication changes in the past six months, an emergency visit for pain, a functional decline, a comorbidity that complicates treatment.

PCM during the global period of a procedure by the same practitioner is also off the table. That matters in pain management, where 10-day and 90-day global procedures such as spinal cord stimulator implants are common: check the global period before you open a PCM month for the same practitioner.

A decision rule the billing practitioner can document

  1. Count the chronic conditions. Two or more, each expected to last at least 12 months and placing the patient at significant risk? That points to CCM.
  2. Check for another CCM biller. Ask the patient, check claims history and your payer portal. If someone else bills CCM, CCM is not available to you this month.
  3. Check the payer. Some plans do not pay CCM separately. If the payer does not pay CCM, PCM may still be payable after a benefits check.
  4. Otherwise use PCM. One serious, complex condition with documented risk and complexity.
  5. Record the choice and the reason in the enrollment visit note, and name the one practitioner who is the billing and supervising provider.

A worked example with a hypothetical patient. A 64-year-old patient has postlaminectomy syndrome and chronic lumbar radiculopathy and sees a pain practitioner every six weeks. Her primary care practitioner bills CCM for her diabetes, obesity and hypertension. The pain practitioner cannot bill CCM, because another practitioner already does. The pain practitioner can bill PCM for the pain condition, with a disease-specific plan, because the condition is high risk (two medication changes this year, an emergency visit for pain in the spring) and complicated by obesity. The two plans are separate, and neither practitioner’s staff time counts toward the other’s code.

A second hypothetical example. A patient with fibromyalgia, type 2 diabetes, obesity and insomnia, with no other practitioner billing CCM, may qualify for CCM if the chart shows each condition is expected to last at least 12 months and places the patient at significant risk. The same patient could also be enrolled in PCM by a pain practitioner if a primary care practitioner bills CCM instead. The chart, not the diagnosis list, decides.

Why PCM is the default for specialists

Three reasons. First, PCM was designed for a practitioner who manages one condition in depth, which is how a pain, rheumatology or neurology practice works. Second, PCM does not collide with a primary care practitioner’s CCM, so there is no month in which the practice finds out after the fact that it cannot be paid. Third, the care plan is narrower and easier to keep patient-specific, which is what an audit looks for.

CCM remains the right answer when the practice is the patient’s medical home in practice if not in name, the patient truly has several qualifying conditions and nobody else bills it. Even then, the OIG has audited CCM specifically, including overpayments where more than one practitioner billed the same patient in the same month, so the check for another biller is not optional.

Pro tip

Add a standing question to enrollment intake: “Does any other doctor’s office call you each month to check on your health between visits?” A yes does not always mean CCM, but it is cheaper to find out before the first claim than after the denial.

Mistakes that turn into denials or recoupments

  • Billing both services in the same month. The second claim denies or, worse, pays and is recouped later.
  • Choosing CCM because it is familiar, then finding another practitioner bills it.
  • Enrolling a patient with one stable condition in PCM without documenting risk and complexity.
  • Using the referring doctor’s NPI. For incident-to services the claim must carry the practitioner who supervised the staff. A referring or ordering doctor who does not supervise cannot bill.
  • Skipping the repeat initiating visit. PCM needs a new one after one year; CCM needs one when the billing practitioner has not seen the patient in the past year.

Frequently asked questions

Can a specialist bill CCM?

Yes, if the patient has two or more qualifying chronic conditions, no other practitioner bills CCM that month, and the specialist is the practitioner supervising the staff. In practice, specialists usually do better with PCM, which does not conflict with another practitioner’s CCM.

Can a patient be in CCM with one practice and PCM with another?

Yes. Medicare allows CCM by one practitioner and PCM by another for the same patient in the same month, for different conditions, with two care plans. The time for each is separate.

What does the PCM risk and complexity test mean in practice?

The note has to show, for this patient, why the one condition places them at significant risk and why management is complex. Facts that work: frequent medication changes, an emergency or urgent visit for the condition, functional decline, risk of exacerbation, or a comorbidity that complicates treatment. Generic statements that apply to every patient with the diagnosis do not.

How long does a patient stay enrolled?

Until they stop. Consent is needed once, unless the patient changes billing practitioner, and the patient may stop at any time effective at the end of the month. The initiating visit has to be repeated after one year for PCM, and for CCM when the billing practitioner has not seen the patient in the past year.

How do we find out whether another practitioner bills CCM?

Ask the patient at enrollment, review claims history if your payer or clearinghouse shows it, and check eligibility responses where the payer returns care management information. There is no single Medicare lookup that is complete, which is why the question belongs in the intake script.

Do these services apply to Medicare Advantage patients?

Often, but not always. Many Medicare Advantage plans follow Medicare on these codes, and some do not pay CCM at all. A benefits check before enrollment settles it for each patient.
