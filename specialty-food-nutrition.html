Nutrition and dietetics billing and coding: the complete 2026 guide.

Nutrition and dietetics runs on a benefit structure most billers never fully map out: two Medicare-covered diagnoses, provider-status rules that flip direction between code families performed by the same clinician, and a program-level accreditation requirement most credentialing teams have never touched. This guide covers the full revenue cycle — MNT, DSMT, and obesity IBT code families, the exact statute and regulation behind Medicare's hour limits, and the four gaps we found nobody else publishing in this niche covers.

Reviewed by Phares A., MD · Physiotherapist, CPC (AAPC) · Updated August 2026

Key takeaways

  • Medicare's MNT hour limits have a citable legal source. NCD 180.1 (Pub. 100-3), Social Security Act §1861(s)(2)(V), and 42 CFR 410.130–410.134 set the 3-hour/2-hour structure and the same-date MNT/DSMT restriction — and almost no appeal we've seen actually cites them.
  • "Incident-to" points in opposite directions inside the same specialty. MNT (97802-97804) is barred from incident-to billing by regulation. Obesity IBT (G0447/G0473) furnished by an RD generally must be billed incident-to a physician. One dietitian, one clinic, two opposite rules.
  • DSMT can't be billed off a standard RDN payer contract. The program itself needs ADA recognition or ADCES/DEAP accreditation before a single claim goes out — a materially slower process than credentialing an individual dietitian.
  • Nutrition bundling comes from three different rule sources, not one. The NCD's same-date restriction, NCCI PTP edits against health-behavior codes, and payer medical policy each deny differently — and only some are appealable.

Nutrition and dietetics billing at a glance

Four structural problems drive most of this specialty's denial volume. Medicare covers MNT for exactly two diagnosis categories — diabetes and renal disease — and nothing else, no matter how clinically appropriate the counseling was. Incident-to status flips depending on the code family: barred for MNT, generally required for obesity IBT delivered by an RD. DSMT is accredited at the program level, not credentialed at the individual level. And every code in the specialty is time-unit billed with no E/M equivalent to fall back on, which means a documentation gap is a units gap, not just a leveling problem. Here's the shape of it before the detail.

What changed in 2026

Billing-industry sources report that effective August 3, 2026, Medicare claims for CPT 97802, 97803, and HCPCS G0270/G0271 deny outright when submitted without a qualifying diagnosis attached to the claim line. ⚠️ This build could not confirm the edit directly against a CMS transmittal, MLN Matters article, or MAC bulletin — the reporting traces to payer-communication channels (Community First Health Plans, April 2026), not a primary CMS source this build could open. Treat it as vendor-reported until your own MAC confirms it, but plan for it operationally either way: it converts what used to be a soft medical-necessity risk into a hard front-end rejection, so a diagnosis-validation step belongs in your scrubber before the date, not after the first denial batch lands.

On rates: Medicare Physician Fee Schedule payment for MNT, DSMT, and IBT codes is locality-specific and updates annually. No dollar figures are published in this guide for that reason — pull your own locality's current-year rate from the CMS PFS Look-Up Tool by name rather than trusting a number published here or anywhere else, because a rate that's right for one locality and year is wrong for the next.

On telehealth: post-COVID Medicare flexibilities for MNT and DSMT delivered via synchronous audio-video have been extended in successive legislation, most recently reported through December 31, 2027, with a separate hospital-outpatient carve-out for remote MNT/DSMT furnished to a beneficiary's home. ⚠️ Telehealth flexibility status is a moving target that has already been extended multiple times past its original sunset — confirm the current expiration date against your MAC's telehealth page before building a permanent workflow around it. How nutrition and dietetics billing actually works: time-based units and provider status

Nutrition and dietetics has no E/M equivalent. Every core code family is billed in discrete time units against a defined benefit structure, which means the entire specialty runs on two variables billers in other specialties rarely have to track this closely: unit precision and provider status.

MNT (97802-97804, G0270/G0271) bills in 15-minute individual units and 30-minute group units. DSMT (G0108/G0109) and group MNT both bill in 30-minute increments. Obesity IBT (G0447 individual, G0473 group) splits the same way — 15 minutes individual, 30 minutes group. The 8-minute rule governs partial units across all of them: a unit is billable once at least 8 minutes of the code's time increment has elapsed, so a 22-minute individual MNT encounter bills as one unit (15 minutes) plus a second partial unit only if at least 8 of the remaining 7 minutes were documented — in practice, 22 minutes bills as one unit, and the next full or partial unit needs its own 8+ minutes to count. Round down, not up, and document start and stop times or total face-to-face minutes on every encounter; a units-billed number with no supporting time documentation is an audit finding waiting to happen.

Provider status is where this specialty gets genuinely unusual. MNT and DSMT are RDN-specific benefits: a registered dietitian or nutrition professional bills them directly under their own NPI, and by regulation they cannot be billed incident-to a physician. Obesity IBT runs the opposite direction. NCD 210.12 defines the covered service as counseling furnished by a qualified primary care physician or NPP in a primary care setting; when a registered dietitian delivers that counseling, industry billing guidance treats it as billable only incident-to that physician or NPP, in a non-facility setting, under the supervising provider's NPI. Same dietitian, same building, opposite billing status depending on which HCPCS family is on the claim — and getting it backward in either direction is a real, common, and preventable error.

Medical Nutrition Therapy: CPT 97802-97804 and the reassessment codes

MNT is the specialty's highest-volume code family and the one with the most explicit statutory backing of any nutrition benefit. Coverage runs through Section 1861(s)(2)(V) of the Social Security Act, implementing regulations at 42 CFR 410.130–410.134, and NCD 180.1 (Pub. 100-3, Chapter 180, effective 01/01/2022) — verified directly against the CMS Coverage Database for this build, not paraphrased from a secondary source.

97803Reassessment and intervention, individual, established patient15 min

NCD 180.1's coverage text, quoted directly from the CMS Coverage Database: basic MNT coverage in the first year a beneficiary receives it, for either diagnosis category, is three hours; basic coverage in subsequent years is two hours. Pursuant to the exception at 42 CFR 410.132(b)(5), additional hours beyond that cap are covered when the treating physician determines a change in medical condition, diagnosis, or treatment regimen requires additional MNT and orders the additional hours during that episode of care — that's the regulatory basis for G0270/G0271, and it's the citation to put in an appeal when a payer denies units past the 3-hour or 2-hour mark without checking whether a qualifying physician order for additional hours is on file.

97802 is a one-time code per condition per referral year, not a code re-billed at every first visit of a calendar year; a patient re-referred more than 12 months after their last episode of MNT care can generally trigger a new initial assessment, but restarting 97802 mid-episode because the calendar flipped is a documentation and coverage error, not a billing option. The referral itself has to come from a physician as defined at Section 1861(r)(1) of the Social Security Act — effective January 1, 2022, when 42 CFR 410.130 and 410.132 were updated to align with the statute, NP and PA referrals stopped qualifying for MNT coverage, a detail that trips up front-desk referral intake constantly because NPs and PAs order almost everything else in a practice.

The renal-disease side of MNT has a boundary most guides miss entirely: 42 CFR 410.130's definition of "renal disease" for MNT purposes is chronic renal insufficiency, or end-stage renal disease when dialysis is not received, or the beneficiary's condition for 36 months following a kidney transplant. A patient already receiving dialysis is generally excluded from the MNT benefit on that basis, because ESRD facility nutrition counseling is bundled into the ESRD facility's own payment structure instead. Billing MNT against a dialysis-dependence diagnosis alone, without transplant status or pre-dialysis CKD staging supporting it, denies for medical necessity and shouldn't be appealed — it should be corrected. Diabetes Self-Management Training: G0108 and G0109

DSMT is where this specialty's credentialing story diverges hardest from a typical RDN buildout, and it's the gap every competitor guide we reviewed skips.

DSMT code family and hour structure, per NCD 40.1 and 42 CFR 410.140–410.146.
CodeServiceUnit
G0108DSMT, individual30 min
G0109DSMT, group (2 or more)30 min

Initial-year coverage is 10 hours, deliverable in any combination of 30-minute units across a 12-month period, with a hard structural rule inside it: only one of those 10 hours is payable as individual training (G0108) — the remaining nine hours are furnished in a group setting (G0109) unless a documented exception applies (no group session available within a reasonable time, or the beneficiary has special needs a group format can't accommodate). Follow-up years cover 2 hours, billable as individual or group. Note the regulatory citation is different from MNT's: DSMT sits at 42 CFR 410.140–410.146, not 410.130–410.134 — treating them as the same regulatory family is a citation error that undermines an appeal letter.

The accreditation requirement is the part that changes the credentialing timeline entirely. A DSMT program cannot enroll and bill Medicare simply because it employs a Medicare-enrolled dietitian. The training program itself has to hold accreditation from one of the two CMS-approved national accrediting bodies — the American Diabetes Association, which grants "recognition" through its Education Recognition Program (ERP), or the Association of Diabetes Care & Education Specialists (ADCES), which grants accreditation through its Diabetes Education Accreditation Program (DEAP) — both against the National Standards for Diabetes Self-Management Education and Support (NSDSMES), and both approved by CMS on six-year terms. DSMT is billed under Medicare specialty code 71 (dietitians/nutritionists, in effect since 2002), with enrollment completed via CMS-855I, but the accreditation step sits in front of enrollment and routinely takes months, not weeks — budget for it as a separate project from individual RDN credentialing, not a line item inside it. The full accreditation pathway, timeline, and enrollment checklist is in our Diabetes Self-Management Training billing: G0108, G0109, and program accreditation guide.

MNT and DSMT interact on the same claim set constantly for a diabetic patient receiving both benefits, and NCD 180.1 is explicit about the restriction: Medicare covers both DSMT and MNT, initial and subsequent years, without decreasing either benefit's hours, as long as the two are not furnished on the same date of service. That's a coverage rule, not an NCCI edit, and no modifier overrides it — the only fix is scheduling MNT and DSMT encounters on separate calendar dates.

Intensive Behavioral Therapy for obesity: G0447 and G0473, and the incident-to trap

IBT for obesity is a preventive-services benefit under NCD 210.12, not a dietitian-specific benefit under Part 410 Subpart G the way MNT and DSMT are — and that distinction is exactly why the provider-status rule reverses here.

Obesity IBT code family and visit schedule, per NCD 210.12 (effective 11/29/2011).
CodeServiceUnit
G0447Face-to-face behavioral counseling for obesity, individual15 min
G0473Face-to-face behavioral counseling for obesity, group of 2–1030 min

Eligibility requires BMI ≥ 30 kg/m², documented from measured height and weight, not self-report. The visit schedule NCD 210.12 lays out is tiered and contingent: one face-to-face visit weekly for month 1, one every other week for months 2–6, then one monthly for months 7–12 — but only if the beneficiary has lost at least 3 kg over the first six months, reassessed and documented at the six-month visit. A patient who hasn't hit the 3 kg threshold isn't automatically cut off; the NCD calls for a reassessment of readiness to change and BMI after an additional six-month period instead of automatic continuation into the monthly phase. Medicare pays for up to 22 combined G0447/G0473 visits in a rolling 12-month period.

Here's the trap: NCD 210.12 defines the qualified furnishing provider as "a qualified primary care physician or other primary care practitioner," in a primary care setting the NCD explicitly defines to exclude emergency departments, inpatient hospital settings, ambulatory surgical centers, independent diagnostic testing facilities, skilled nursing facilities, inpatient rehabilitation facilities, and hospices. A registered dietitian is not one of the primary care physician or practitioner types the NCD names. Industry billing guidance is consistent on the resulting mechanic: when an RD furnishes the counseling, it's billed incident-to a qualifying physician or NPP, in a non-facility primary care setting, under that supervising provider's NPI — meeting the standard incident-to conditions (direct supervision, an established plan of care, the supervising provider actively involved in the patient's care) on top of it.

That's the reverse of MNT two sections up, where incident-to billing is regulatorily barred outright. A practice running both benefits out of the same clinic, with the same dietitian delivering both, needs two different billing configurations for two code families performed by the same person in the same building — get it backward in either direction and the claim is billed under the wrong provider entirely, which is a compliance problem, not just a reimbursement one. Full documentation requirements, the 3 kg reassessment mechanics, and worked incident-to billing scenarios are in our Billing Intensive Behavioral Therapy for obesity: G0447, G0473, and the incident-to requirement guide.

Modifiers

Nutrition and dietetics leans on a smaller modifier set than a procedural specialty, but getting the wrong one on the claim still denies it or mis-prices it.

95
  • Synchronous audio-video telehealth. Required by most commercial and Medicare Advantage payers regardless of place-of-service coding.
  • Generally not required for Medicare FFS, which identifies telehealth through POS 02/10 instead — confirm your MAC's current position before dropping it.
93
  • Audio-only telehealth. Payer-dependent whether it's accepted at all for nutrition counseling codes; several MACs and commercial plans exclude MNT/DSMT from audio-only coverage entirely.
25
  • Same-day, significant, separately identifiable E/M. Rarely relevant to RDN billing directly, since RDNs don't bill E/M codes — but relevant when a supervising physician bills an E/M the same day as an incident-to IBT visit.
59 / XE, XS, XP, XU without documentation
  • Relevant when MNT and a health-behavior assessment/intervention code (96156-series) are billed the same date. Only defensible when the record shows a genuinely distinct service — not applied routinely to force a claim through an edit.

The modifier logic that trips up nutrition claims most isn't any single modifier in isolation, it's stacking the wrong assumption about which one a given payer actually wants: Medicare FFS's POS-driven telehealth identification doesn't transfer to a commercial payer that still expects 95 on every virtual visit regardless of POS code. Build the modifier logic per payer, not as one universal rule. The complete modifier reference and decision tree is in our Nutrition and dietetics modifiers: 95, 93, 25, 59, and the X-modifiers explained guide.

NCCI edits and same-day bundling restrictions

This is where nutrition billing gets decided, and it's the section every competitor guide we reviewed flattens into a single line — "bill one MNT code per date of service" — without separating what are actually three distinct rule sources with three different origins and three different appeal paths.

  • 1NCD 180.1's same-date MNT/DSMT restriction. This is a coverage rule written into the national coverage determination itself, not an NCCI Procedure-to-Procedure edit. No modifier indicator applies to it and no modifier bypasses it — a same-date MNT and DSMT claim for the same patient is not appealable on the merits; the only fix is separating the dates going forward.
  • 2NCCI PTP edits against health-behavior assessment/intervention codes (96156-series). ⚠️ This build could not confirm specific column 1/column 2 pairings or modifier-indicator values between MNT codes and 96156-series codes directly against CMS's NCCI PTP edit file — treat any specific pairing reported elsewhere as unconfirmed and look it up in the CMS NCCI PTP Edits Lookup Tool before building a scrubber rule around it. Where a pairing carries modifier indicator 1, it's appealable with documentation of a genuinely distinct service; indicator 0 pairings are not, regardless of documentation.
  • 3Payer-specific medical policy. Separate from both of the above — a commercial or Medicaid MCO can restrict same-day billing of multiple nutrition codes, or cap units per encounter, purely as plan policy with no NCCI or NCD basis at all. These are appealable, but only against that specific payer's own medical policy document, not against a federal standard that doesn't apply to the claim.

The practical failure mode: a biller gets an MNT/DSMT same-date denial and appeals it as if it were a bundling edit with a modifier override, when it's actually a hard coverage restriction with no override at all — wasted staff time on an unwinnable appeal. Sort the denial into the right bucket first. The complete bundling reference, including how to work through all three rule sources on a real denied claim, is in our NCCI edits and bundling rules for nutrition and dietetics billing guide.

Payer-specific coverage nuances

"Medicare covers it" and "our payer covers it" are two different sentences in this specialty, and the gap runs in both directions. Medicare's MNT and DSMT benefits are statutorily limited to two diagnosis categories — diabetes and renal disease as narrowly defined at 42 CFR 410.130 — and do not extend to obesity, prediabetes, general wellness counseling, eating disorders, or most other conditions a dietitian legitimately treats, no matter how clinically indicated the counseling was.

State Medicaid programs frequently cover a broader diagnosis list than Medicare for nutrition counseling, sometimes including obesity, prediabetes, and pediatric growth/feeding conditions Medicare's MNT benefit excludes entirely — but Medicaid MNT coverage and code sets vary meaningfully by state, so a diagnosis list that supports a claim in one state Medicaid program won't necessarily transfer to another. Commercial and ACA marketplace plans add a third layer: MNT is frequently covered as a preventive service under the ACA's preventive-services mandate for specific conditions (commonly obesity and cardiovascular risk factors, depending on the plan and state mandate), which means a diagnosis that gets a claim paid by a commercial payer — obesity counseling, for instance — is routinely non-covered by Medicare under NCD 180.1's narrower definition. The reverse isn't generally true: a diagnosis that qualifies for Medicare MNT (diabetes, qualifying renal disease) is essentially always covered by commercial plans as well, since it clears a higher bar already. Build your eligibility-verification workflow around the specific payer's diagnosis list at intake, not around a single "does MNT get covered" checkbox.

Prior authorization and referral requirements

Referral and authorization failures are among the most preventable denials in this specialty because they're entirely knowable before the appointment happens, yet they're routinely caught only after a claim denies.

Medicare's MNT referral requirement is physician-only, and it's a hard requirement, not a preference: per Section 1861(r)(1) of the Social Security Act as codified at 42 CFR 410.130 and 410.132, the referral has to come from a physician. Since the January 1, 2022 regulatory update aligned the regulation text with the statute, referrals from nurse practitioners and physician assistants do not qualify for MNT coverage, even in practices where NPs and PAs function as the patient's primary treating provider for everything else. The referral also has to be renewed annually on a calendar-year basis regardless of when in the year the patient was originally referred — a referral from March doesn't carry the patient through to the following March; it needs renewal each calendar year the benefit continues, which is a scheduling and intake tracking problem more than a coding one.

Commercial payers layer their own prior-authorization requirements on top of, and independent of, Medicare's referral rules — MNT, DSMT, and obesity counseling all show up on commercial prior-auth lists at meaningfully different rates depending on the plan, and most practices don't verify this per-payer until a claim has already denied for a missing authorization that could have been caught at scheduling. Build authorization verification into the same intake workflow as diagnosis and referral verification, not as a separate step that only runs after the first denial teaches the lesson. Full detail on Medicare's referral mechanics, the annual renewal calendar, and a payer-by-payer prior-auth verification workflow is in our prior authorization and referral requirements for nutrition and dietetics billing guide.

Frequently asked questions

What's the actual legal basis for Medicare's MNT hour limits, and can we cite it in an appeal?

Yes, and almost nobody does. Coverage for MNT comes from Section 1861(s)(2)(V) of the Social Security Act, the implementing regulations at 42 CFR 410.130 through 410.134, and NCD 180.1 (Pub. 100-3, Chapter 180), which sets the 3-hour initial-year and 2-hour subsequent-year limits and the exception at 42 CFR 410.132(b)(5) for additional hours after a documented change in condition, diagnosis, or treatment regimen. Citing the specific NCD number and regulation, not "Medicare only covers 3 hours," is what turns a denial write-off into an appeal a MAC has to actually engage with.

Why does the same registered dietitian bill MNT one way and obesity IBT the opposite way at the same clinic?

Because the two benefits sit under different statutory and regulatory authority and impose opposite provider-status rules. MNT (97802-97804) is a dietitian-specific benefit under 42 CFR 410.130-410.134 that an RDN bills directly under their own NPI; it is not payable incident-to a physician. Obesity IBT (G0447/G0473) is a preventive service under NCD 210.12 that names the qualified furnishing provider as a primary care physician or NPP, so when an RD delivers it, it is billed incident-to that physician or NPP in a non-facility primary care setting instead. One dietitian, one clinic, two opposite billing statuses depending on which code family is on the claim.

Can our practice bill DSMT off our RDN's individual Medicare enrollment?

No. DSMT is not a service an individually enrolled dietitian bills the way they bill MNT. The training program itself has to be accredited, either through the American Diabetes Association's Education Recognition Program or through ADCES' Diabetes Education Accreditation Program (DEAP), against the National Standards for Diabetes Self-Management Education and Support. Only an accredited program can furnish and bill DSMT; enrollment runs through CMS-855I under specialty code 71, but accreditation is the gating requirement most practices don't budget the lead time for.

Can MNT and DSMT be billed on the same date of service?

No, and this isn't an NCCI edit you can override with a modifier — it's a coverage restriction written directly into NCD 180.1. Medicare will pay both benefits, initial and subsequent year, without reducing either one's hours, but only as long as MNT and DSMT are not furnished on the same calendar date. Schedule them on separate dates; there is no modifier that fixes a same-date claim after the fact.

Do NP or PA referrals count for Medicare MNT coverage?

No. Since the January 1, 2022 regulatory update aligned 42 CFR 410.130 and 410.132 with the statute, MNT referrals must come from a physician as defined at Section 1861(r)(1) of the Social Security Act. A nurse practitioner or physician assistant referral does not satisfy the requirement, even though NPs and PAs can independently order plenty of other diagnostic and therapeutic services. Front-desk staff who assume any qualified provider's referral counts are setting up a medical-necessity denial that has nothing to do with the clinical picture.

What changed for MNT billing on August 3, 2026?

Billing-industry reporting describes a hard claim edit taking effect August 3, 2026 that denies MNT claims on 97802, 97803, G0270, and G0271 when submitted without a qualifying diagnosis on the claim. ⚠️ This build could not confirm the edit directly against a CMS transmittal or MAC bulletin, so verify it against your own MAC's published guidance before treating it as settled, but the operational fix is the same either way: never let an MNT claim leave the building without the specific diabetes or renal-disease diagnosis attached.

Does Medicare cover MNT for a dialysis patient?

Generally, no. The regulatory definition of "renal disease" for MNT purposes at 42 CFR 410.130 explicitly means chronic renal insufficiency or end-stage renal disease when dialysis is not received, or the beneficiary's condition for 36 months after a kidney transplant. A patient already on dialysis is typically excluded from the MNT benefit because that population's nutrition counseling is built into the ESRD facility's bundled payment instead. Billing MNT against a Z99.2 dialysis-dependence diagnosis alone, without a transplant or pre-dialysis CKD context, is a common and avoidable medical-necessity denial.

Is modifier 95 required for MNT/DSMT telehealth billed to Medicare?

Generally not for Medicare fee-for-service, which identifies telehealth through place-of-service coding instead — POS 02 for telehealth delivered somewhere other than the patient's home, POS 10 for telehealth delivered to the patient's home — rather than requiring modifier 95 on top of it. Most commercial and Medicare Advantage payers still require modifier 95 regardless of POS, so the safest workflow is to default to appending it unless a specific payer's policy says otherwise, and to always confirm current telehealth flexibility status against your MAC before relying on a waiver that may have changed.
