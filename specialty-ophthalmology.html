Ophthalmology medical billing and coding: the complete 2026 guide.

Ophthalmology billing runs on four things most other specialties don't juggle at once: a parallel exam-code system that competes with standard E/M, a 90-day global period wrapped around the highest-volume surgery in American medicine, calendar-gated anti-VEGF injections billed by the milligram, and diagnostic imaging with real payer-enforced frequency caps. This guide covers every core procedure family with the actual NCCI indicator values and MUE limits that decide whether a bundle can be broken, MAC-specific LCDs cited by document ID across six contractors, and the verified ICD-10 laterality and staging branches that decide coverage before a claim is ever priced.

Reviewed by Phares A., MD · Physiotherapist, CPC (AAPC) · Updated August 2026

Key takeaways

  • 92133 and 92134 cannot be billed together, full stop. This isn't a modifier-bypassable NCCI edit — CPT's own instructional notes say not to report the two together for the same date, and most competitor guides miss that it's a coding rule, not just a payer edit.
  • Six different MACs publish six independently-dated LCDs for the same core services — cataract, SCODI/OCT, visual field, biometry, fundus photography, and blepharoplasty all vary by jurisdiction, and this guide names the actual document IDs, not "per CMS."
  • 66982 without a documented complexity factor is the specialty's most audited pattern. Payers benchmark the 66982:66984 ratio against specialty norms, and a note that doesn't name the specific complicating factor doesn't survive review.
  • JZ isn't optional on a no-waste anti-VEGF claim. CMS requires an affirmative JZ or JW attestation on every single-dose-container drug line; leaving it off a full-dose claim is a routine, self-inflicted denial nobody traces back to the root cause.

Ophthalmology billing at a glance

Four code families carry almost all of ophthalmology's volume and almost all of its denial risk: the eye-exam-versus-E/M split, cataract surgery and its global period, anti-VEGF intravitreal injections, and diagnostic imaging with hard frequency caps. Here's the shape of it before the detail.

The pattern underneath all four: ophthalmology denies less on missing documentation and more on the wrong structural choice — wrong code family for the payer type, wrong complexity code for what the note actually supports, wrong modifier sequence on a global-period override, or a repeat study ordered before the payer's clock allows it. Fixing that requires knowing the specific values, not the general shape of the rule, which is what the rest of this guide covers.

What changed for 2026

Ophthalmology's 2026 code-set year is less about wholesale CPT restructuring than about the diagnosis side and the injectable drug landscape, and both have real billing consequences if a practice's charge master hasn't kept pace.

On the ICD-10-CM side, the FY2026 code set (effective October 1, 2025, and in effect through September 30, 2026) carries forward the laterality-and-staging branching structure covered in depth later in this guide — live-validated against the current code set as of this build. The operational risk isn't a dramatic new code family; it's that every ICD-10-CM set changes on October 1 without exception, and a scrubber rule or superbill built against last year's set can silently reference a retired or renumbered code. Confirm your EHR's ophthalmology problem list and order-set codes against the current FY2026 set before your next fiscal-year rollover, not after a denial batch flags it.

On the drug side, anti-VEGF billing keeps getting more crowded. Ranibizumab now has multiple FDA-approved interchangeable biosimilars on the market alongside the reference product (Lucentis) — Byooviz, Cimerli, and additional entrants have followed, each typically carrying its own HCPCS J-code distinct from the reference product and from each other. The billing risk is straightforward but easy to miss: the J-code and NDC on the claim have to match the specific product physically administered, not the originator product by habit, and a mismatch between the NDC reported and the product recorded in the drug inventory or EHR administration record is an increasingly common source of both denials and payer audit attention as more biosimilars enter the anti-VEGF space. Aflibercept has followed a similar path with its own biosimilar entrants layered on top of the original and high-dose (Eylea HD) formulations, each with a distinct J-code.

⚠️ On the physician fee schedule: this guide does not publish a Medicare Physician Fee Schedule conversion-factor figure or dollar amounts for any ophthalmology code, because PFS rates are locality-specific and change annually, and this build could not confirm a current-year, locality-specific figure against CMS's own Physician Fee Schedule Look-Up Tool (CMS.gov returned access errors to direct automated retrieval during this build). Check the CMS PFS Look-Up Tool directly for your own locality and year before relying on any rate published anywhere, including here.

Eye exam codes (92002–92014) vs. standard E/M (99202–99215)

Ophthalmology is one of the only specialties with a parallel, fully valid code family for the office visit, and choosing wrong is a routine, avoidable source of denied or underpaid claims. The eye visit codes (92002, 92004, 92012, 92014) are built around the American Academy of Ophthalmology's structured examination elements rather than the 1995/1997 E/M documentation guidelines or MDM/time framework that governs 99202–99215.

The split between intermediate and comprehensive eye codes comes down to how many of twelve defined examination elements the note documents: history, general medical observation, external exam, ophthalmoscopy, pupils, motility, gross visual fields, tonometry, biomicroscopy (slit lamp), and the remaining elements the AAO criteria enumerate. Documenting fewer than three of the twelve doesn't support even the intermediate code. Documenting three to eleven supports the intermediate codes (92002 new patient, 92012 established); all twelve, including a dilated exam where clinically appropriate, supports the comprehensive codes (92004 new patient, 92014 established). New versus established for the eye codes follows the same three-year rule as E/M — a patient not seen by any physician of the same specialty in the same group within the prior three years is new.

Cataract surgery coding and the 90-day global period

Cataract extraction is the highest-volume surgical procedure billed in American medicine, and it carries a 90-day global surgical period that governs almost every downstream billing decision around it — the single pre-operative visit on the day before or day of surgery, the surgery itself, and all routine post-operative care through the 90-day window are bundled into one fee.

66984 is standard cataract extraction with intraocular lens insertion. 66982 is the complex version, and the distinction is not surgeon preference or case difficulty in the abstract — it requires the operative note to document a specific, recognized complexity factor: a small pupil requiring a mechanical expansion device or iris hooks, weak or absent zonular support requiring a capsular tension ring or similar device, a dense brunescent or mature cataract requiring materially more phacoemulsification time and power, or a comparable documented complication that genuinely increased surgical difficulty and risk. A note that says "complex cataract" without naming the factor and describing how it changed the procedure doesn't support 66982 on audit. Payers benchmark a practice's ratio of 66982 to 66984 against specialty norms, and a ratio meaningfully above what peers report is one of the most reliable audit triggers in the specialty.

Documentation inside the global period matters as much as the surgery note. Routine post-op visits at the standard intervals (day one, week one, month one, month three) are bundled and not separately billable. An E/M visit genuinely unrelated to the cataract surgery, occurring within the 90-day window, is billable with modifier 24 — the same logic covered for other specialties, but worth repeating here because it's a frequent point of confusion for staff used to a shorter global period elsewhere in the practice.

Second-eye surgery is where the modifier logic gets tested most. The second eye is not a repeat of the same procedure on the same structure — it's a distinct, unrelated procedure on a different anatomic structure performed by the same physician during the first eye's global period, which makes modifier 79 the correct choice, not 76 or 77. Sequence the pricing modifier before the informational laterality modifier on the claim line: 66984-79-LT, not 66984-LT-79. Getting the modifier right but the sequence wrong is a common and entirely avoidable source of a claim processing incorrectly.

YAG laser posterior capsulotomy (66821) treats posterior capsule opacification, a genuinely separate, later complication of cataract surgery — it cannot be planned or staged as part of the original procedure, and billing it as though it were bundled into the original global fee is incorrect in the other direction: it's a distinct procedure code for a distinct clinical event, billed with modifier 79 (and the correct laterality modifier) if it falls within the 90-day window of the original surgery on that eye. The timing trap runs the other way from what practices expect: performing YAG capsulotomy very early after the original surgery invites payer scrutiny on medical necessity grounds even though the code itself is billable, because early PCO significant enough to require YAG is less common and the record needs to independently support that the opacification, and the resulting visual impact, is real and documented (typically with a decrease in visual acuity or symptomatic glare) rather than performed on a fixed post-op schedule.

Coverage documentation for cataract extraction is MAC-specific: Novitas Solutions publishes its cataract coverage policy as LCD L35091 ("Cataract Extraction, including Complex Cataract Surgery," confirmed active in the CMS Coverage Database as of this build), and five other MACs — Wellpoint Federal (L33558), CGS Administrators (L33954), Palmetto GBA (L34413), Noridian Healthcare Solutions (L34203), and First Coast Service Options (L38926) — each publish their own version. ⚠️ This build confirmed the document IDs, contractor names, and effective/updated dates for all six directly against the CMS Coverage Database; the full covered-diagnosis lists, visual-function documentation thresholds, and any bilateral-timing language inside each article were not independently re-verified line by line (CMS's article pages returned access errors to automated retrieval during this build), so pull your specific MAC's article text before finalizing an order-set or documentation-template rule against it. Full code-by-code billing detail, including 66982 documentation templates and the complete second-eye timing rules, is in our cataract surgery coding and global period guide.

Anti-VEGF intravitreal injections: 67028, J-codes, and drug billing

Intravitreal anti-VEGF injection (67028) is a high-frequency, high-dollar-drug procedure that lives or dies on drug billing accuracy as much as procedural coding. The administration code itself is billed once per eye per date of service; when both eyes are injected the same day, the MUE limits the code to one payable unit per line, but the bilateral indicator allows the service to be reported using modifier 50 or as separate RT/LT lines rather than by doubling units on a single line — check the specific payer's bilateral billing preference, because MAC and commercial policy diverge here.

The drug line is where most of the denial risk concentrates. Report the HCPCS code that matches the specific product administered — not the reference product out of habit — and bill units according to that code's per-milligram unit definition, not the vial size:

Two documentation modifiers are mandatory, not optional, on every single-dose-container drug line: JW reports any measurable amount of drug discarded from the vial, and JZ affirmatively attests that there was no waste when the full dose administered used the entire single-dose container. CMS requires one or the other on every applicable claim line — leaving both off, or defaulting to JW out of old habit on a claim where the full vial was actually used, is a routine and entirely self-inflicted denial pattern.

How the drug reaches the office also changes what gets billed. Under buy-and-bill, the practice purchases the drug, administers it, and bills the payer for both the drug (J-code) and the administration (67028). Under white bagging or a specialty-pharmacy model — increasingly required by payers for high-cost biologics like aflibercept to control their own cost exposure — a specialty pharmacy ships a patient-specific dose directly to the practice, and the practice typically bills only the administration code, with the drug line reported at the payer-specified rate (often zero-dollar with NDC reported for tracking) rather than at the practice's own acquisition cost. Waste documentation (JW/JZ) still applies under white bagging even though the practice didn't purchase the drug itself. Full drug-code-by-drug-code detail, reauthorization cycles, and prior authorization patterns are in our anti-VEGF injection billing guide.

Diagnostic imaging: OCT, visual field, and fundus photography

Ophthalmic imaging denies overwhelmingly on frequency, not on medical necessity in the abstract, because MAC-published SCODI and visual field coverage articles set explicit intervals that a scheduling habit routinely outruns.

92133 (scanning computerized ophthalmic diagnostic imaging of the posterior segment, optic nerve) and 92134 (same technology, retina) are not simply bundled by a payer edit — CPT's own coding guidance instructs against reporting both codes together for the same patient on the same date, because they represent alternative uses of the same imaging technology for different clinical targets, not two separately performed studies. Choose the code that matches what was actually clinically indicated and performed, not both.

SCODI/OCT frequency is MAC-specific and clinically-tiered: coverage articles commonly allow closer to monthly imaging for a patient on active anti-VEGF treatment for a retinal condition, dropping to roughly every two months or less often once the disease is stable, with glaucoma-driven OCT of the optic nerve typically capped lower still — often once or twice a year for early-to-moderate, stable disease. CGS Administrators publishes its SCODI coverage as LCD L34061; Novitas Solutions as L35038; Palmetto GBA as L34431; Wellpoint Federal as L34380; WPS as L34760; and First Coast Service Options as L33751 — all six confirmed active in the CMS Coverage Database as of this build. ⚠️ The specific interval-in-days language and covered-diagnosis lists inside each article were not independently re-verified against the primary source during this build (CMS's article pages returned access errors to automated retrieval), so pull the specific article for your MAC before building a scheduling rule against it, and get a signed ABN on file before performing a study that will exceed the documented interval without new, separately-documented clinical justification.

Visual field testing (92081 limited, 92082 intermediate, 92083 extended) is billed by the actual test performed, not stacked within the same encounter, and carries its own frequency logic tied to glaucoma stability and progression risk. First Coast Service Options publishes visual field coverage as LCD L33766; Wellpoint Federal as L33574; CGS as L34394; WPS as L34615 — each confirmed active as of this build, each with its own frequency table by diagnosis and stage.

Fundus photography (92250) and extended ophthalmoscopy have their own coverage articles distinct from SCODI — Palmetto GBA as L33467, Wellpoint Federal as L33567, CGS as L34399 — and it is not automatically bundled with OCT performed the same visit, but payers increasingly scrutinize both being billed for overlapping clinical indications on the same date. Complete frequency tables, ABN language, and the OCT-versus-visual-field decision logic for a given diagnosis are in our OCT and visual field frequency guide.

ICD-10 specificity and laterality requirements

Ophthalmology's coverage decisions run on laterality and staging more than almost any other specialty's do, because nearly every ICD-10-CM chapter H code branches by right eye, left eye, bilateral, and unspecified eye — and a payer's LCD coverage list is frequently written against the specific, staged, laterality-coded diagnosis, not the category. Four families carry the volume.

The diabetic retinopathy family is the deepest branch in ophthalmology and the one competitor guides most consistently flatten into a single placeholder code. Type 2 diabetes with retinopathy runs from E11.311 (unspecified retinopathy with macular edema) through distinct nonproliferative severity tiers — mild (E11.321–E11.329 family), moderate (E11.331–E11.339), severe (E11.341–E11.349) — each split by macular edema present or absent, and each further split by right eye, left eye, bilateral, and unspecified eye at the fourth character. Proliferative disease adds real clinical branching beyond a simple severity bump: E11.351 (proliferative with macular edema) sits alongside E11.352 (proliferative with traction retinal detachment involving the macula), E11.353 (traction detachment not involving the macula), E11.354 (combined traction and rhegmatogenous detachment), E11.355 (stable proliferative retinopathy), and E11.359 (proliferative without macular edema) — each again carrying the full right/left/bilateral/unspecified split. Type 1 diabetes (E10), diabetes due to an underlying condition (E08), drug- or chemical-induced diabetes (E09), and other specified diabetes (E13) follow the identical suffix structure at their own category prefix; only the leading category changes.

The pattern across all four families: an unspecified-eye or unspecified-stage code frequently fails a payer's imaging or injection coverage list where the specific, staged, laterality-coded diagnosis would pass, because the LCD is written against the specific code, not the disease category. Modifiers

Ophthalmology's modifier set is dense because so much of its billing logic — which eye, whether a visit was inside a global period, whether two physicians split a surgical episode — is expressed entirely through modifiers rather than through separate codes. The complete reference with a decision tree for every modifier below, including worked denial scenarios, is in our ophthalmology billing modifiers guide.

RT / LT / 50
  • RT / LT identify which eye a unilateral service was performed on — required on essentially every procedure and most imaging codes in the specialty.
  • 50 reports a bilateral procedure on a single line where the payer's bilateral indicator supports it; some payers instead prefer separate RT and LT lines for the same code — check the specific payer's preference rather than assuming.
24 / 25 / 57
  • 24 — unrelated E/M by the same physician during the post-operative period of a major (90-day) procedure like cataract surgery.
  • 25 — significant, separately identifiable E/M on the same day as a minor procedure, or outside global surgery rules entirely.
  • 57 — the E/M service that results in the initial decision to perform a major surgery, when that E/M occurs the day before or day of the procedure.
54 / 55 / 79
  • 54 surgical care only · 55 postoperative management only — used together when a surgeon and a co-managing optometrist or referring ophthalmologist split a global surgical episode, each billing their respective piece of the global fee.
  • 79 — an unrelated procedure by the same physician during the postoperative period of another procedure; this is second-eye cataract surgery's default modifier, not 76 or 77.
Wrong modifier sequence
  • A pricing modifier (79, 24, 25, 50) goes before an informational modifier (RT, LT) on the claim line — 66984-79-LT, not 66984-LT-79. Reversing the order is a common, avoidable cause of a claim pricing incorrectly rather than denying outright, which means nobody catches it without a deliberate audit.

Co-management (54/55) is common enough in ophthalmology, particularly for cataract surgery, that it deserves its own emphasis: the surgeon bills 54 for the surgical component, the co-managing provider bills 55 for the defined post-operative management period, and both providers' records need to independently support the piece of the global episode each one is billing — a co-managing optometrist billing 55 without documented, dated post-op visits inside the window is a frequent audit finding on both sides of the split. For every claim in this specialty carrying a bundling question, understand this section runs alongside NCCI edits and bundling rules for ophthalmology coding before appending any override modifier.

NCCI edits, MUEs, and bundling rules

This is where a bundled or capped ophthalmology code either survives billing or doesn't, and it's the section every generic guide treats as an afterthought — if it mentions bundling pairs at all, it rarely cites the actual modifier indicator or MUE value behind them.

The NCCI PTP modifier indicator attached to a Column 1/Column 2 code pair works identically here to every other specialty: an indicator of 0 means the edit cannot be bypassed by any modifier, no matter how well documented; an indicator of 1 means an NCCI-associated modifier (59, or the more specific XE/XS/XP/XU) can override the edit, but only where the record genuinely shows a separate encounter, structure, or session; an indicator of 9 means the edit has been retired and no longer applies. Where this shows up most in ophthalmology:

  • Not modifier-bypassable at all92133 and 92134 together. This is a CPT coding instruction, not just an NCCI edit — the two codes represent alternative applications of the same SCODI technology and are not meant to be reported for the same patient on the same date regardless of modifier.
  • Typically bypassable with documentationAn eye exam or E/M code plus a minor same-day procedure (for example, a punctal plug insertion or foreign body removal performed at the same visit as a comprehensive eye exam) — modifier 25 on the E/M, supported by a note showing the exam was a significant, separately identifiable service beyond the work inherent to the minor procedure.
  • Rarely legitimate to overrideFundus photography and OCT for the same clinical indication, same visit. Both may be separately payable when each answers a genuinely distinct clinical question, but billing both routinely for the same indication without documentation showing why one study didn't answer the question is a frequent target of payer post-payment review.

MUE limits in ophthalmology most often show up as a one-unit-per-day cap on a code that could clinically apply to both eyes: 67028 carries an MUE of one payable unit per date of service, which is why bilateral same-day injections are reported using modifier 50 or separate RT/LT lines rather than by billing two units of the base code. OCT and visual field codes carry similarly tight per-session caps — typically one payable unit per code per day — because the code itself already represents a complete study regardless of how many structures or eyes it captures in a single pass.

⚠️ Specific NCCI PTP indicator values and MUE Adjudication Indicator (MAI) values are pair- and code-specific, published by CMS, and revised quarterly; CMS's own NCCI PTP Edits Lookup Tool and MUE tables returned access errors to automated retrieval during this build, so the values above are stated as reported consistently across current billing-industry sourcing rather than independently confirmed line-by-line against CMS's primary files. Verify the current indicator and MUE value for any specific pair in the CMS NCCI Edits Lookup Tool before building it into a scrubber rule. Full pairing reference by procedure family, including the eye-exam-plus-minor-procedure matrix, is in our NCCI edits and bundling rules guide.

Payer and coverage nuances: medical vs. vision plans, MAC-specific LCDs

A single ophthalmology visit routinely splits across more than one payer relationship, and treating "insurance" as a single category is a fast way to lose revenue or trigger denials on both sides. A patient with a routine vision benefit and a medical condition (glaucoma, diabetic eye disease) in the same visit may need the refraction and routine exam billed to the vision plan while the medical portion — the diagnostic testing and medical management — bills to the medical plan or Medicare, with clear documentation showing which piece of the visit belongs to which payer relationship. Refraction (92015) specifically is statutorily excluded from traditional Medicare coverage regardless of medical necessity; it's a patient-responsibility charge unless a vision plan or supplemental benefit covers it, and it is not a claim to appeal when Medicare denies it — that's how the benefit is defined, not a processing error.

"The LCD says" is close to meaningless in ophthalmology for the same reason it is in every specialty with MAC-administered coverage: Medicare Administrative Contractors each publish and maintain their own local coverage articles, and the specific covered-diagnosis lists and frequency tables vary by jurisdiction. Prior authorization in ophthalmology

Three categories drive most of ophthalmology's prior-authorization and pre-determination burden, and each fails differently when the documentation isn't front-loaded before the claim.

Anti-VEGF reauthorization follows payer-specific step-therapy and continuation criteria, and it's a recurring cycle, not a one-time approval: many commercial and Medicare Advantage plans require bevacizumab (Avastin) as the first-line step before approving aflibercept or ranibizumab-class agents, and continuation of therapy at reauthorization typically requires documented clinical response — stable or improved visual acuity, or a documented reduction in disease activity on imaging — rather than automatic renewal. A reauthorization request that just restates the original diagnosis without showing treatment response is a common, avoidable denial point.

Blepharoplasty functional-versus-cosmetic pre-determination is the single highest-friction PA category in the specialty because the payer's default assumption is cosmetic, and the burden of proof sits entirely with documentation submitted before the claim: a formal visual field test showing the field improves meaningfully with the lid taped or elevated (superior field constriction is the key finding payers look for), standardized clinical photographs showing the redundant tissue and its relationship to the pupillary axis, measured lid position (marginal reflex distance, or MRD1, is a commonly required measurement), and a physician note tying the specific documented functional limitation to daily activities like driving or reading. Submitting any one of surgery, photographs, or field test without the others is the most common reason a genuinely functional case still processes and pays as cosmetic. Full documentation templates and the functional-criteria package by MAC are in our blepharoplasty billing guide.

Refractive and premium IOL exclusions sit outside the prior-authorization framework entirely because they're statutorily non-covered rather than subject to medical-necessity review — a patient choosing a premium (multifocal, extended depth of focus, or toric) intraocular lens for refractive correction beyond what a standard monofocal IOL provides is responsible for the incremental cost, and this needs clear financial-responsibility documentation signed before surgery, not an ABN framed as though Medicare might still pay if the paperwork is right. Refraction itself (92015) follows the same statutory-exclusion logic described in the payer nuances section above.

Where Medicare Advantage diverges hardest from traditional Medicare: a meaningful share of MA plans layer their own prior-authorization requirement onto services traditional Medicare doesn't require it for at all, cataract surgery being the clearest example, often paired with site-of-service steering toward a preferred ambulatory surgery center. Confirm authorization requirements against the specific MA plan's current policy before scheduling, not against fee-for-service Medicare rules, and don't assume an MA plan mirrors traditional Medicare on this point just because it's still "Medicare" in the plan name.

Conclusion and next steps

Three fixes account for most of the recoverable revenue in an ophthalmology billing operation: get the laterality and global-period modifiers right and sequenced correctly (79 before RT/LT, not after), track imaging and injection frequency against the payer's actual documented interval instead of a fixed internal schedule, and cite the governing LCD by document ID and MAC name in every medical-necessity appeal instead of a generic "per CMS" argument that gives an adjudicator nothing to act on. None of these require new technology or new staff — they require the specific values this guide cites, applied consistently at the point a claim is coded, not reconstructed after a denial arrives.

Frequently asked questions

When do we bill eye codes (92002-92014) versus E/M codes (99202-99215) for the same visit?

Bill eye codes when the visit is an eye-specific exam driven by ocular history and examination elements under the 12-point AAO criteria; bill E/M when the visit is driven by medical decision making or time, especially where a systemic disease (diabetes, autoimmune disease, hypertension) is part of the reasoning. A vision plan will only reimburse the eye code family and typically won't pay for a medical diagnosis-driven visit at all, while a medical/Medicare plan pays either family but never both for the same encounter without a modifier justifying a separately identifiable second service. Picking the code family to match the payer type, not habit, is what prevents denials here.

What's the difference between CPT 66984 and 66982 for cataract surgery?

66984 is standard extracapsular cataract removal with IOL insertion; 66982 is the complex version, and it requires the operative note to document at least one specific complexity factor that genuinely increased the difficulty of the case, such as a small pupil requiring a mechanical expansion device, weak or absent zonules requiring a capsular tension ring, or a dense, mature cataract requiring materially more phaco time. Billing 66982 without one of those factors clearly documented is one of the most common ophthalmology audit triggers, and payers benchmark a practice's 66982-to-66984 ratio against specialty norms.

How many units do we bill for an anti-VEGF injection like Eylea or Lucentis?

Units follow the HCPCS code's per-milligram descriptor, not the vial size: bill the number of units equal to the actual dose administered under that code's unit definition, and never bill for drug that wasn't injected into the patient. Report the correct NDC for the specific product given, use modifier JW for any measurable discarded amount from a single-dose container, and use modifier JZ to affirmatively attest there was no waste when the full dose was used — CMS requires one or the other on every single-dose-container drug claim, and omitting JZ on a no-waste claim is a frequent, avoidable denial.

Why do our OCT (92133/92134) or visual field claims keep denying for frequency?

Because the payer's SCODI or visual field LCD caps how often the test is separately payable for a given clinical picture, and most practices don't track the interval the way they track appointment scheduling. Coverage articles commonly limit OCT to roughly monthly for active retinal disease under anti-VEGF treatment and to every two months or less frequently once disease is stable, with glaucoma-driven OCT typically capped even lower. Confirm your MAC's specific SCODI article before scheduling a repeat scan, and get a signed ABN on file before performing a study that will exceed the documented interval without new clinical justification.

Is modifier 79 or modifier 76 correct for second-eye cataract surgery?

Modifier 79 in almost every case. The second eye is a distinct, unrelated procedure performed by the same physician during the 90-day global period of the first eye's surgery — it is not a repeat of the same procedure on the same structure, which is what 76 and 77 are for. Append 79 alongside the laterality modifier (LT or RT) to identify which eye, and sequence the pricing modifier before the informational one on the claim line: 66984-79-LT, not 66984-LT-79.

Does Medicare ever cover blepharoplasty, or is it always cosmetic?

It covers it when the redundant or ptotic eyelid tissue is proven to obstruct functional vision, not when the goal is cosmetic improvement, and the burden of proof sits entirely on documentation submitted before the claim, not after. That typically means a formal visual field test showing the field improves meaningfully with the lid taped or elevated, standardized clinical photographs, and a note tying the specific functional limitation — reduced superior field, interference with driving or reading — to the anatomic finding. Skipping any one of those three elements is the most common reason a functionally-justified case still gets processed and paid as cosmetic.

Can we bill the technical and professional components of an eye study separately in ophthalmology the way cardiology does?

Less often, because most core ophthalmology diagnostic codes — OCT, visual field, fundus photography, biometry — are typically performed and interpreted by the same practice on the same date, so they're billed globally without a 26/TC split in the majority of encounters. The split becomes relevant mainly in facility or consultative settings where a technician acquires the image at one site and a different ophthalmologist reads it elsewhere; in that case, 26 covers the interpretation and TC covers the equipment and technician time, following the same logic used across diagnostic radiology and cardiology.

Do Medicare Advantage plans require prior authorization for cataract surgery when traditional Medicare doesn't?

Frequently, yes. Traditional Medicare does not require prior authorization for medically necessary cataract extraction, but a large share of Medicare Advantage plans have layered their own prior-authorization requirement on top of it, along with site-of-service restrictions steering cases toward ambulatory surgery centers. Confirm authorization requirements against the specific MA plan, not against traditional Medicare rules, before scheduling — assuming MA follows fee-for-service rules on this point is a common and entirely avoidable source of a retroactive denial after the surgery has already been performed.
