Family medicine billing and coding: the complete 2026 guide.

No specialty stacks more code combinations into a single encounter than family medicine — a preventive exam, a chronic-condition problem visit, a vaccine, and a minor procedure can all legitimately happen in one 20-minute slot, and every one of those has its own diagnosis-linkage rule. Every competitor guide we reviewed lists CPT ranges with no ICD-10 pairing at all, which is exactly where these claims fail on CO-50 and CO-167. This guide pairs every code family with the specific diagnosis that supports it, names the NCCI modifier indicators and MACs nobody else in this niche names, and gives the AWV-versus-preventive-exam decision tree and the CCM consent-and-attribution rules the full treatment they need.

Reviewed by Phares A., MD · Physiotherapist, CPC (AAPC) · Updated August 2026

Key takeaways

  • Medicare does not pay 99397 — full stop. Family practices with any Medicare panel need the G0402/G0438/G0439 pathway, not the commercial preventive codes, and no modifier fixes billing the wrong code family.
  • Every code family here is paired with the ICD-10 code that actually supports it. An unspecified diagnosis is frequently the real reason a preventive, CCM, or chronic-condition claim denies — not the CPT code.
  • CCM attribution conflicts are a real, recurring denial driver — only one practitioner can bill CCM for a patient in a given month, and two practices both billing it is one of the most common preventable CO-151 patterns in primary care.
  • Same-day preventive-plus-problem visits need a documentation-separation paragraph, not just a modifier 25. The modifier is necessary but not sufficient — the note has to actually show two distinct services.

Family medicine billing at a glance

Family medicine's denial volume comes from one structural fact: more code families collide in a single visit here than in almost any other specialty. A same-day preventive exam, a chronic-condition E/M, a vaccine with its administration code, and a minor procedure can all legitimately appear on one claim, and each one has its own diagnosis-linkage requirement, its own modifier logic, and its own bundling edit against the others. Here's the shape of it before the detail.

What changed for 2026

Three changes matter operationally for a family medicine panel this year, not just as trivia for a coding newsletter.

ICD-10-CM, effective October 1, 2025. The FY2026 code set added 487 new diagnosis codes, deleted 28, and revised 38, and it's the set in effect for every date of service from October 1, 2025 through September 30, 2026. If a scrubber rule or superbill still references a deleted FY2025 code, claims deny on code validity before medical necessity is even evaluated — worth an audit of your charge master and templates before the next batch goes out, not after.

CPT, effective January 1, 2026. The 2026 CPT set added 288 new codes, deleted 84, and revised 46 industry-wide. Family medicine's core E/M, preventive, and vaccine families were not restructured the way some procedural specialties' code sets were this cycle, but any practice running digital-health or remote-monitoring services alongside primary care should confirm none of the codes they bill fall inside this year's deletions.

G2211 same-day preventive rule, carried into 2026. G2211 is the visit-complexity add-on for E/M codes that reflects the ongoing complexity of longitudinal primary care. Since 2025, CMS allows G2211 to be billed alongside an E/M carrying modifier 25 specifically when the same-day service is a Medicare Part B preventive benefit — the Annual Wellness Visit, vaccine administration, or another Part B preventive service — rather than a procedure, which is exactly family medicine's most common same-day billing pattern. Reporting on the 2026 Medicare Physician Fee Schedule final rule describes this preventive-visit exception being carried forward and, per some industry summaries, extended to the home/residence E/M code family (99341–99345, 99347–99350) as well. ⚠️ This build could not open the CMS Physician Fee Schedule final rule text directly to independently confirm the home-visit expansion detail (cms.gov returned an access error to automated retrieval) — it is reported consistently across RCM industry sources but should be confirmed against the final rule or your MAC's own guidance before building it into a charge rule.

Chronic care management reimbursement. Multiple RCM industry sources describe a roughly 10% increase to CCM-family payment (99490, 99439, 99487, 99489, 99491, 99437) in the 2026 Medicare Physician Fee Schedule final rule relative to 2025 rates. ⚠️ No dollar amount is stated in this guide for any of these codes — Physician Fee Schedule payment is locality-specific and this build did not verify a locality-specific rate against a CMS primary source. Check your own locality and year in the CMS Physician Fee Schedule Look-Up Tool before building a rate into a budget or a patient estimate.

E/M coding: 99202–99215 by time or MDM

New patient visits (99202–99205) and established patient visits (99212–99215) are leveled on either total time on the date of the encounter or medical decision making, not history or exam — fully satisfying either pathway justifies the code, and history/exam are documented only to the extent clinically appropriate. This is the 2021 E/M framework, and it's still the operative standard.

Established patient office visit time thresholds (total time on the date of the encounter).
CodeTimeMDM level (alternate pathway)
9921210–19 minutesStraightforward
9921320–29 minutesLow
9921430–39 minutesModerate
9921540–54 minutesHigh

The MDM pathway is scored on three elements, and the code level is set by whichever two of the three the encounter meets: the number and complexity of problems addressed, the amount and complexity of data reviewed and analyzed, and the risk of complications, morbidity, or mortality from the management decisions made that day. A single stable chronic illness is typically low complexity; two or more stable chronic illnesses, or one chronic illness with exacerbation, is typically moderate; a chronic illness with severe exacerbation or a decision about drug therapy requiring intensive monitoring for toxicity is typically high.

Family medicine's most common underlevel error lives right in that middle band: chronic-condition management — medication titration, a patient carrying three or four comorbidities, a diabetes regimen adjustment — frequently supports 99214 or 99215 on MDM alone, and gets coded 99213 because the documentation captured the visit narratively without capturing the complexity elements a payer's auditor is actually scoring against. Prescription drug management is, by itself, one of the risk factors that can support moderate complexity; a note that lists "continue metformin" without documenting the titration reasoning or the comorbidity interaction under-supports the level the visit actually earned. The full decision tree — new-versus-established code selection, worked MDM scoring examples, and the time-tracking documentation that survives an audit — is in our family medicine E/M coding guide.

Preventive medicine vs. problem-oriented E/M: modifier 25

The age-banded preventive medicine codes (99381–99387 new patient, 99391–99397 established patient) cover a comprehensive, age-and-gender-appropriate history, exam, counseling, and risk-factor reduction visit — and by CPT's own structure, they already include management of minor, ordinarily stable problems the way a routine physical normally would. The trigger for adding a problem-oriented E/M on top of that visit is a problem significant enough that it requires its own separate work — a new complaint, a medication change, a chronic condition that needs its own assessment and plan beyond what a preventive exam covers — not simply that a chronic condition exists and got mentioned.

Modifier 25 on the E/M is legitimate when the note shows genuinely separate, additional work: its own history of present illness for the new problem, its own exam findings relevant to it, and its own MDM supporting a specific level. It's an unbundling risk when the "extra" work is really just a passing mention folded into the preventive note's general review — refilling a stable medication without any new assessment, or noting "blood pressure well controlled" without a plan change. Payers that audit modifier-25 usage specifically look for this documentation-separation requirement: a distinct paragraph or section addressing the problem, separate from the preventive exam's own narrative, is what survives review. G2211 interacts here too — since the Part B preventive exception described above applies to Medicare's own preventive services (the AWV, vaccine administration), not the commercial 99381–99397 codes, don't assume G2211 pays the same way on a commercial preventive-plus-problem claim as it does on a Medicare AWV-plus-problem claim; confirm against the specific payer. The complete decision tree, including how to structure the note so the separation is unambiguous, is in our preventive-vs-problem-visit guide.

Medicare Annual Wellness Visit vs. preventive exam

This is the single most consequential code-family decision a family practice makes on its Medicare panel, and it's the one every competitor guide we reviewed mentions in passing without giving it real treatment. Medicare Part B does not cover 99381–99397 at all — there's no benefit category for a routine physical exam under those codes. Billing 99397 to Medicare isn't a documentation shortfall; it's the wrong code family, and it denies as a statutory exclusion every time.

Medicare's preventive-visit code family and when each applies.
CodeServiceFrequency
G0402Initial Preventive Physical Examination (the "Welcome to Medicare" visit) — review of history, vitals, risk-factor screening, and an end-of-life planning discussion if the patient wants itOnce only, within the first 12 months of Medicare Part B enrollment
G0438Initial Annual Wellness Visit — establishes the Health Risk Assessment and the Personalized Prevention PlanOnce per patient, after the first 12 months of Part B enrollment have passed
G0439Subsequent Annual Wellness Visit — updates the HRA and prevention planOnce every 12 months thereafter (at least 11 full months since the last AWV)

The AWV is not a physical exam in the commercial-preventive-code sense — it's a structured risk-assessment and prevention-planning visit. Required components include the Health Risk Assessment, review and update of the patient's medical and family history, a list of current providers and medications, measurement of height/weight/blood pressure and BMI, a cognitive impairment assessment, a personalized prevention plan with a screening schedule, and personalized health advice. It does not require a hands-on physical exam in the way a commercial annual physical does, and it does not include treatment of new or existing problems — a distinction that matters directly for the modifier-25 question below.

Same-day billing of an AWV plus a problem E/M works exactly like the preventive-vs-problem logic above, with one more layer: the AWV's own content (the HRA, the prevention plan, routine risk counseling) can't be the source material for the problem E/M's medical decision making. If the physician addresses an actual complaint or manages an actual chronic condition beyond the AWV's scope — adjusting a blood pressure medication, working up a new symptom — that's billable with modifier 25 on the E/M, documented as its own distinct section of the note with its own assessment and plan. What gets denied on audit is an AWV note that discusses a chronic condition only in the context of the prevention plan, with a 99214 billed against that same discussion and no separate workup documented anywhere in the chart.

Worked scenario: a patient comes in for a subsequent AWV (G0439) and mentions new-onset knee pain unrelated to any condition on the prevention plan. The physician examines the knee, orders imaging, and starts a treatment plan — that's a legitimate 99213 or 99214 with modifier 25 attached, billed alongside G0439, because the knee complaint required its own history, exam, and plan that the AWV structure doesn't cover. Contrast that with a patient whose AWV surfaces poorly controlled hypertension through the risk assessment, and the physician simply notes "will continue current regimen" inside the AWV note — that's prevention-plan content, not separately billable E/M work, no matter how it's coded. Full worked examples, the exact HRA/PPPS documentation checklist, and the G0402 one-time-only trap are in our Medicare Annual Wellness Visit billing guide.

Chronic care management: 99490, 99439, 99487

CCM pays for the between-visit work every family practice already does informally — care coordination, medication reconciliation, and monitoring for patients with multiple chronic conditions — and it's one of the more consistently underbilled services in primary care, because the attribution and consent rules trip practices up before the clinical work ever becomes the problem.

Core CCM code family.
CodeServiceRequirement
99490Non-complex CCM, clinical staff time under general supervisionAt least 20 minutes per calendar month; 2 or more chronic conditions expected to last at least 12 months, or until the patient's death, that place the patient at significant risk of decompensation or functional decline
99439Add-on: each additional 20 minutes of clinical staff timeReported with 99490; capped at 2 units per month (a 60-minute ceiling on the non-complex tier before moving to the complex tier)
99487Complex CCM, clinical staff time, moderate or high complexity medical decision making requiredAt least 60 minutes per calendar month
99489Add-on: each additional 30 minutes of complex CCMReported with 99487 only

A separate physician-time pathway exists for practices that prefer it: 99491 covers 30 minutes personally performed by the physician or qualified health professional (not clinical staff), with 99437 as its add-on for each additional 30 minutes. A patient is billed under one tier only, non-complex or complex, never both in the same month, and never both a physician-time and a staff-time code for the same 20-minute block.

Three requirements decide whether CCM is billable at all, and each one is a real-world denial driver when it's skipped. Documented consent: the patient has to be informed — verbally or in writing — that CCM is being furnished, told about cost-sharing, and told they can stop at any time, with that conversation documented in the chart before the first month is billed; missing consent is one of the most common findings in a CCM audit. One practitioner, one patient, one month: attribution has to be unambiguous, because CMS pays exactly one practitioner's CCM claim per patient per calendar month. A patient managed jointly by a family physician and a specialist, where both offices believe they're the CCM lead, generates a real-world CO-151 denial on whichever claim processes second — this is worth a specific front-desk workflow (asking new CCM enrollees directly whether another practice already provides it) rather than discovering the conflict at denial. Time and supervision: clinical staff time has to be tracked to the minute, under general physician supervision, and the work has to be genuinely care-coordination in nature — medication reconciliation, care plan updates, communication with other providers — not simply a receptionist logging a callback.

CCM interacts with two other services worth flagging. Billed alongside an AWV in the same visit, CCM's initiating visit requirement (a face-to-face E/M, AWV, or IPPE within the year prior to starting CCM for a new enrollee) is frequently satisfied by that same AWV, which makes AWV month a natural CCM enrollment point. Billed alongside Remote Physiologic Monitoring (99453/99454/99457/99458) in the same month, the clinical staff time counted toward CCM and the time counted toward RPM must be kept separate and non-overlapping in the documentation — the same 20 minutes cannot support both codes. Full consent-language templates, the initiating-visit requirement, and the RPM-overlap documentation pattern are in our chronic care management billing guide.

Vaccine administration: 90460–90474

Vaccine administration splits into two structurally different code families, and mixing them up is a routine source of under- or over-coding in a pediatric-and-adult family panel.

Vaccine administration code families.
CodeBasisAge / counseling requirement
90460First or only component of each vaccine/toxoid, with face-to-face physician or QHP counselingThrough age 18 only; counseling required
90461Add-on: each additional component of the same vaccine (list once per additional component)Same age/counseling requirement as 90460
90471One vaccine, percutaneous/intradermal/subcutaneous/intramuscular routeAny age; counseling not required
90472Add-on: each additional vaccine, same visit, injectable routeRequires a primary administration code (90471 or 90460) same encounter
90473One vaccine, intranasal or oral routeAny age; counseling not required
90474Add-on: each additional vaccine, intranasal or oral route, same visitRequires a primary administration code same encounter

The practical difference: 90460/90461 count vaccine components, and 90471-family codes count injections. A combination vaccine with three antigens given to a counseled 4-year-old bills as 90460 plus two units of 90461 — one line per component — regardless of how many needle sticks it actually took. The same combination vaccine given to an adult, where the 90460 age restriction doesn't apply, bills as a single unit of 90471 regardless of how many antigens the combination contains, because 90471 counts the injection event, not the components inside it. Getting this backward in either direction either under-bills a pediatric visit (using 90471 when counseling and the age band actually support the higher-value component-based code) or attempts to bill 90460 on an adult, which simply isn't payable outside the pediatric/adolescent age range.

90472 and 90474 are add-on codes only — they require a primary vaccine administration code on the same claim and are never billed alone. A visit with two separate injectable vaccines for an adult bills 90471 for the first and 90472 for the second; three injectable vaccines add a second unit of 90472.

One state-specific nuance worth flagging operationally rather than as a coding rule: under the Vaccines for Children (VFC) program and many state Medicaid plans, the vaccine product itself is provided at no cost to VFC-enrolled providers, and only the administration fee (billed through the 90460/90471 family) is separately reimbursable — billing for the vaccine product itself on a VFC-eligible patient is a compliance issue distinct from a coding one, and the rules on which administration fee applies vary by state Medicaid program. Full code-by-code combination-vaccine billing scenarios and the VFC administration-fee distinction by payer type are in our vaccine administration billing guide.

In-office procedures: injections, skin, spirometry

Family medicine's minor-procedure volume is high and low-dollar individually, which makes documentation shortcuts expensive at scale — a missed drug line or a missing modifier 25 on one injection visit is a rounding error, but the same gap across a year of visits is real revenue.

Joint and bursa injections (20600–20611). These split by joint size and by whether ultrasound guidance was used, and the substance injected is always a separate line, never included in the procedure code.

The injected substance — a corticosteroid, a local anesthetic, hyaluronic acid — bills separately under its own J-code or Q-code, in addition to the procedure code; billing only the arthrocentesis code and never the drug line is a common way this service is under-billed. The ultrasound-guidance codes (20604/20606/20611) require permanent image documentation, not just that a probe was used in the room, to support the higher-value code.

Benign and premalignant skin lesion destruction. Two separate families cover different lesion types and don't cross-bill against each other for the same lesion. 17000/17003/17004 cover premalignant lesions such as actinic keratoses — 17000 for the first lesion, 17003 as an add-on for each of the second through fourteenth lesions, and 17004 replacing the whole set when 15 or more lesions are treated in one session. 17110/17111 cover benign lesions other than skin tags or vascular proliferative lesions — warts and molluscum are the common family-medicine examples — billed as 17110 for up to 14 lesions treated and 17111 for 15 or more, with no per-lesion add-on structure the way the premalignant family has one. Documentation has to specify lesion count and, ideally, location, because the code selected has to match the count actually treated, not an estimate.

Therapeutic injection administration (96372). This covers the administration of a therapeutic, prophylactic, or diagnostic drug given subcutaneously or intramuscularly — a B12 injection, a Depo-Provera injection, a corticosteroid for an acute reaction — separate from the vaccine-administration family above. 96372 is typically bundled with a same-day E/M unless the E/M represents a significant, separately identifiable service, which is the same modifier-25 logic covered throughout this guide; billed alone against a visit that was purely an injection encounter, no E/M and no modifier 25 apply. As with the joint injections, the drug administered is a separate line item under its own J-code and is never included in 96372's payment.

Spirometry (94010, 94060). 94010 covers basic spirometry — graphic recording of forced vital capacity and expiratory flow rates. 94060 covers spirometry performed before and after bronchodilator administration to assess reversibility, commonly used in asthma and COPD workups; it isn't billed alongside 94010 for the same encounter, since 94060 already includes the baseline measurement. Full code tables, ultrasound-guidance documentation requirements, and worked injection-plus-E/M billing scenarios are in our in-office procedure billing guide.

ICD-10 specificity: the codes that decide coverage

Family medicine's medical necessity denials trace back to diagnosis specificity more often than to the CPT code itself, because an unspecified diagnosis frequently passes for an initial visit and then fails to support everything that follows — a chronic-condition E/M level, CCM enrollment, or a repeat service. Three conditions carry most of the volume in a typical panel.

The pattern across all three: an unspecified or category-level code (note that N18.3 itself is a non-billable category header — the billable code is N18.30 for unspecified stage 3, or N18.31/N18.32 for 3a/3b) passes for a first encounter documenting a new problem, but fails to support ongoing chronic-condition management, CCM enrollment, or a higher E/M level once the relationship with the payer is established. A CCM claim built on E11.9 and I10 alone reads as two stable, low-acuity conditions to a reviewer; the same patient coded with E11.22 and I13.10 demonstrates the ongoing complexity CCM is actually paying for. Modifiers

Five modifiers carry real weight in family medicine, and getting them wrong either loses the claim outright or leaves it under-coded in a way nobody notices without a specific audit.

25
  • Significant, separately identifiable E/M on the same day as a preventive exam, an AWV, a vaccine, or a minor procedure.
  • Requires its own documented history, exam, and MDM for the problem — not a passing mention inside the preventive or procedure note.
59 / XE, XS, XP, XU
  • Distinct procedural service — stacked joint injections at genuinely separate sites, or lesion destruction at separate anatomic locations.
  • Prefer the specific X-modifier over generic 59 wherever it applies; XS (separate structure) covers most defensible family-medicine unbundling, since it's usually a separate joint or lesion site.
33
  • Preventive service, no patient cost-sharing under the ACA, for services on the USPSTF A/B list.
  • Signals to a commercial payer that the service should process as first-dollar-covered preventive care rather than applying a deductible or copay.
24 confused with 25
  • Modifier 24 covers an unrelated E/M by the same physician during the post-op period of a procedure with a global period (e.g., a minor in-office procedure with a 10-day global) — not on the day of the procedure itself. Modifier 25 handles same-day E/M.
  • GA/GZ — append when an ABN is on file (GA) or was not obtained (GZ) for a service Medicare may find not medically necessary, most relevant when a wellness-visit frequency limit or CCM eligibility criterion isn't clearly met.

The most common modifier error in the specialty is billing a same-day problem E/M with modifier 25 but no documentation separation to back it up — the modifier itself doesn't create the separately identifiable service, the note does. NCCI edits, MUEs, and bundling in FM

This is where family medicine billing actually gets decided at the line-item level, and it's the section every competitor guide we reviewed treats as an afterthought — gomedicalbilling.com's own NCCI table for this specialty lists Column 1/Column 2 pairs with no indicator value at all, which leaves a biller with no way to tell whether an edit can be bypassed. Two numbers control that, and neither is a guess.

The NCCI modifier indicator attached to a Column 1/Column 2 code pair:

  • 0Never bypassable. No modifier, however well-documented, overrides it. If the pair carries a 0, the second code simply isn't separately payable, full stop.
  • 1Bypassable with documentation. An NCCI-associated modifier (59 or the specific X-modifier) can override the edit, but only where the record actually shows the second service was distinct.
  • 9Edit deleted. The pair no longer applies; the indicator itself is not meaningful going forward.

The MUE Adjudication Indicator (MAI) attached to a per-code, per-day unit cap:

  • 1Claim-line edit. Units above the cap deny that line, but a genuinely repeated service can sometimes be split across separate lines with the correct modifier and documentation.
  • 2Absolute, date-of-service edit. CMS treats exceeding it as clinically implausible. There is no appeal path — confirm the MAI before staff spend time writing one.
  • 3Appealable, date-of-service edit. A real path exists with documentation showing the excess units reflect genuinely distinct, medically necessary services.

Where these show up most in family medicine: 96372's bundling into the same-day E/M is the pattern behind most CO-97 denials in this specialty — per industry-reported NCCI data, established-patient E/M codes commonly edit against 96372 with a modifier-allowed indicator, meaning modifier 25 on the E/M can legitimately unbundle it when the visit genuinely supports a separate service, but the edit exists and applies by default. A preventive exam billed with same-day vaccine counseling can similarly edit against the counseling component if it's billed as a separate E/M rather than folded into the preventive service itself. Multiple joint injections at different sites on the same date are the most common MUE trip point in this specialty — two or three small-joint injections in one visit for a patient with polyarticular symptoms can hit a unit cap on 20600/20604 that a single-joint visit never approaches, and the fix is the appropriate X-modifier per site plus documentation naming each joint, not simply adding units.

⚠️ On specificity: this build could not open CMS's primary NCCI PTP Edits file or the current MUE table directly to confirm the specific indicator and MAI values cited above (cms.gov returned an access error to automated retrieval attempts made while researching this page) — the 96372-vs-E/M pattern and the modifier-allowed classification are reported consistently by billing-industry sources but were not independently re-verified against the primary CMS files for this build. Treat every indicator and MAI value in this section as reported-but-unconfirmed and verify the current pair value in the CMS NCCI PTP Edits Lookup Tool before building it into a scrubber rule, because these values change quarterly and the lookup tool is the only source that's always current. Pro tip

Before appealing any CO-50 on a preventive or chronic-condition claim, check the diagnosis specificity first — not the CPT code. Family medicine's medical-necessity denials trace back to an unspecified diagnosis more often than to the procedure code itself, and the fix is usually a chart review for the specific condition already documented, not a phone call to the payer.

Payer-specific nuances and prior authorization

Medicare's AWV and CCM benefits are national in structure, but almost everything else in this guide varies by payer, and treating Medicare's rules as universal is a recurring source of denials on the commercial side of a mixed panel.

Commercial preventive benefits. Under the ACA, USPSTF Grade A and B preventive services must be covered by most commercial plans without patient cost-sharing when billed with modifier 33 — this is the mechanism that makes an annual physical, certain vaccines, and specific screenings "free" to the patient on paper. Coverage specifics (which exact services, which age bands) still vary by plan, and grandfathered plans are exempt from the requirement entirely, so verify eligibility rather than assuming universal first-dollar coverage.

State Medicaid variation. Well-child and well-adult visit periodicity schedules vary by state Medicaid program, and some states set their own frequency limits distinct from commercial or Medicare norms. VFC vaccine billing, covered above, layers state-specific administration-fee rules on top of the federal program — confirm your state's Medicaid vaccine administration fee schedule directly rather than assuming it matches a neighboring state's.

Prior authorization touchpoints. Family medicine's prior-auth exposure concentrates in a handful of predictable places: imaging referrals (particularly advanced imaging ordered from a primary care visit), certain injectable medications administered in-office, some DME orders, and specialty medication requests initiated at the primary care level even when the prescribing ultimately happens elsewhere. Building prior-auth verification into the referral or order workflow, rather than discovering the requirement at claim submission, is the single highest-leverage prevention step for the CO-197/CO-201 pattern above.

ABNs for AWV and CCM. An Advance Beneficiary Notice is appropriate whenever eligibility for a Medicare-covered service isn't clearly met — an AWV requested before the 11-month interval has elapsed since the last one, or CCM for a patient whose chronic-condition count or duration is genuinely borderline. Signing and filing the ABN before the service, with modifier GA appended, protects the practice's ability to bill the patient if Medicare denies; billing without one and appending GZ after the fact concedes the practice can't collect from the patient either.

Frequently asked questions

Why does Medicare deny 99397 for a wellness visit?

Medicare Part B does not cover the commercial preventive exam codes (99381-99397) at all — there is no benefit category for a routine "physical" under those codes, so 99397 denies as a statutory exclusion (CO-96 or CO-B1 depending on the payer's remark code), not a documentation problem. Medicare's preventive visit pathway runs through the G-codes instead: G0402 for the one-time Welcome to Medicare visit, G0438 or G0439 for the Annual Wellness Visit. Billing 99397 to Medicare is not a coding error you fix with a modifier; it's the wrong code family entirely.

Can we bill an Annual Wellness Visit and a problem E/M on the same day?

Yes, when the problem work is genuinely separate from the AWV's health risk assessment and prevention plan and the note documents it as its own paragraph with its own history, exam, and medical decision making. Append modifier 25 to the problem E/M and bill it alongside G0438/G0439. What doesn't work is folding chronic-condition management into the AWV's general discussion and then billing a 99214 off the same three sentences — that's the pattern payers audit hardest, because the documentation-separation requirement is the whole test.

Who can bill chronic care management for a patient, and what happens if two practices try?

Only one practitioner can bill CCM (99490, 99439, 99487, 99489) for a given patient in a given calendar month, and it requires the patient's documented consent naming that practitioner as the one furnishing it. When two practices both bill CCM for the same patient in the same month — common when a patient sees both a family physician and a cardiologist who each think they're the CCM lead — the second claim to process typically denies as a frequency or duplicate conflict (CO-151), and untangling it means confirming with the patient and the other practice who actually holds the consent on file.

Do we need a different ICD-10 code for CCM enrollment than for a routine visit?

Not a different code, but a more specific one. CCM requires two or more chronic conditions expected to last at least 12 months or until death, and the diagnosis codes on the CCM claim have to be specific enough to demonstrate that ongoing severity — E11.9 (type 2 diabetes without complications) or I10 (essential hypertension) alone often reads as stable and low-acuity to a reviewer, while E11.22 (with diabetic chronic kidney disease) or I13.10 (hypertensive heart and chronic kidney disease) demonstrates the complexity that justifies 20+ minutes of monthly care coordination. An unspecified code doesn't get the CCM claim denied outright, but it's the first thing a payer's medical reviewer pulls on if the account is audited.

Is CPT 96372 always bundled into the same-day E/M?

The injection administration itself, yes — 96372 is typically bundled with an E/M reported the same day unless the E/M represents a significant, separately identifiable service, in which case modifier 25 unbundles it. But 96372 is only the administration; the injected drug or biologic is a separate, additional line item under its own J-code or Q-code and is never included in 96372's payment. Billing the injection administration without a separate drug line is one of the more common ways family practices leave money on the table on same-day injection visits.

What's the practical difference between how 90460 and 90471 bill combination vaccines?

90460 and its add-on 90461 are component-based and require face-to-face counseling by the physician or qualified health professional, and they apply only through age 18 — a combination vaccine with three antigens bills as 90460 plus two units of 90461, one line per component, regardless of how many needle sticks it took. 90471 and its add-on 90472 are route-based and apply at any age with or without counseling — the same combination vaccine bills as a single unit of 90471 regardless of how many antigens it contains, because the code is counting injections, not components. Billing 90471 for a pediatric visit where counseling did occur under-codes the encounter; billing 90460 for an adult overbills it, because the code isn't payable outside the pediatric/adolescent age range.

What's the difference between a CO-50 and a CO-97 denial in family medicine?

CO-50 means the diagnosis on the claim doesn't support medical necessity for the service billed — the classic version in family medicine is a preventive or AWV claim where the diagnosis pointer doesn't match what the payer's policy allows, or a chronic-condition E/M billed with a symptom-only code instead of the established diagnosis. CO-97 means the service itself is bundled into another code already paid on the same claim, most often 96372 or a minor procedure billed without modifier 25 against the same-day E/M. The fix for CO-50 is diagnosis specificity; the fix for CO-97 is confirming whether the NCCI edit's modifier indicator even allows an override before appealing.
