UnitedHealthcare credentialing: one national process, several distinct networks.

UnitedHealthcare operates nationally, which removes the state-by-state fragmentation of the Blues but introduces a different complexity: multiple product networks, a separate behavioral health organization, and a demand for demographic accuracy that becomes an ongoing obligation rather than a one-time submission.

Key takeaways

  • Behavioral health routes through Optum. Mental health and substance use practitioners credential with Optum Behavioral Health Solutions, not the medical network — a different portal and a different phone number entirely.
  • Networks are product-specific. Commercial, Medicare Advantage and Medicaid participation are separate decisions, not one approval.
  • CAQH underpins the application. An incomplete, unattested, or UnitedHealthcare-unauthorized profile stalls the process before review even begins.
  • Track status yourself. The uhcprovider.com self-service tools typically show application status before any letter or email arrives.
  • Demographic accuracy is an ongoing duty. Directory information must be re-attested periodically or participation and directory listing can be affected.

How UnitedHealthcare credentialing works

UnitedHealthcare runs a centralized credentialing process through its provider portal, with onboarding handled in a guided workflow rather than paper forms mailed to a regional office. That gives more consistency than dealing with independent regional licensees the way BCBS practices must, but the network question remains separate from credentialing itself. Being credentialed does not automatically place a provider in every product network; commercial, Medicare Advantage and Medicaid plans each carry their own participation decision, and a provider can be fully credentialed and still be out of network for a product nobody explicitly requested.

It also helps to separate three things practices routinely conflate: credentialing, contracting, and network participation. Credentialing is the verification step — confirming the practitioner is who they say they are and holds the licenses, training and clean history they claim. Contracting is the negotiated agreement covering fee schedule and terms. Network participation is the specific product-level decision to include that practitioner in a given plan's directory and claims-adjudication logic. A provider can clear the first, sign the second, and still discover the third was never actually completed for a plan the practice assumed was included — which is exactly what happens when a network isn't named explicitly in the original request.

Behavioral health is administered by a related but organizationally distinct entity, Optum Behavioral Health Solutions, which runs its own credentialing pathway, its own provider portal at providerexpress.com, and its own phone line at 800-817-4705. Practices with both medical and behavioral practitioners effectively run two separate credentialing processes under one parent brand, and submitting a therapist, psychologist or psychiatrist through the medical pathway is one of the most common and most costly mistakes a behavioral health practice can make — the application does not bounce back with a clear rejection, it simply goes nowhere, and weeks pass before anyone notices nothing happened. Why the two pathways diverge, exactly what differs in the Optum application itself, and how to recognize the wrong-channel mistake before it costs a month, is in our behavioral health credentialing guide.

UnitedHealthcare also places significant emphasis on provider demographic accuracy, requiring periodic attestation of directory data, with consequences for participation where it is not maintained. This is not unique to UnitedHealthcare — federal and state directory-accuracy rules apply across payers — but UnitedHealthcare's enforcement of it through the portal is unusually direct, and a lapsed attestation is one of the quieter ways a fully credentialed, fully contracted provider ends up with claims processing incorrectly.

CAQH ProView and how UnitedHealthcare verifies it

Almost every credentialing pathway for a UnitedHealthcare-participating practitioner starts with CAQH ProView rather than a UnitedHealthcare-specific application form. UnitedHealthcare pulls licensure, education, work history, malpractice history and other core credentialing data directly from the CAQH profile, which is why an incomplete or stale profile stalls an application before UnitedHealthcare's own review has meaningfully started — there is nothing yet for a credentialing analyst to verify against.

CAQH is the data source, not the whole verification. UnitedHealthcare independently screens every applicant against sanctions and exclusion lists, confirms licensure directly with the issuing board rather than only trusting what CAQH displays, and for some facility-based specialties requires a site visit before an application can be approved regardless of how complete the CAQH profile is. A practitioner who assumes an attested CAQH profile is sufficient on its own, without separately authorizing UnitedHealthcare to access it, is often the reason an application sits with no visible movement for weeks.

Two CAQH mistakes account for most of the avoidable delay. The first is attesting a profile that still has a data gap — an unexplained period in the work-history section, a missing malpractice policy number, a professional liability question left blank — because attestation confirms the data is accurate as entered, not that every field is complete. The second is attesting the profile broadly but never granting the UnitedHealthcare-specific authorization inside CAQH that lets this particular payer pull the data at all; a profile can be fully attested and still invisible to UnitedHealthcare's credentialing team if that authorization was never checked. Full walkthrough of exactly what UnitedHealthcare verifies beyond CAQH, which specialties commonly trigger a site visit, and the specific steps that make a CAQH-based application move faster rather than stall, is in our CAQH and UnitedHealthcare credentialing guide.

Enrollment timeline and effective dates

There is no single published number for how long a UnitedHealthcare application takes, and any figure quoted without qualification should be treated skeptically. The realistic planning range runs from several weeks for a clean, fully complete application in a specialty that doesn't require a site visit, up to a few months where the CAQH profile has gaps, a development request is issued, or the specialty and location trigger additional review. Specialty matters more than most practices expect: a straightforward primary care application in a location with an open network moves differently than a facility-based specialty requiring a site inspection.

What actually sets the date a provider becomes billable is not the credentialing approval alone but the combination of credentialing completion and an executed participation agreement naming the specific product networks requested. A provider can clear credentialing and still not be billable for a network that was never named in the original request. Rather than waiting on mailed correspondence, check status directly through the self-service tools on uhcprovider.com, which is typically the fastest way to see where an application actually stands.

Practices planning a hiring timeline should treat credentialing as a parallel track that starts the day an offer is signed, not a step that begins after the physician's start date. Waiting until a new hire's first day to begin the CAQH and UnitedHealthcare process routinely means weeks or months where the physician is seeing patients whose claims cannot yet be billed as in-network, because nothing about clinical onboarding shortens the credentialing timeline. The full breakdown of what drives the timeline, how the effective and network dates are actually set, and how to use the portal's status tools instead of guessing, is in our enrollment timeline guide.

Step by step: how to do it

  1. Complete and attest CAQH ProViewFill every section, explain history gaps, upload current documents, and specifically authorize UnitedHealthcare to access the profile — attestation alone does not grant access.
  2. Determine the correct pathwayMedical practitioners go through the standard UnitedHealthcare process; behavioral health practitioners route through Optum Behavioral Health Solutions at providerexpress.com. Confirm before submitting anything.
  3. Submit the participation requestUse the UnitedHealthcare Provider Portal onboarding workflow, providing practice details, specialties, locations and the networks sought.
  4. Specify every network requiredState explicitly whether you are seeking commercial, Medicare Advantage, Medicaid, or all three. Omitting a network usually means it is simply not granted, not that it is granted by default.
  5. Complete credentialing verificationPrimary source verification of licensure, education, board certification, malpractice history and sanctions screening, plus a site visit where the specialty requires one, followed by committee review.
  6. Track status through the portal, not the mailboxCheck the self-service status tools on uhcprovider.com on a fixed schedule rather than waiting for a letter, and call Provider Services at 877-842-3210 if status has not moved in a reasonable window.
  7. Execute the participation agreementReview the contract and fee schedule, confirm the products included by name, and execute. Credentialing approval alone does not create network participation.
  8. Attest demographic data and verify the directoryConfirm addresses, phone numbers, panel status and accepting-new-patients flags, and re-attest on the required cycle going forward.

Roster management for multi-provider and multi-location practices

A single-provider practice deals with one credentialing file. A group with a dozen physicians across three locations is really managing a roster — a living list of who is credentialed, for which networks, at which locations, with which effective dates — and the portal-based process makes it easy to lose track of individual entries inside that larger list. A physician who is fully credentialed and contracted at the group's main location is not automatically credentialed to see patients at a satellite office opened eighteen months later; location has to be added and, in some cases, reviewed as its own event.

The roster problems that cost practices the most are quiet ones: a physician who left the group eighteen months ago but was never removed from the roster, so the group is still listed as their in-network billing entity in UnitedHealthcare's directory; a locum or part-time physician added to the roster for a single location who was never added for the second location they started covering; or a physician whose specialty designation on file no longer matches what they actually practice after a fellowship or a scope change. None of these produce an obvious denial the way a missing signature does — they produce a directory that's quietly wrong, and directory accuracy is exactly what UnitedHealthcare's demographic attestation cycle is designed to catch, which is why that attestation deserves a named owner inside the practice rather than being treated as a box to check when a reminder arrives.

Practices that add and remove providers regularly are better served treating roster maintenance as a standing process rather than a series of one-off applications: a single tracking sheet or system listing every practitioner, every network they're actually contracted for, every location they're credentialed at, and the date each item was last verified. Reconciling that list against the UnitedHealthcare portal on a fixed schedule — quarterly is reasonable for most groups — catches the quiet drift before it shows up as a denied claim or a demographic-attestation deadline missed because nobody remembered which locations needed updating.

Delegated credentialing for large groups and IPAs

Large medical groups, health systems and independent practice associations with enough scale can enter a formal delegation agreement with UnitedHealthcare, under which the group runs its own NCQA-compliant credentialing program internally rather than sending each individual practitioner through UnitedHealthcare's standard process one at a time. Done well, delegation dramatically shortens the effective time-to-billable for a large group, because new hires move through the group's own credentialing committee on the group's own schedule rather than queuing behind every other application UnitedHealthcare is processing nationally.

Delegation is not a lighter-touch arrangement, though. UnitedHealthcare periodically audits the delegated entity's credentialing files against NCQA standards, and a failed or marginal audit can put the delegation agreement itself at risk, which would push every practitioner in the group back onto standard, non-delegated credentialing at once. Building and maintaining a delegation-ready credentialing function requires a standing committee, defined policies, and file discipline that is simply not worth building at small scale — a five-physician practice gains nothing from delegation and should not pursue it, and the administrative overhead of running an NCQA-compliant program would exceed anything it saves in processing time.

The decision point is usually less about physician count and more about whether the group already has, or is prepared to build, the credentialing infrastructure a delegation audit expects: dedicated staff, primary-source verification capability, a functioning credentialing committee that actually meets and documents decisions, and a file-retention system that can survive an external audit without scrambling. Groups that clear that bar get a real operational advantage; groups that pursue delegation without it tend to find the audit itself becomes the largest new administrative burden they've taken on. The operational detail of how a delegation agreement is structured, what the audit actually checks, and the scale at which delegation starts to make financial sense, is in our delegated credentialing guide.

Recredentialing: staying in-network after your first approval

Initial credentialing is not a one-time event. UnitedHealthcare recredentials participating practitioners on a recurring cycle, consistent with the NCQA standards it follows, commonly around every three years. The trigger for most practitioners is CAQH re-attestation: a profile that has gone stale or unattested at the point recredentialing is due can hold up the cycle in exactly the way an incomplete profile holds up an initial application, except now there is an existing network status at risk rather than a pending one.

The consequence of missing a recredentialing deadline is not a warning letter followed by a grace period in every case — it can mean the provider's network status lapses, at which point claims that would otherwise have processed as in-network start denying or processing out of network, with a disruption to both the practice's revenue and the patient's cost share. Reinstatement after a lapse is generally a heavier lift than simply completing the recredentialing on time would have been, and in some cases resembles a fresh initial application more than a renewal.

The practical fix is the same one that works for demographic attestation: put the recredentialing due date on a calendar the day initial credentialing is approved, not the day a reminder notice arrives, because notices sent by mail or through the portal message center are easy to miss in a practice that has changed its administrative contact, moved, or simply gets a high volume of payer correspondence. A named owner checking CAQH attestation status against the known recredentialing date every quarter catches a lapse months before it becomes a network-status problem. The recredentialing cadence, exactly how CAQH re-attestation feeds into it, and what happens to claims in the window around a missed deadline, is in our recredentialing cycle guide.

Do and don't

Do
  • Route behavioral health practitioners through Optum at providerexpress.com from the outset.
  • Name every product network you require in the initial request.
  • Keep CAQH attested and specifically authorized for UnitedHealthcare throughout.
  • Check application status in the uhcprovider.com portal rather than waiting on mail.
  • Attest demographic data and complete recredentialing on the required cycles.
  • Ensure the W-9 entity name matches the billing entity exactly.
Don't
  • Don't submit behavioral health providers through the medical pathway.
  • Don't assume credentialing places you in all product networks.
  • Don't let demographic attestation or recredentialing lapse.
  • Don't bill as participating before the contract effective date.
  • Don't pursue delegated credentialing at a scale too small to support it.
  • Don't leave the directory listing unverified after approval.

Frequently asked questions

Why did our behavioral health application go nowhere?

Almost certainly because it went through the medical pathway. UnitedHealthcare administers behavioral health through Optum Behavioral Health Solutions, which runs its own credentialing and contracting at providerexpress.com. Applications for therapists, psychologists and psychiatrists submitted to the medical network are typically not processed rather than actively rejected, so they appear to stall silently. Resubmit through the Optum provider pathway, or call Optum directly at 800-817-4705.

Are commercial and Medicare Advantage separate applications?

They are separate network participation decisions, even though credentialing itself may be shared. If the initial request did not name Medicare Advantage, a credentialed provider can be in the commercial network only, with Medicare Advantage patients processing out of network. Specify every product you need at the outset, and confirm in writing which networks the executed contract covers.

How often must we attest demographic data?

On the cycle UnitedHealthcare specifies, and it is treated as an ongoing obligation rather than an administrative nicety. Directory accuracy requirements mean stale addresses, phone numbers or panel-status flags can affect participation as well as sending patients to the wrong location. Build the attestation into a recurring calendar with a named owner rather than responding to reminders.

What if the network is closed for our specialty?

A closed network is a business decision rather than a credentialing failure, so the response is commercial. Request reconsideration with a specific access argument: an underserved area, a service the network currently lacks, extended hours, or a language capability. Where patients need care meanwhile, single-case agreements can cover individual episodes. Networks reopen periodically, so it is worth revisiting rather than abandoning.

How long does UnitedHealthcare credentialing typically take?

Plan in weeks to a few months rather than a fixed number, because the real driver is completeness: a fully attested CAQH profile with no gaps moves faster than one that triggers a request for more information. Specialty, the networks requested and whether a site visit applies all shift the timeline further. Track status directly in the uhcprovider.com self-service tools rather than waiting for correspondence, since portal status typically updates before any letter arrives.

What is delegated credentialing and does our practice qualify?

Delegated credentialing lets a large group or IPA run its own NCQA-compliant credentialing program under a formal agreement, so individual practitioners are not each processed one at a time by UnitedHealthcare. It only makes sense at scale, because it requires a standing credentialing committee, a compliant file structure and regular audits by UnitedHealthcare of the delegated entity's files. A small or single-location practice is almost always better served by standard, non-delegated credentialing.

Do you handle UnitedHealthcare and Optum together?

Yes. We route each practitioner through the correct pathway, name all required product networks in the initial submission, maintain CAQH accessibility throughout, and track both applications where a practice has medical and behavioral providers. We also manage the recurring demographic attestation and recredentialing cycle, which are the obligations practices most often let slip after initial approval.

Want UnitedHealthcare credentialing handled end to end?

We prepare, submit and chase applications on a schedule, keep CAQH attested, and tell you the week each provider becomes billable.
