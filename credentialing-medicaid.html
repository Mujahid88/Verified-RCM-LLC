Medicaid credentialing: state enrollment first, managed care plans second.

Medicaid is not one payer but fifty-plus programmes, each administered by its own state agency, and most of them delegate the actual patient population to managed care organisations. That produces a two-stage process that catches practices out: enrolling with the state does not let you bill the plans, and contracting with the plans does not work until the state enrolment exists.

Find your state agency
medicaid.gov state contacts

There is no single national Medicaid phone number or enrollment portal — each state runs its own system. This CMS directory routes you to your specific state Medicaid agency's provider enrollment contact. Most Medicaid enrollees today are covered through managed care rather than fee-for-service, so you will also need each MCO's own credentialing contact separately.

MCO credentialing data
proview.caqh.org

Most Medicaid managed care plans pull credentialing information from CAQH ProView rather than a separate paper form. Keep the profile current and re-attested before applying to any plan.

Key takeaways

  • There is no national Medicaid phone line or portal. Every state runs its own system — start with the CMS state-contact directory above, not a search-engine guess.
  • State enrollment comes first. Managed care plans generally require an active state Medicaid ID before they will contract.
  • Screening levels differ by provider type. Limited, moderate and high risk categories trigger different checks including site visits and fingerprinting.
  • Every state is procedurally different. Forms, portals, timelines and revalidation cycles vary, so multi-state practices need per-state playbooks.
  • Ordering and referring providers must enrol too. A practitioner who never bills Medicaid may still need enrolment for their orders to be payable.

How Medicaid credentialing works

Medicaid enrolment begins with the state agency, which issues the Medicaid provider identifier that everything else depends on. Federal rules require states to screen applicants according to a risk category assigned by provider type: limited risk providers face licence and database verification, moderate risk adds an unannounced site visit, and high risk adds criminal background checks with fingerprinting for owners and managing employees. Knowing your category in advance tells you whether to plan for weeks or months.

That state-level enrolment does two jobs at once. It is what lets the practice bill fee-for-service Medicaid claims directly in states or for populations still billed that way, and in nearly every state it is also the prerequisite record that a managed care plan checks for before it will even open a credentialing file. Skip it, or let it lapse, and both paths close at the same time — not just the one you were actively using.

State enrollment: there is no single system to learn

Because Medicaid is federally structured but state-administered, the practical starting point is never a national form — it is finding your specific state's provider enrollment system, which may sit with the state Medicaid agency directly or with a contracted fiscal agent operating the enrollment portal on the state's behalf. A practice entering a new state for the first time should start with the CMS state agency directory above, confirm whether that state requires state enrollment before any managed-care credentialing can begin (nearly all of them do), and build the timeline around that state's own application rather than assuming familiarity with one state carries over to the next.

Portal names, application forms and even the terminology differ enough between states that a credentialing coordinator experienced in one state's system can still be genuinely slowed down by another's, and that is before accounting for state-specific quirks like whether the application must be submitted online only, whether a paper backup is accepted, or whether certain provider types route through a separate specialty-specific unit within the same agency. Full workflow for enrolling in a state you don't already work in, including how to confirm you're using the current portal rather than a retired one, is in our state-by-state Medicaid enrollment guide.

Managed care vs. fee-for-service: two credentialing processes, not one

Once the state enrolment is active, the practice contracts separately with each managed care organisation serving the population it treats. These are commercial contracting processes with their own credentialing committees, timelines and network decisions, and a plan can decline to contract even where the state enrolment is perfectly valid. Practices frequently assume state enrolment made them in-network with every Medicaid plan, then discover months of claims denying as out-of-network. Because most Medicaid enrollees nationally are covered through managed care rather than traditional fee-for-service, for the majority of practices the state Medicaid ID is a prerequisite, not the finish line, and the plan-level credentialing is where most of the actual billing depends. Some states also carve specific services — behavioral health or dental are common examples — out of managed care entirely and keep them fee-for-service even where the rest of the state's Medicaid population is in a plan, which means a single practice can legitimately be billing the same state two different ways depending on the service line. The full breakdown of what state enrollment gets you, what each MCO's own credentialing process additionally requires, and why a practice can be fully state-enrolled and still unable to bill a specific plan's patients, is in our MCO vs. fee-for-service credentialing guide.

Step by step: how to do it

  1. Identify the correct state programme and portalEach state operates its own Medicaid enrolment system under its own name. Confirm the current portal and whether your provider type enrols through the agency directly or a contracted administrator.
  2. Determine your screening risk levelProvider type determines whether you face licence verification only, an unannounced site visit, or fingerprint-based background checks. This sets the realistic timeline before you start.
  3. Complete the state enrollment applicationProvide practitioner and organisational details, NPIs, taxonomy, licensure, ownership disclosure and practice locations. Ownership disclosure requirements are stricter than most commercial payers, and inconsistent answers between sections of the same application are a common, entirely avoidable cause of delay.
  4. Complete screening obligationsWhere moderate or high risk applies, prepare for a site visit or arrange fingerprinting for owners and managing employees. Site visits are usually unannounced, so the location must always be operational and identifiable, with signage and staffed hours matching exactly what the application states.
  5. Obtain the state Medicaid provider IDThis is the identifier every managed care plan will ask for, and no plan contracting can proceed without it. Record it centrally the moment it's issued, since it will be requested repeatedly across every MCO application that follows.
  6. Contract with each managed care organisationApply separately to each plan serving your patients, usually through CAQH plus a plan-specific application. This is a network decision as much as a credentialing one, and each plan has its own committee and timeline, so build a tracker rather than relying on memory once more than one or two plans are in motion.
  7. Confirm effective dates per planEach MCO carries its own effective date, which may differ from the state enrolment date and from every other plan's date. Hold claims per plan until its own date is confirmed rather than assuming one plan's approval means the others are close behind.

Multi-state and telehealth practices

A practice or telehealth group enrolling in several states at once runs into a specific operational problem: there is no shared multi-state Medicaid application, and each state's process runs independently, on its own timeline, with its own document and screening requirements. Groups that try to run every state application in lockstep tend to lose track of which state is waiting on what; staggering applications and tracking each state's requirements separately — including whether that state accepts a multi-state license compact or insists on a state-specific license — is what keeps the process from stalling entirely. Telehealth groups in particular tend to underestimate this, because seeing a patient across a state line is a licensure and enrollment event even when nothing about the visit itself changes, and a workflow built around one home state rarely survives contact with the second or third. The full approach for practices scaling across state lines is in our multi-state Medicaid credentialing guide.

Where to go: portals and systems

Every state names its systems differently, and some split enrolment, claims and provider-file maintenance across two or three separate logins rather than one. Before assuming a system is broken or a login has expired, confirm you are on the current portal for that specific function — states migrate and rebrand these systems periodically, and old bookmarks and old guidance (including guidance a colleague picked up in a different state) are a common source of wasted time.

Documentation and licensing requirements

Exact requirements are set by each state, and they do vary, but a common baseline recurs across nearly all of them: NPI, an active state license, malpractice coverage, ownership and control disclosure, exclusion screening against the OIG and state exclusion lists, and W-9 and EFT banking details. The table below is what to have ready before you start an application in any state; the fuller state-general checklist, with the reasoning behind each item and how it differs from a typical commercial payer's document list, is in our required documents guide.

Revalidation: staying enrolled once you're in

Enrollment is not a one-time event at either level. States must revalidate enrolled providers on a cycle, most commonly a five-year federally-mandated minimum, though states vary in exactly how they schedule and notify it; managed care plans recredential separately on their own cycle, commonly every two to three years depending on the plan, so a single practice can be tracking several different clocks at once. Losing state enrollment through a missed revalidation is more than a state-level problem — because every MCO credentialing decision was built on top of that state enrollment, a lapse can cascade into losing standing with plans as well, not just the state. Notices are typically sent by mail or through the state's provider portal message centre, both of which are easy to miss if the practice's administrative contact on file is out of date, and reinstatement after a lapse is generally slower and more paperwork-heavy than simply completing the revalidation on time would have been. The full revalidation and recredentialing cycle, how the two schedules interact, and what a lapse actually does to claims already in flight, is in our Medicaid revalidation guide.

Most of these are correctable without a formal appeal — a corrected resubmission is usually faster than disputing a rejection. Where a state's decision looks genuinely wrong, or a screening result was matched to the wrong individual, states are required to offer an appeal or reconsideration path, though the rights, forms and deadlines are set at the state level and have to be confirmed per state rather than assumed from federal rule alone. Pro tip

Enrol your ordering and referring practitioners even if they never submit a Medicaid claim themselves. Many states will deny claims where the ordering or referring provider is not enrolled, which means a hospitalist or consultant who never bills Medicaid can silently cause denials for the laboratories, imaging centres and suppliers acting on their orders.

Do and don't

Do
  • Start with the CMS state-contact directory, not a search engine, when entering a new state.
  • Complete state enrollment before approaching managed care plans.
  • Determine your screening risk level before promising a timeline to anyone.
  • Keep signage, hours and address exactly as stated, since site visits are unannounced.
  • Maintain a per-state playbook if you operate across borders.
  • Re-attest CAQH on schedule so MCO applications are not stalled.
Don't
  • Don't assume state enrollment makes you in-network with Medicaid plans.
  • Don't omit any owner or managing employee from the disclosure.
  • Don't apply for a state programme without licensure in that state.
  • Don't assume another state's process transfers.
  • Don't overlook enrollment for ordering and referring practitioners.
  • Don't let a revalidation deadline pass unnoticed; reinstatement is harder than renewal.

Frequently asked questions

Why do our Medicaid claims deny when we are enrolled with the state?

Because the patient is almost certainly in a managed care plan, and state enrolment does not make you in-network with that plan. It only issues the identifier that lets you apply. Each MCO contracts separately with its own credentialing and network decision, and claims submitted before that plan's effective date deny as out-of-network. Check which plans your Medicaid patients actually carry and contract with each one.

What are the screening risk levels?

Federal rules require states to categorise providers as limited, moderate or high risk. Limited risk generally means licence verification and database checks. Moderate risk adds an unannounced site visit. High risk adds fingerprint-based criminal background checks for owners and managing employees. The category depends on provider type and history, and it is the single largest determinant of how long enrolment takes, so establish it before committing to a timeline.

How different is Medicaid between states?

Different enough that experience in one state provides limited advantage in another. Portals, forms, required disclosures, revalidation cycles and managed care landscapes all vary. A practice expanding across a state line should plan the second enrolment from scratch rather than assuming the first process transfers. Multi-state groups need a maintained playbook per state rather than one shared procedure.

Do providers who never bill Medicaid need to enrol?

Often yes, if they order or refer for Medicaid patients. Many states deny claims where the ordering or referring practitioner is not enrolled, which affects laboratories, imaging providers and suppliers rather than the ordering physician directly. That makes the denial hard to trace, because it appears in someone else's claims. Enrol all practitioners who order or refer, regardless of whether they bill.

Do you handle multi-state Medicaid enrollment?

Yes. We maintain per-state processes rather than a single template, sequence the state enrolment before MCO contracting, track screening obligations including site visit readiness, and manage each managed care application separately with its own effective date. For groups expanding across state lines this sequencing is where most of the avoidable delay is removed.

Where do I find my state's Medicaid enrollment portal?

Start with CMS's own state-contact directory at medicaid.gov, which routes you to your specific state Medicaid agency's provider enrollment contact and system rather than a stale third-party link. There is no single national Medicaid enrollment portal or phone number to call instead — every state runs its own system, and most states also require you to separately identify and contact each managed care plan operating in that state once state enrollment is active.

Want Medicaid credentialing handled end to end?

We prepare, submit and chase applications on a schedule, keep CAQH attested, and tell you the week each provider becomes billable.
