Prior authorization: the write-off that started as a scheduling problem.

A missing or lapsed authorization is one of the only denials a practice usually cannot bill the patient for, which makes it the most expensive category of avoidable loss in the revenue cycle. The fix runs entirely before the appointment, never after.

Typical turnaround
Same-day to two weeks

Set by the payer and the urgency of the request (standard vs. expedited). Confirm the current timeframe in that specific payer's policy — don't quote a patient a decision date from memory.

Appeal path
Reconsideration → peer-to-peer → formal appeal

The typical structure across commercial, Medicare Advantage and managed Medicaid plans. Exact steps, sequencing and deadlines are payer- and plan-specific.

Peer-to-peer window
Short and payer-set

Usually a matter of days after the denial letter, not weeks. Request it the day the denial arrives rather than after other options are exhausted.

Where to check requirements
Payer provider portal or phone

Many commercial payers run authorization checks through Availity or a similar shared platform. Requirements are code-specific and updated on the payer's own schedule.

Key takeaways

  • An unauthorized service is usually a full write-off, not a receivable. Most contracts treat the missing authorization as the practice's failure, so the patient cannot be billed for it.
  • Requirements are payer- and code-specific. The same procedure can need authorization from one plan and none from another, so a single shared checklist does not work.
  • An approval has an expiration date and a visit count. Care delivered after either limit is exceeded is treated the same as care delivered with no authorization at all.
  • A peer-to-peer review is usually faster than a written appeal. Request it immediately on denial; most payers set a short window to ask for one.

Why this sits upstream of billing, not inside it

Prior authorization is decided before the service is delivered, which means every failure here is locked in before a claim is ever generated. A biller who does everything else correctly, clean coding, accurate modifiers, a well-scrubbed claim, still cannot recover a service that required authorization and never got it. That is what makes this a scheduling and intake discipline first, and a billing discipline second: the work that actually prevents the loss happens days before the appointment, not after the remittance arrives.

The second reason this category is expensive is that it is frequently invisible until it is large. A single missed authorization is one denied claim. A workflow that does not track approval expiration dates and visit counts against the schedule accumulates lapses quietly across every recurring-visit patient in the practice, and the pattern usually surfaces only when someone reconciles a quarter of denials and finds the same root cause repeated dozens of times.

There's a third reason this deserves ownership separate from billing: the people best positioned to catch a requirement, the front-desk and scheduling staff, are rarely the people who feel the pain of a missed one. The denial lands on a biller's desk weeks later, disconnected from the scheduling decision that caused it, which makes the root cause hard to see without deliberately connecting the two. Practices that treat authorization checking as part of scheduling, with a named owner and a hard rule that nothing gets booked until the check is done, catch far more of this upstream than practices that treat it as a billing cleanup step.

What tends to trigger a prior authorization requirement

There is no universal list, since every payer publishes and revises its own, but certain categories of service are authorization-dependent often enough across payers that they're worth checking by default rather than by exception. Treat the table below as a starting checklist for where to look first, not a substitute for the payer's own current policy.

The workflow, step by step

  1. 1Eligibility and requirement check. Confirm active coverage and look up whether the specific CPT/HCPCS code and diagnosis require authorization under that plan, before the visit is scheduled, not after.
  2. 2Documentation assembly. Gather the clinical notes, orders and medical-necessity justification the payer's own policy asks for, since a generic submission is the most common reason for an avoidable delay.
  3. 3Submission with tracking. File through the payer's required channel, portal, fax or phone, and log a reference number immediately, not after confirmation arrives.
  4. 4Proactive follow-up. Check status on a fixed schedule (commonly every 48–72 hours) rather than waiting for the payer to respond, since silence is not the same as approval.
  5. 5Peer-to-peer coordination. On denial, request a peer-to-peer review immediately and prepare the ordering physician for the call, rather than defaulting straight to a written appeal.
  6. 6Authorization tracking to expiration. Log the approval number, the visit count authorized and the expiration date against the actual schedule, and flag it before either limit is reached.
Pro tip

For patients on a recurring plan of care — physical therapy, chemotherapy, wound care — ask the payer whether a single authorization can cover the full course of treatment instead of requesting per visit. Not every payer offers this and it varies by service, but where it's available it collapses the tracking burden from dozens of individual authorizations down to one, with only the expiration date and the visit cap left to watch.

Peer-to-peer review: the fastest way back from a denial

A peer-to-peer review is a direct phone conversation between the treating physician and the payer's medical director, offered after an initial denial and typically available before or during a formal written appeal. It exists because a written appeal has to travel through a review queue, sometimes staffed initially by non-clinical reviewers checking the submission against a policy checklist, before it ever reaches a physician who can evaluate the actual clinical picture. A peer-to-peer skips that queue entirely: it puts your physician on the phone with a physician on the other side, and a meaningful share of denials reverse in that single call because the medical director can approve on the spot once the clinical necessity is actually explained.

The reason peer-to-peer requests fail has almost nothing to do with whether the service was medically necessary and almost everything to do with who takes the call and how prepared they are. Delegating the call to a scheduler or a biller, however capable, hands the payer's medical director someone who cannot answer a clinical follow-up question, which ends the conversation in a denial by default. A physician who joins the call without the chart open in front of them is in the same position: the medical director asks about a specific exam finding or a specific failed conservative treatment, and "I'd have to check the note" is not an answer that reverses anything. And the single most common failure is arguing policy instead of medical necessity — telling the medical director the plan "should" cover this instead of explaining why this specific patient, with this specific clinical history, needs it. The physicians who win these calls consistently talk about the patient in front of them, not the payer's policy language.

Getting this right starts with treating the denial letter as urgent the moment it arrives, since most payers set a short, defined window to request the peer-to-peer at all, and it also means logging what was actually said on the call, not just the outcome, since a reversal sometimes needs to be confirmed in writing afterward before it shows up correctly on the claim. The full mechanics — exactly what to hand the physician before the call, how to actually get it scheduled quickly, and the specific reasons these calls fail even when the underlying clinical case is strong — are in our peer-to-peer review guide.

Gold-carding: when a strong track record buys you out of the process

Gold-carding is an increasingly recognized industry term for programs that exempt a provider from prior authorization on specific services once that provider has demonstrated a consistently high approval rate over time. It is not a single national program — it's a patchwork, with several states now having passed gold-carding legislation of their own and a number of payers running their own version independent of any state mandate. Where it applies, it removes the authorization step entirely for the covered services, which for a practice doing the same handful of high-volume, authorization-dependent procedures can eliminate a meaningful share of the administrative burden this page describes.

The trend line matters here even for practices whose current payer mix doesn't offer it yet. Gold-carding legislation has expanded state by state rather than arriving all at once nationally, and payers have been under sustained pressure, from regulators, from providers, and from public reporting on prior authorization burden, to expand exemption programs rather than contract them. A practice that starts tracking its own approval-rate data now is positioned to claim an exemption the moment one becomes available in its state or its payer mix, instead of finding out a year later that it would have qualified from the start.

Qualification is built around historical approval-rate data: a provider whose requests for a given service are approved at a high rate over a defined lookback period becomes eligible, with the exact threshold, lookback window and list of covered services set independently by each payer or state law. That variation is the reason a practice cannot assume gold-carding applies just because it's heard the term — it takes a direct check against the specific payers in that practice's own mix, service by service, since eligibility for one procedure with one payer says nothing about eligibility for a different procedure or a different plan.

Gold-card status is also not something a practice earns once and keeps forever. Programs typically re-evaluate eligibility on a recurring cycle, and a drop in approval rate, a change in the covered service list, or a change in state law can pull a previously exempt provider back into full prior authorization requirements without much warning. That is exactly why tracking your own approval rate by service and by payer has value on its own, independent of whether any formal gold-carding program is currently in play: it is the same data a payer would use to grant the exemption, it is leverage in a payer negotiation even absent a formal program, and it is the early-warning system that tells a practice its status is at risk before a claim gets denied under a program it assumed still applied. How to check whether your specific payer mix offers it, and how to build the approval-rate tracking that makes the case either way, is in our gold-carding guide.

When authorization is denied: appeal levels and the proceed-or-wait decision

An authorization denial and a claim denial are not the same event, even though practices often react to both the same way. An authorization denial happens before the service is delivered, which makes it a decision point rather than a loss: nothing has been billed yet, and the practice still has real options — reconsideration, peer-to-peer, a formal appeal, or a decision to reschedule. A claim denial happens after the service was already delivered and billed, at which point the cost is sunk and the only question left is whether it's recoverable at all. That distinction is why an authorization denial deserves urgency rather than dread: acted on quickly, it rarely has to become a financial loss.

The real risk in this category isn't the denial itself, it's what a practice does next. Proceeding with a scheduled service on the assumption the authorization "will probably come through" or "should apply retroactively" converts a recoverable decision point into exactly the write-off this page opened with, because most payer contracts treat a missing or denied authorization as the practice's administrative failure rather than the patient's responsibility. The appeal path itself typically runs through reconsideration, then peer-to-peer (covered above), then a formal written appeal, and in some states an external review beyond that — the exact sequence, deadlines and what each level requires varies by payer and plan type, so treat the structure as a starting map rather than a fixed script.

The one place this calculus genuinely changes is urgent or emergent care, where the clinical risk of waiting for an authorization decision can outweigh the financial risk of proceeding without one, and that's a decision the practice and the patient make deliberately and document, not one that happens by default because a schedule was already set. Documenting it means the chart shows the clinical reasoning for proceeding, not just the fact that it happened, since that documentation is often what a later appeal or an internal write-off review will actually turn on. For elective and non-urgent services, the appeal path is almost always faster and cheaper than an unauthorized write-off. The full appeal-level breakdown, how to document a proceed decision defensibly when timing genuinely doesn't allow for it, and where practices most often get this sequencing wrong, are in our prior authorization denials and appeals guide.

Tracking prior authorizations across payers without losing one

The operational reality behind most of the mistakes in the table above is simple: a practice working with a dozen payers is juggling a dozen different portals, a dozen different submission formats, and a dozen different turnaround expectations, often for the same handful of high-volume procedures. Nothing about that is unified across payers, and a workflow built around remembering it all rather than tracking it systematically is the reason lapses accumulate quietly across every recurring-visit patient in the practice until a denial audit surfaces the same root cause dozens of times over.

What a prior authorization tracker needs to capture, at minimum, for every request.
FieldWhy it matters
Submission date and methodEstablishes the clock for follow-up and is the first thing a payer's rep asks for on a status call
Payer reference / confirmation numberWithout it, every status inquiry starts from zero and takes longer
Expected decision dateTurns "waiting" into a specific date to follow up on, rather than an open-ended hope
Visits or units authorizedLets the practice track a running balance against the schedule instead of discovering the count was exceeded after the fact
Expiration dateHas to be checked against the actual appointment date, not the date the authorization was requested
Escalation trigger and ownerA named person and a specific day past which a stalled request gets escalated, rather than sitting in a queue indefinitely

The cost of losing track of any one of these fields is the same regardless of which field slipped: either the service gets delayed while the practice scrambles to re-verify status, or it gets delivered on the assumption authorization was still in force, and the practice finds out otherwise when the claim denies weeks later. The tool matters less than the discipline — a well-maintained spreadsheet reviewed daily by a named owner beats an expensive authorization module nobody actually opens, though at real volume a system that surfaces expirations automatically instead of relying on someone remembering to look is worth the cost. What a tracking system actually needs to look like, whether a spreadsheet is ever good enough, and how to build the escalation step so a stalled request doesn't sit silently until it expires, are in our authorization tracking guide.

Do and don't

Do
  • Check authorization requirements before scheduling, per payer and per code.
  • Track every approval's visit count and expiration date against the actual calendar.
  • Request a peer-to-peer immediately on denial, before considering a written appeal.
  • Re-verify authorization whenever the ordered service changes.
  • Check whether gold-carding applies to your highest-volume authorization-dependent procedures.
Don't
  • Don't assume a service doesn't need authorization because it didn't last year.
  • Don't treat an approval as open-ended; every one has a limit.
  • Don't deliver care and hope authorization "should" apply retroactively.
  • Don't let a denial letter sit before requesting peer-to-peer review.
  • Don't rely on memory for payer-specific requirements; check the current policy.

Frequently asked questions

How long does prior authorization actually take?

It varies by payer and by whether the request is standard or expedited. Commercial plans commonly decide standard requests within a few business days and expedited requests within 24 to 72 hours, but the only reliable number is the one published in that specific payer's current policy, since timeframes are revised periodically and are sometimes governed by state prompt-authorization laws layered on top of the payer's own standard.

What is a peer-to-peer review and when do we ask for one?

A peer-to-peer is a direct conversation between the ordering physician and the payer's reviewing physician after an initial denial, usually the fastest path to reversing a medical-necessity denial without a full written appeal. Request it as soon as the denial letter arrives rather than waiting to exhaust other options, since most payers set a deadline for requesting it.

What is gold-carding and does it apply to us?

Gold-carding programs exempt a provider from prior authorization for specific services once that provider has demonstrated a consistently high approval rate over a defined period, and a growing number of states and payers have adopted some version of it. Eligibility criteria, the services covered and the renewal process are entirely payer- and state-specific, so check your own payer mix rather than assuming the exemption applies broadly.

Can a patient be billed if we deliver care without authorization?

Usually not. Most payer contracts treat a missing authorization as the practice's administrative failure rather than the patient's responsibility, which makes it a full write-off rather than a collectible balance. An Advance Beneficiary Notice, where applicable and signed in advance, is one of the few ways to shift that risk to the patient for Medicare-related denials.

Do you handle prior authorization as part of your service?

Yes. We run eligibility and authorization requirements before scheduling, submit and track every request to its expiration date, and prepare providers for peer-to-peer review when a request is denied, so authorization delays stop showing up as denied claims weeks later.

What's the difference between an authorization denial and a claim denial?

An authorization denial happens before the service is delivered, so it's a decision point rather than a financial loss — the claim hasn't been billed yet, and there's still time to appeal, peer-to-peer, or reschedule. A claim denial happens after the service was already delivered and billed, which means the cost is already sunk and the only question left is whether it's recoverable. The real risk with an authorization denial is treating it like a formality and proceeding with care anyway, which converts a recoverable decision point into a claim denial with far fewer options.

Do we need a separate system to track authorizations, or does our EHR handle it?

Most EHRs store an authorization number on the encounter, but few track it the way it needs to be tracked: against the visit count remaining, the expiration date compared to the actual scheduled appointment, and an escalation trigger before either limit is hit. If your EHR's authorization module does all three and someone actually checks it on a fixed schedule, it's enough. If authorizations live in a field nobody reviews until a claim denies, that's a tracking gap, not an EHR limitation.
