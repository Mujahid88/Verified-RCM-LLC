Chiropractic Billing and Coding: The Complete Guide

Chiropractic billing lives or dies on two things almost every other specialty gets to treat as secondary: a documentation standard (PART) that decides medical necessity claim by claim, and a modifier (AT) that has to match that documentation exactly or the claim denies outright. Add a two-diagnosis coding rule most coders half-learn, dense NCCI bundling between manual therapy and adjustment codes, and a Medicare Advantage prior-authorization change that's actively denying visit 7 and beyond right now, and it's clear why this specialty denies at rates most practices never fully diagnose. This guide covers the full picture: what changed in late 2025 and 2026, the exact code families, the MAC-by-MAC coverage comparison nobody else publishes, and the documentation language that survives audit.

Reviewed by Phares A., MD · Physiotherapist, CPC (AAPC) · Updated August 2026

Key takeaways

  • Medicare covers exactly one chiropractic service: manual manipulation of the spine to correct a subluxation. Everything else a chiropractor bills — x-rays, exams, modalities, extraspinal manipulation — is statutorily excluded, not a coverage judgment call.
  • Novitas (A58345) and First Coast (A58412) retired their chiropractic billing articles 11/13/2025 as those jurisdictions transitioned to Wellpoint Federal's new article, A57889, effective 4/1/2026. No other guide we found mentions this transition.
  • Every Medicare chiropractic claim needs two ICD-10 codes — a primary subluxation code plus a secondary code for the condition it's causing — and the missing-secondary-diagnosis pattern is the single most common driver of a CO-50 denial in this specialty.
  • UnitedHealthcare Medicare Advantage now requires prior auth review past visit 6 within 8 weeks for chiropractic and outpatient therapy — live since January 2025 and actively causing denials that have nothing to do with coding accuracy.

Chiropractic billing at a glance

Four structural facts drive most chiropractic denial volume: Medicare's benefit is limited to spinal manipulation alone, the AT modifier has to match documentation or the claim denies rather than downcodes, coverage articles are written and maintained per MAC rather than nationally, and 97140 collides with the adjustment codes under NCCI more often than most practices realize. Here's the shape of it before the detail.

The rest of this guide works through every piece of that sentence in enforceable detail: what documents a subluxation, what the AT modifier actually certifies, and what happens the moment a treatment plan stops being active care.

What changed recently in chiropractic coding and coverage

Three things changed between late 2025 and mid-2026 that affect how a chiropractic claim gets built and paid, and none of them show up in the competitor guides we reviewed.

The Novitas and First Coast MAC transition. Novitas Solutions' chiropractic billing and coding article (A58345) and First Coast Service Options' equivalent article (A58412) were both retired effective 11/13/2025, confirmed directly against the CMS Coverage Database. Both jurisdictions transitioned their chiropractic coverage to Wellpoint Federal, whose new article — A57889, "Chiropractic Services – Medical Policy Article" — took effect 4/1/2026. If your practice bills into a former Novitas or First Coast jurisdiction and your reference documentation, scrubber rules, or staff training still cite the old article numbers, you're working from a retired document. Pull A57889 directly and confirm your current MAC before your next chart audit; the core PART/AT framework carries forward, but article language and update cadence do not.

UnitedHealthcare's Medicare Advantage prior-authorization expansion. UHC announced in August 2024 that chiropractic services delivered in multidisciplinary offices and outpatient hospital settings would require prior authorization effective September 1, 2024 (home-based services were excluded). The policy was refined further for authorization requests submitted on or after January 13, 2025: the first 6 visits of a new plan of care are covered without clinical review, as long as those 6 visits fall within 8 weeks of the first date of service and the patient is new to the practice, presents with a new condition, or has had a 90-plus-day gap in care. Anything beyond 6 visits or 8 weeks gets reviewed for medical necessity before it's authorized. This is live now, and it's a distinct denial category from anything coding-related — a clean, correctly-coded claim for visit 7 still denies if the plan of care wasn't submitted and authorized for that volume. Full detail on the visit-count mechanics and the ABN workflow once care transitions to maintenance is in our chiropractic prior authorization guide.

FY2026 ICD-10-CM. The current code set (effective through 9/30/2026) carries forward the M54.5 low back pain split that eliminated the non-billable M54.5 parent category: claims still have to use a billable child — M54.50 (unspecified), M54.51 (vertebrogenic, imaging-confirmed), or M54.59 (other specified) — and none of the three changed structurally for FY2026. These three sit among the most common secondary diagnoses paired with a chiropractic subluxation code, covered in the two-diagnosis section below and in full in our ICD-10 subluxation coding guide.

Medicare coverage rules and the PART documentation standard

Medicare's chiropractic benefit, under the statute governing the program, covers exactly one service: manual manipulation of the spine to correct a subluxation. That's the entire benefit. No other chiropractor-rendered service is covered — not an x-ray, not an exam, not a modality, not extraspinal manipulation — regardless of whether the service was clinically reasonable or necessary. This is a statutory exclusion, not a medical-necessity judgment call, and it's the single fact that shapes every modifier and every ABN decision in this specialty.

A subluxation has to be demonstrated, and Medicare accepts two paths: physical examination findings, or an x-ray. The physical-exam path is documented using the PART framework, referenced consistently across the active MAC billing and coding articles (WPS A56273, CGS A56455, Palmetto GBA A56616, Wellpoint Federal A57889):

  • PPain/tenderness. Evaluated by asking about the pain's location, quality, intensity, and duration, or by palpation. "Patient reports pain" alone doesn't meet the standard — document location, character (sharp, dull, radiating), and how it was elicited.
  • AAsymmetry/misalignment. Identified on a sectional or segmental level through observation or palpation — name the specific segment and the direction of the misalignment, not "spine misaligned."
  • RRange of motion abnormality. Changes in active, passive, and accessory joint movement, including hypermobility, hypomobility, or pain on motion — document which direction of movement is restricted or excessive.
  • TTissue/tone changes. Changes in the characteristics of contiguous or associated soft tissue, including spasm, hypertonicity, hypotonicity, or tenderness — note the muscle group and whether it was found on palpation.

Documentation must show at least 2 of the 4 PART elements, and at least one of those two must be A or R — P plus T alone does not satisfy the standard, because pain and tissue changes alone don't demonstrate a structural subluxation the way asymmetry or a motion restriction does. This 2-of-4-with-A-or-R rule is the single most audited documentation requirement in chiropractic Medicare billing, and it has to be present at every visit, not established once at intake and assumed to carry forward.

The x-ray alternative has its own proximity rule: imaging has to be taken within the 12 months immediately preceding, or within 3 months following, the initiation of a course of chiropractic treatment. There's a chronic-subluxation exception — a condition like scoliosis (M41.9 and its more specific children) that's documented as long-standing can rely on x-ray evidence older than 12 months, provided the record explains why the imaging remains clinically valid for the current course of care. Medicare has not required an x-ray to establish subluxation since 1/1/2000 — a properly documented PART exam is sufficient on its own, and requiring imaging for every new patient as internal policy adds cost and delay without improving claim outcomes.

Pro tip

Build a PART checklist into the note template with a required field for which two-plus elements were positive and which specific segment or muscle group each one references. A note that just says "PART positive" without naming the elements and the anatomic finding reads, on audit, exactly like a note where PART was never actually assessed.

E/M coding for chiropractors: initial visits and modifier 25

New and established patient office visits (99202–99215) are billable in chiropractic practice the same way they are anywhere else — leveled on total time or medical decision making. The complication is specific to this specialty: a same-day E/M billed alongside CMT gets scrutinized harder here than almost anywhere else in outpatient medicine, because payers assume every chiropractic visit includes some level of routine reassessment before the adjustment, and routine reassessment is bundled into the CMT code, not separately billable.

Modifier 25 on a same-day E/M is defensible in three scenarios, and only three: a genuinely new complaint unrelated to the condition already being treated, a documented exacerbation or flare that changes the clinical picture, or a formal re-evaluation of the treatment plan (typically at a defined interval, such as every 30 days or every 12 visits, depending on payer policy). It is not defensible for a routine pre-adjustment check-in — asking how the patient's been feeling and doing a quick PART recheck before manipulating is part of the CMT service, not a separate E/M.

What the note has to show to support modifier 25: a distinct history and exam addressing the new or changed complaint, medical decision making that's clearly separable from "decide whether to adjust today," and ideally a physically separate section of the note rather than MDM findings interwoven with the CMT documentation. Payers that audit chiropractic 25 usage are looking for exactly that separability. The full time-vs-MDM leveling grid and a documentation template built around this scrutiny live in our chiropractic E/M and modifier 25 guide.

Chiropractic manipulative treatment: CPT 98940–98942

These three codes are differentiated by nothing except spinal region count, drawn from five defined regions: cervical, thoracic, lumbar, sacral, and pelvic.

CMT code family by region count.
CodeRegionsMedicare-covered?
989401–2 spinal regionsYes, with AT for active/corrective treatment
989413–4 spinal regionsYes, with AT for active/corrective treatment
98942All 5 spinal regionsYes, with AT for active/corrective treatment
98943Extraspinal (see below)No — statutorily excluded, bill with GY

Only one CMT code is billed per encounter, regardless of how many distinct manipulation techniques were used within that visit — a diversified technique on the cervical spine plus an Activator adjustment on the lumbar spine is still one CMT code, selected by total region count, not two codes for two techniques.

The top audit trigger in this code family, by a wide margin, is a mismatch between the region count billed and the region-specific clinical findings documented. Billing 98942 requires clinical findings — ideally PART findings — supporting involvement in all five regions, not five regions manipulated with findings only in two. The note should read region by region: which segment in each region showed which PART elements, not a single global paragraph describing "the spine" and a code selection that assumes every region billed was actually assessed and found positive. A practice billing 98942 on close to every visit, with documentation that doesn't vary region-by-region from visit to visit, is exactly the pattern a payer's SIU or a Medicare RAC audit is built to catch.

The AT modifier is mandatory on every Medicare 98940–98942 line for active/corrective treatment — not optional, not a coding-nicety. Its absence causes the claim to deny outright as CO-4, not to pay at a reduced rate. AT specifically certifies that the treatment is reasonably expected to result in improvement, or is necessary to establish a diagnostic baseline early in a course of treatment; it does not certify that manipulation occurred, which is a common and costly misunderstanding. The complete code-by-code region documentation standard, plus worked examples of notes that pass and fail an audit, are in our CMT coding guide.

Extraspinal manipulation and timed therapy codes

98943 covers extraspinal manipulation — the extremities, rib cage, and temporomandibular joint (shoulders, knees, wrists, TMJ, and similar non-spinal structures). Medicare excludes it under any circumstance, per the MAC billing and coding articles, because the statutory chiropractic benefit is limited to spinal manipulation only; extraspinal work isn't a coverage-tier distinction, it's outside the benefit entirely regardless of medical necessity. Bill 98943 to Medicare with modifier GY so the denial posts as statutorily excluded rather than looking like a documentation failure, and collect from the patient or a secondary payer directly.

Chiropractors also bill a set of timed adjunct codes alongside CMT, and every one of them shares the same Medicare status as 98943: not covered under the chiropractic benefit, even when performed and even when medically appropriate.

  • 97110Therapeutic exercise, timed, per 15 minutes.
  • 97112Neuromuscular re-education, timed, per 15 minutes.
  • 97140Manual therapy techniques, timed, per 15 minutes — this is the code most often billed same-day as CMT, and the one that triggers NCCI bundling, covered in full below.
  • 97530Therapeutic activities, timed, per 15 minutes.

All four are billed under the standard CMS 8-minute rule: a single 15-minute unit requires at least 8 minutes of the timed service; mixed-remainder minutes across multiple timed codes performed the same visit are totaled and divided by 15 to determine total billable units, with any single code's remainder counted only if it's 8 minutes or more on its own once the largest-remainder unit has been assigned. That unit-counting logic matters for commercial payers that do cover these codes; for Medicare, it's frequently moot, because none of the four are payable under the chiropractic benefit regardless of how correctly the units are counted. If your practice performs these services on Medicare patients, they are non-covered by statute, not merely often-denied — plan the ABN and patient-responsibility conversation before the visit, not after a GY-modified claim posts.

Modalities, G0283, and x-ray coding traps

Unattended electrical stimulation has two codes describing the same clinical service, and mixing them up is a clean, avoidable denial. CPT 97014 is not separately payable to Medicare under any provider type — it was never assigned relative value units for Medicare payment purposes. G0283 is the Medicare-specific HCPCS substitute for the identical unattended e-stim service. Billing 97014 to Medicare denies every time, regardless of medical necessity or documentation quality, because the code simply isn't in Medicare's payable set. Bill G0283 wherever Medicare requires it and 97014 to commercial payers that specifically use the CPT code instead.

Both codes, like every modality and adjunct therapy code in this specialty, remain outside the chiropractic benefit for Medicare regardless of which code is used — the G0283-vs-97014 distinction only matters for correct code selection when billing a non-Medicare payer, or for accurately posting the GY-modified denial to Medicare so secondary billing proceeds cleanly.

Spinal x-rays support subluxation documentation under the imaging alternative described above, using the applicable spinal radiography CPT code family for the region imaged (paraphrased here, not reproduced from the CPT manual — confirm exact descriptors and units against your CPT Professional edition or encoder). The trap: since 1/1/2000, Medicare has not required an x-ray to establish a subluxation at all — a properly documented PART exam is sufficient on its own. Practices that treat imaging as a prerequisite for every new chiropractic patient are adding cost, radiation exposure, and administrative delay without any coding or coverage requirement driving it. Order imaging when it's clinically indicated, not as a billing formality.

ICD-10 coding: the two-diagnosis subluxation rule

This is the single coding rule most responsible for chiropractic medical-necessity denials, and it's the one competitor guides handle shallowest or skip entirely. Every Medicare chiropractic claim needs two diagnosis codes working together, not one:

  1. A primary nonallopathic lesion/subluxation code identifying the affected spinal region, verified billable in the current ICD-10-CM code set.
  2. A secondary ICD-10 code identifying the neuromusculoskeletal condition the subluxation is causing — the actual clinical reason manipulation was medically necessary on that date of service.

The primary code comes from one of two region-based series, both confirmed billable against the current code set:

Most MAC chiropractic articles describe the two series as functionally interchangeable for establishing the subluxation diagnosis — the distinction between "segmental and somatic dysfunction" (M99.0x) and "subluxation complex, vertebral" (M99.1x) is largely a documentation-style difference rather than a coverage one, but confirm your specific MAC's article language, since phrasing varies by contractor. M99.9 (biomechanical lesion, unspecified) is also verified billable, but it's a documentation-specificity red flag whenever the note actually names a region — if the exam documents cervical PART findings, the claim should carry M99.01 or M99.11, not M99.9. Unspecified codes on a claim where the note supports specificity are exactly what a payer's coding-accuracy review is built to flag.

Two traps sit on either side of this rule. Under-coding: billing the primary subluxation code alone, with no secondary diagnosis at all, leaves the claim with no stated reason manipulation was necessary — this is a fast track to CO-50. Over-specifying without support: billing a highly specific secondary code (say, M54.51 vertebrogenic low back pain) when the note doesn't actually document the imaging finding that supports it is equally risky on audit, even though the claim may pay initially. The secondary code has to be exactly as specific as the documentation supports, no more and no less. Modifiers: AT, GA, GY, GZ, 25, and 59/XS

Chiropractic billing runs on a small, high-stakes modifier set, and getting the wrong one on a claim is rarely a minor error — it's usually the difference between paid, denied-and-appealable, and denied-and-uncollectable.

Chiropractic modifier reference: when to use each, and where it gets audited.

The throughline across all six: every one of these modifiers makes a specific factual claim about the encounter, and every one of them is checkable against the note. AT claims the treatment was active and expected to help. GA claims a signed ABN exists. GY claims the service is categorically excluded. 25 claims a separable E/M happened. 59/XS claims a genuinely separate region was treated with manual therapy. None of them are administrative formalities — they're representations a payer or auditor can and does check against documentation after the fact. The full decision tree, plus a same-visit worked claim example walking through all six, is in our chiropractic billing modifiers guide.

NCCI bundling and MUE limits for chiropractic codes

97140 is a Column 2 code bundled into the CMT codes (98940–98942) under NCCI Procedure-to-Procedure edits whenever both are performed in the same anatomic region during the same encounter. The logic behind the bundle: manual therapy technique overlaps substantially with the manipulation itself when applied to the same region, so CMS treats the manual therapy as included in the adjustment rather than separately payable there.

Unbundling is possible — this is not a hard-0 edit — but only with modifier 59 or the more specific XS (separate structure), and only where the documentation independently supports it: the manual therapy has to target a region genuinely distinct from the region(s) adjusted, with its own clinical findings justifying the manual therapy and its own timed-minutes documentation separate from the CMT note. A common defensible pattern: CMT to the cervical and thoracic spine (98941, AT) plus 97140 manual therapy to the ipsilateral shoulder girdle for a documented soft-tissue restriction unrelated to the spinal adjustment — genuinely separate structures, separately justified. A common indefensible pattern: CMT to the lumbar spine plus 97140 "manual therapy to the lumbar paraspinals" on the same visit with no distinct finding beyond what already supports the adjustment — same region, same rationale, not separable no matter what modifier is appended.

Full technical detail on the specific edit pair, its NCCI modifier indicator, and worked documentation examples that survive versus fail a payer audit are in our NCCI bundling and 97140 guide.

MUEs (Medically Unlikely Edits) cap units of a single code payable on one date of service. For chiropractic CMT specifically, the practical limit chiropractors hit daily isn't a published high number — it's the one-CMT-code-per-encounter billing rule itself, which functions as the real-world unit cap regardless of the code's published MUE value. ⚠️ This build could not open CMS's primary NCCI MUE table directly to confirm current published MUE unit values for 98940–98943 and 97110/97112/97140/97530 against the source (CMS.gov coverage-database and NCCI pages returned access errors to automated fetch attempts made while researching this page). Treat any specific unit-cap number for these codes as unconfirmed here, and check the current value in the CMS Medically Unlikely Edits (MUE) Tool by name before building a scrubber rule against it — MUE values are republished quarterly.

Top chiropractic denials and how to appeal them

Four denial codes account for most of the recoverable and non-recoverable chiropractic denial volume, and each one has a different correct response — treating all four the same way wastes staff time on unwinnable appeals and misses winnable ones.

Real appeal letter structure for a CO-50 medical-necessity denial: open by citing the specific MAC article or LCD by document ID (for example, "per WPS Insurance Corporation Billing and Coding: Chiropractic Services, Article A56273"), restate the exact PART findings documented at the visit in question by element and region, state the primary and secondary ICD-10 codes and how the secondary code establishes the clinical reason for treatment, and attach the treatment plan showing measurable improvement across visits — a decreasing pain scale, an expanding range of motion, a functional milestone met. For a maintenance-care denial where GA was correctly applied, the appeal is different in kind: it's not a request to overturn the denial, it's a demonstration that GA was applied correctly and the balance is properly patient responsibility, with the signed, service-specific ABN attached as proof.

Medicare, Medicare Advantage, and commercial payer nuances

"The article says" is close to meaningless in chiropractic billing without naming which MAC's article, because coverage documentation is written and maintained per Medicare Administrative Contractor, not nationally. We confirmed this directly against the CMS Coverage Database: four active chiropractic billing and coding articles and two LCDs are currently in force, plus two articles retired within the past year.

The full comparison, including how to identify your jurisdiction's MAC, is in our Medicare vs. Medicare Advantage vs. commercial coverage guide.

Medicare Advantage plans inherit Medicare's active-vs-maintenance limitation on chiropractic coverage but administer it through their own utilization review process rather than the PART/AT framework directly — which is exactly why UnitedHealthcare's prior-authorization expansion, covered above, layers on top of the underlying Medicare coverage rule rather than replacing it. Commercial payers generally cover CMT without importing Medicare's active-versus-maintenance distinction at all, but nearly every commercial plan applies its own visit caps (a fixed annual visit count is common) and its own modifier requirements, which frequently do not match Medicare's AT/GA/GY/GZ set. Never assume a commercial payer's chiropractic policy mirrors Medicare's just because the underlying CPT codes are identical — verify the specific plan's visit limit and required modifiers before the first visit, not after a denial.

Prior authorization and visit limits

UnitedHealthcare's Medicare Advantage prior-authorization expansion for chiropractic and outpatient therapy is the clearest current example of a denial category that has nothing to do with coding accuracy. Under the policy refined effective for authorization requests submitted on or after January 13, 2025: the initial evaluation itself does not require prior authorization, the first 6 visits of a new plan of care are covered without clinical review as long as they fall within 8 weeks of the first date of service, and any visits beyond that 6-visit/8-week threshold require the full plan of care to be submitted and reviewed for medical necessity before they're authorized. A correctly coded, correctly documented claim for visit 7 still denies if the extended plan of care wasn't submitted and cleared in advance.

Beyond UHC's specific policy, Medicare Advantage plans broadly set their own annual chiropractic visit limits — commonly in the 20-to-30-visit range, though some plans offer unlimited visits when medical necessity is documented, and this varies materially by plan design. Confirm the specific plan's visit limit and prior-auth trigger before treatment begins, not after a denial arrives; eligibility and benefits verification for chiropractic specifically should include this check as standard, not as an exception-handling step.

When a course of active treatment plateaus and shifts to maintenance care, the correct workflow is sequential, not simultaneous: recognize the plateau in the documentation (no further expected improvement in the PART findings or functional status), have the patient sign a service-specific ABN before the next visit describing exactly what's expected to be denied and why, stop appending AT and start appending GA on 98940–98942 going forward, and continue billing Medicare with GA so the expected denial posts and the ABN converts the balance to patient responsibility. Skipping the ABN step and just switching to GA doesn't protect the practice; skipping the switch to GA and continuing to bill AT once documentation no longer supports active treatment is upcoding, plain and simple. Frequently asked questions

Does Medicare cover chiropractic care beyond spinal adjustments?

No. The Medicare chiropractic benefit is statutorily limited to manual manipulation of the spine to correct a demonstrated subluxation. X-rays, exams, extraspinal manipulation (98943), physical therapy modalities, and any other service a chiropractor performs are all excluded from the chiropractic benefit, even when medically reasonable, because the exclusion is written into the statute itself, not a coverage judgment call. Bill 98943 and any adjunct therapy codes with modifier GY so the denial posts correctly to secondary insurance or the patient.

What is the PART exam and how many elements does Medicare require?

PART stands for Pain/tenderness, Asymmetry or misalignment, Range of motion abnormality, and Tissue/tone changes. Medicare's chiropractic billing and coding articles require at least 2 of the 4 elements to be documented, and at least one of the two must be A or R — Pain plus Tissue/tone alone does not meet the standard. Each element needs a specific finding, not a checkbox: which segment, which direction of restriction, which muscle group.

Why do our 97140 claims deny when billed with a CMT code?

97140 is a Column 2 code bundled into 98940-98942 under NCCI PTP edits whenever both are performed in the same anatomic region. The bundle can be overridden with modifier 59 or the more specific XS, but only when the manual therapy targeted a genuinely separate region from the adjustment and the note documents the region and the timed minutes independently. Appending 59 without that documentation is the single most commonly reversed pattern in a chiropractic NCCI audit.

When does a chiropractic claim need the GA modifier instead of AT?

GA replaces AT the moment care crosses from active/corrective treatment into maintenance therapy — when the documentation no longer supports expected improvement in the patient's condition. GA signals that an Advance Beneficiary Notice was signed before the service and the expected denial should post as patient financial responsibility (PR-204), not a payer error. Billing AT past the point documentation supports it is upcoding; billing GA without a signed ABN on file voids the patient-liability protection entirely.

Why did our Novitas or First Coast chiropractic billing article stop matching what pays?

Novitas Solutions (article A58345) and First Coast Service Options (A58412) both retired their chiropractic billing and coding articles on 11/13/2025 as their jurisdictions transitioned to Wellpoint Federal. Wellpoint Federal's replacement article, A57889, took effect 4/1/2026. If your scrubber rules or reference documentation still cite the old article numbers, confirm your current MAC and pull A57889 directly rather than relying on a retired document.

Does a chiropractic claim need two ICD-10 codes to pass Medicare medical necessity?

Yes, as the standard billing pattern under the MAC chiropractic articles: a primary nonallopathic lesion/subluxation code from the M99.00-M99.05 or M99.10-M99.19 series identifying the affected spinal region, plus a secondary ICD-10 code identifying the neuromusculoskeletal condition the subluxation is causing. The primary code alone does not establish why manipulation was medically necessary on that date of service; the secondary code does. Missing or mismatched secondary diagnoses are one of the most common drivers of a CO-50 denial in this specialty.

Is G0283 the same thing as CPT 97014 for Medicare?

They describe the same clinical service — unattended electrical stimulation — but only G0283 is payable to Medicare; 97014 is not separately payable under the Medicare Physician Fee Schedule for any provider type, and even where it is payable to other specialties, both remain outside the chiropractic benefit entirely, since Medicare does not cover any chiropractor-rendered service beyond CMT. Bill G0283 to non-Medicare payers that specifically request it; append GY to any adjunct modality billed to Medicare.

How many spinal regions can one CMT code cover?

98940 covers 1-2 regions, 98941 covers 3-4 regions, and 98942 covers all 5 regions (cervical, thoracic, lumbar, sacral, pelvic). Only one CMT code is billed per encounter regardless of how many separate manipulation techniques were used within that visit, and the region count billed has to match region-specific findings documented in the note — billing 98942 without five regions of distinct clinical findings is the top upcoding trigger auditors look for in this code family.
