Orthopedic Medical Billing and Coding: The Complete 2026 Guide

Orthopedics runs on global periods, compartment-specific arthroscopy codes, and imaging-gated joint injections — and most guides to it stop at telling you those things exist without ever naming the actual NCCI indicator, the actual LCD, or the actual code pair that trips the edit. This guide names them: the specific PTP relationships (29826 into 29827, G0289 into 29881/29880, 76942 into 20611), three current LCDs by number and MAC, and the exact modifier that rescues a claim the payer's scrubber flagged as a duplicate. It covers the full revenue cycle for the specialty — E/M, fracture care, injections, arthroscopy, DME, ICD-10 specificity, prior auth, and appeals — with the codes verified live against the FY2026 ICD-10-CM set and the CMS Coverage Database.

Reviewed by Phares A., MD · Physiotherapist, CPC (AAPC) · Updated August 2026

Key takeaways

  • 76942 is bundled into 20611, not billable alongside it. If ultrasound guidance is used for a joint injection, 20611 already includes it — billing 76942 on the same line is what most orthopedic in-office claims get wrong about this pair.
  • 29881 reported twice for a two-compartment meniscectomy is a hard denial, not an edge case. The correct code for both compartments in the same knee is 29880, reported once.
  • Modifiers 78 and 79 are frequently swapped, and it costs real money. 78 (unplanned return for a complication) pays at the intraoperative-only rate and doesn't restart the global clock; 79 (unrelated procedure) pays in full and starts a brand-new one.
  • CJR-X was finalized July 31, 2026 as a nationwide mandatory bundled-payment model for hip and knee replacement (MS-DRG 469/470/521/522, HCPCS 27130/27447), effective October 1, 2027 — every guide we reviewed either skips it or still calls it "proposed."

Where orthopedic practices lose revenue (at a glance)

Orthopedics denies at a high rate for a specific reason: it stacks more billing complexity per encounter than almost any other specialty. A single visit can carry a global-period E/M question, an imaging-guidance modifier decision, a compartment-specific arthroscopy code, and a laterality-specific diagnosis all at once — and any one of those, coded wrong, denies the whole line. Before the code-by-code detail, here's where the dollars actually go.

What changed for orthopedics in 2026

Two developments actually change how orthopedic claims get billed and paid this year; a third is worth knowing about even though it doesn't touch your practice's own CPT coding directly.

2026 developments relevant to orthopedic billing.

The practical read: CJR-X is the headline, but it's a hospital-accountability model, not a line-item change to how your practice codes a knee replacement visit. E/M coding for orthopedic visits: new patient, established, and global-period exceptions

Orthopedic E/M leveling runs on the same MDM-or-time framework as every other specialty, but the presentations are distinctive enough to walk through directly. A new patient with acute knee pain after a fall, worked up with an x-ray order and a differential that includes fracture versus ligament injury, typically supports moderate MDM (99204) on the strength of an acute complicated injury plus an independent imaging interpretation if the physician personally reviews the films. An established patient returning for osteoarthritis management with a stable medication plan and no new complaint is usually a straightforward-to-low encounter (99212–99213) unless a new finding — a flare, a new joint, a medication side effect — pushes it to moderate.

Where orthopedic E/M coding actually gets hard is inside a global period, because so much of the specialty's volume is post-surgical or post-fracture follow-up. Three modifiers govern whether an E/M is separately payable at all.

Modifier 24
  • Unrelated condition, same physician, during the post-op window of a 000/010/090-day global procedure.
  • Needs its own distinct ICD-10 code on the E/M line, not the surgical diagnosis.
Modifier 25
  • Significant, separately identifiable E/M, same day as a minor procedure (000/010-day global) — e.g., an E/M plus a same-day joint injection.
  • The note must stand on its own as billable E/M work, not just describe the procedure decision.
Modifier 57
  • The decision-for-surgery visit, day of or day before a major (090-day global) procedure.
  • Distinguishes "we decided today to operate" from routine pre-op planning already bundled into the surgical fee.

Worked example: a patient in a 90-day global period for an ankle ORIF returns three weeks later with a new, unrelated ankle sprain on the opposite side — or a genuinely new problem like shoulder pain from a separate fall. Bill 99213-24 with the distinct ICD-10 for the new complaint (not the ORIF diagnosis), and document exam findings and MDM specific to the new problem. What breaks this claim in practice: coders reflexively bundle every visit in a global window into "no separate bill" without checking whether the presenting complaint is actually related to the surgery. It frequently isn't, and that's real, recoverable revenue left on the table — the opposite failure mode from the audit risk of over-using 24 on visits that genuinely are related follow-up.

Fracture care coding and global periods (10-day vs 90-day)

Fracture treatment coding is selected by method, not just anatomy, and the method selected is what sets the global period. A closed fracture treated without manipulation is typically a minor global (000 or 010 days); closed treatment with manipulation and open treatment (ORIF) are typically major, 090-day globals. This single decision — manipulation or not, closed or open — cascades into how much of the following three months of care is bundled into that one fracture code versus separately billable, and getting it wrong at the point of initial coding is the single biggest driver of fracture-care revenue leakage we see. For the complete code-by-code breakdown, the global-period logic by fracture site, and the full worked examples, see our Fracture Care Coding and Global Periods in Orthopedics guide.

What's bundled into the global fracture-care fee, without exception: routine follow-up visits related to the fracture, standard cast or splint changes, and follow-up x-rays taken to confirm routine healing progress. None of these generate a separate line item, and billing them separately is one of the most reliably audited patterns in fracture-care claims, because it shows up as a pattern across a provider's entire fracture-care volume, not just one claim.

What breaks the global bundle and is separately billable, each with its own modifier:

  • 54/55/56Split care. 54 is surgical care only, 55 is post-operative management only, 56 is pre-operative management only — used when different physicians (or different practices) handle different phases of a fracture's global period. Requires the expanded 2026 transfer-of-care documentation discussed above.
  • 58Staged or planned procedure. A planned second surgical stage related to the original — a scheduled hardware removal already anticipated at the time of the original ORIF, for example.
  • 78Unplanned return to the OR for a complication. A related, unplanned re-operation — infection washout, hardware failure — reimbursed at the intraoperative-only portion of the fee, and the original global period keeps running rather than resetting.
  • 79Unrelated procedure, same physician, during the global period. A genuinely unrelated surgery on the same patient during the fracture's global window — this one starts its own fresh global period.

The full decision tree for choosing between 58, 78, and 79 — including worked scenarios for each — is covered in depth in our Modifiers 58, 78, and 79: Staged, Unplanned, and Unrelated Procedures guide.

Diagnosis specificity matters as much as procedure selection here, and it's where a lot of otherwise-correct fracture claims still deny on follow-up imaging. S82.001A (unspecified fracture of the right patella, initial encounter for closed fracture) validates as billable, but it's the least specific option available — ICD-10-CM also offers fracture-type-specific codes within the same S82.0 family, and using the unspecified variant when the operative note actually documents a displaced or comminuted pattern is a self-inflicted denial risk on any repeat imaging ordered later in the global period. A payer's medical-necessity review for a follow-up CT or repeat x-ray checks whether the diagnosis on file still supports imaging at that point in the healing timeline — an unspecified code gives the reviewer nothing to anchor that judgment to, where a specific, documented fracture pattern does. Code to the highest specificity the operative note actually supports on the very first claim, not just the follow-ups.

Joint injections and aspiration: 20610 vs 20611 and the ultrasound-guidance trap

This is the single most miscoded orthopedic in-office code, and the volume makes the error expensive even though each individual instance is small. 20610 reports arthrocentesis, aspiration, or injection of a major joint or bursa without imaging guidance. 20611 reports the identical clinical service performed with ultrasound guidance — and 20611 carries a documentation requirement that 20610 doesn't: permanently saved images plus a separate interpretive report in the chart. If the physician used ultrasound to confirm needle placement but didn't save an image or dictate a brief interpretation, the documentation doesn't actually support 20611, regardless of what happened at the bedside. The complete code family — including the small- and intermediate-joint equivalents and the full documentation checklist — is in our Joint Injection and Aspiration Billing: 20610 vs 20611 guide.

The NCCI trap sits right next to it: 76942 (ultrasound guidance for needle placement, reported alone) is bundled into 20611. Since 20611's own value already accounts for the guidance, billing 76942 on the same line for the same joint is not a documentation gap you can fix with a modifier — it's a code you shouldn't be billing at all in that scenario. ⚠️ Multiple billing-industry sources describe this pair as non-bypassable under NCCI; this build could not open CMS's primary NCCI PTP edit file directly to confirm the current-quarter modifier indicator (CMS's site returned an access error to automated fetches during this build), so confirm the live indicator value in the CMS NCCI PTP Edits Lookup Tool before finalizing a scrubber rule.

Bilateral billing is where this code family splits by payer type, and naming that split explicitly is worth doing because guessing wrong either denies the claim or underpays it silently:

Pull the current version of that article before billing a hyaluronan series, because it specifies the diagnosis and prior-treatment criteria WPS actually checks against, and a claim submitted without matching them denies as not medically necessary regardless of clinical appropriateness.

Knee arthroscopy coding and NCCI bundling (29880/29881 family)

Compartment logic is the whole story with knee arthroscopy meniscectomy coding, and it's a frequent, expensive unbundling error. 29881 reports a meniscectomy in a single compartment — medial or lateral. 29880 reports a meniscectomy addressing both compartments of the same knee. The rule that gets broken constantly: 29881 is never reported twice for a two-compartment case. If both compartments were treated, the correct code is 29880 once, not 29881 billed on two lines. This is exactly the kind of pattern that draws payer attention, because it looks like an attempt to double the reimbursement for what's actually a single, higher-valued combined code. The full code family, worked scenarios, and every current NCCI relationship in knee arthroscopy is in our Knee Arthroscopy CPT Codes: 29880, 29881, and NCCI Bundling guide.

Diagnostic arthroscopy (29870) is inherently part of the surgical decision-making for any therapeutic knee arthroscopy performed in the same joint, same session — it's not separately billable alongside a therapeutic code like 29880/29881 for that same knee. Every knee scope starts as a diagnostic look before the surgeon decides what to treat; that diagnostic component is built into the value of the therapeutic code and isn't reported on its own line.

G0289 is the code that trips up practices most often in this family. It reports removal of a loose or foreign body, or chondroplasty, performed in a different compartment than the primary arthroscopic procedure, during the same operative session. It is only separately billable when the compartment is genuinely different from where the primary procedure occurred — the operative note has to document compartment location for both the primary procedure and the G0289-reported work, or the distinction isn't supportable. Where a PTP edit exists between G0289 and the primary code billed, modifier 59 or the more specific XS ("separate structure") is what unbundles it — but only where the documentation actually shows the different-compartment finding; some code pairs in this family carry no PTP edit at all, in which case no override modifier applies or is needed.

Understanding what the NCCI modifier indicator on a pair actually means is what separates a defensible override from an audit flag:

  • 0Never bypassable. No modifier, however well-documented, changes the outcome — the second code in the pair simply isn't separately payable.
  • 1Bypassable with documentation. An NCCI-associated modifier (59 or the specific X-modifier) can override the edit where the record genuinely shows the second service was distinct — different compartment, different session, different structure.
  • 9Edit deleted / not applicable. The pair no longer carries an active edit.

⚠️ The 29826/29827 and G0289 relationships described in this guide and the arthroscopy sections below are stated using the modifier-indicator framework above and reflect what's consistently reported across current orthopedic coding references; this build could not independently re-confirm the specific current-quarter indicator digit for every individual pair against CMS's primary NCCI PTP file (CMS.gov blocked automated retrieval during this build). Verify the live indicator for any specific pair in the CMS NCCI PTP Edits Lookup Tool before relying on it operationally — these values are reviewed and can change quarterly.

Shoulder arthroscopy and rotator cuff repair coding

29827 (arthroscopic rotator cuff repair) and 29826 (arthroscopic subacromial decompression) are the pairing that generates the most same-session billing questions in shoulder arthroscopy. 29826 is commonly bundled into 29827 under NCCI when both are performed in the same subacromial space during the same session — decompression is frequently an inherent part of exposing and repairing the cuff. It becomes separately payable with modifier 59 or the more specific XU only where the documentation supports a genuinely distinct decompression beyond what rotator cuff repair itself requires; billing 29826 on every rotator cuff repair claim as a matter of habit, rather than as a documented separate clinical decision, is a pattern payers flag.

Diagnosis specificity on rotator cuff claims decides more than most coders expect, because the ICD-10 code itself has to match the documented mechanism of injury. M75.121 and M75.122 (complete rotator cuff tear or rupture, right and left shoulder, not specified as traumatic — both confirmed billable against FY2026) are appropriate for degenerative, chronic presentations. S43.421A (sprain of the right rotator cuff capsule, initial encounter — also confirmed billable) is the traumatic-injury code, appropriate when the chart documents a specific inciting event: a fall, a lifting injury, a defined mechanism. Payers increasingly deny claims where a traumatic injury (a fall, a motor vehicle accident) is coded with the non-traumatic M75 family, or where a chronic, insidious-onset cuff tear is coded with a traumatic S43 sprain code — the documentation of mechanism has to support whichever family is billed, not just the anatomic diagnosis.

Modifiers 58, 78, 79, 59/X{EPSU}: the global-period and unbundling toolkit

These are mutually exclusive and each maps to a distinct clinical scenario — the confusion between them, especially 78 versus 79, is one of the costliest recurring errors in orthopedic global-period billing.

58 — staged/planned
  • Same physician, related to the original, planned at the time of the first procedure — a scheduled two-stage ligament reconstruction.
  • Starts a new global period for the staged procedure itself.
78 — unplanned, related
  • Same physician, unplanned return to the OR for a complication of the original procedure — a post-op infection washout.
  • Reimbursed at the intraoperative-only rate; the original global period keeps running, it does not reset.
79 — unrelated
  • Same physician, genuinely unrelated procedure during the global period — a new fracture on the opposite limb.
  • Paid in full and starts its own brand-new global period, independent of the original.

Worked example: a patient three weeks out from a knee arthroscopy returns with a wound infection requiring irrigation and debridement in the OR — that's modifier 78, related, unplanned, intraoperative-only payment, original global period continues. The same patient returning instead with a new rotator cuff tear from an unrelated fall gets modifier 79 on the shoulder procedure — unrelated, paid in full, its own new 90-day clock starts. Billing the infection washout as 79 (over-collecting relative to the correct rate) or the new shoulder injury as 78 (under-collecting and incorrectly tying it to the knee's global period) are both errors we see regularly, and both are avoidable with one question: is this related to the original procedure, and was it planned? The complete worked-example set for all three modifiers is in our Modifiers 58, 78, and 79 guide.

Modifier 59 and the X-modifiers serve a different purpose entirely — they don't touch global periods, they override NCCI bundling edits. Medicare's own guidance prefers the specific X-modifiers over blunt 59 wherever one applies:

  • XESeparate encounter — a distinct service during a separate patient encounter.
  • XSSeparate structure — the most common orthopedic case: a shoulder scope plus a separate, unrelated knee injection performed the same visit, for example.
  • XPSeparate practitioner — a distinct service performed by a different provider.
  • XUUnusual, non-overlapping service — the catch-all for a genuine distinction that doesn't fit the other three.

Use plain 59 only when none of the four X-modifiers accurately describes the distinction. In every case, the chart has to independently document the separateness — site, session, structure, or practitioner — at the time of the encounter, not reconstructed later to justify a denied claim.

Total joint replacement, bundled payment models, and DRG basics

Total hip and knee replacement sit at the center of orthopedics' biggest payment-model shift in years. CJR-X — the Comprehensive Care for Joint Replacement Expanded model — is a mandatory, nationwide bundled-payment model finalized July 31, 2026 as part of the FY2027 IPPS Final Rule. It's triggered by inpatient stays under MS-DRG 469 (major hip/knee joint replacement with major complication or comorbidity, or total ankle replacement) and 470 (the same procedures without MCC), plus MS-DRGs 521 and 522, and by outpatient procedures billed under HCPCS 27130 (total hip arthroplasty) and 27447 (total knee arthroplasty). Each triggers a 90-day post-discharge episode during which the hospital carries financial accountability for the full spend — Part A and Part B, hospital and physician and post-acute — against a target price. Full episode mechanics, the participation requirements, and what it means operationally are covered in our Total Joint Replacement Billing Under CJR and Bundled Payment Models guide.

The financial risk in CJR-X sits with the hospital, not your orthopedic practice — your own CPT and E/M billing for the surgeon's professional services doesn't change under the model. What does change is coordination pressure: post-acute care, physical therapy, and any follow-up visits inside that 90-day window are being tracked against the hospital's bundle target, and duplicate or uncoordinated billing for services already inside the episode window draws scrutiny even when each individual claim is technically correct. If your practice does any joint-replacement volume with a participating hospital, expect tighter data-sharing requests and post-acute care coordination requirements starting well before the October 2027 effective date, because hospitals build their episode-management infrastructure ahead of the compliance date, not on it.

DME billing: braces, walking boots, and orthoses

Walking boots are the single most common DME item an orthopedic practice dispenses, and the HCPCS split on them is a hard-denial trap rather than a documentation nuance. L4360, L4361, L4386, and L4387 all describe prefabricated ankle-foot orthoses (walking boots), differentiated by pneumatic/vacuum mechanism versus non-pneumatic, and by whether the item was customized to the patient by someone with fitting expertise versus dispensed off-the-shelf. L4631 covers an ankle-foot orthosis with a full walking boot shell and interface, used when a more rigid, higher-support configuration is indicated. Full code-by-code selection logic, plus the fitting-expertise documentation the "customized" codes require, is in our DME Billing for Orthopedic Braces, Boots, and Orthoses guide.

A9283 is a different code entirely, and using it interchangeably with the L4360–L4387 family is the single most consequential coding error in this category: A9283 applies only when the boot is dispensed solely for pressure reduction or pressure-ulcer prevention, not as an orthopedic brace for a fracture, sprain, or post-surgical joint. Under that indication, Medicare frequently treats the item as non-covered with no applicable benefit category at all — this isn't a lower-paying code, it can be a non-payable one. The split turns entirely on the documented medical indication, so the order has to state explicitly why the boot is being dispensed, and the diagnosis on the claim has to match that stated reason.

Custom-fabricated boots — genuinely made for the individual patient, not merely fitted from a prefabricated shell — are billed under L2999 (lower extremity orthosis, not otherwise specified) with a required narrative: manufacturer, model, the specific indication, and why a prefabricated option was clinically insufficient. Because L2999 has no fixed fee schedule rate and demands a claim-specific narrative every time, it draws heavier payer scrutiny and prior-authorization requirements than the coded L4360–L4387 family, and documentation gaps here are a common, avoidable denial source.

Two documentation requirements sit underneath every DME claim in this category regardless of which code is used. A Detailed Written Order Prior to Delivery (DWOPD) has to be signed and dated before the item is dispensed — not requested from the physician after the fact once a payer flags the claim. Proof of delivery — a signed acknowledgment the patient actually received the item — has to be retained in the file. Medicare's coverage criteria for the ankle-foot/knee-ankle-foot orthosis family specifically are published in LCD L33686, "Ankle-Foot/Knee-Ankle-Foot Orthosis" (CGS Administrators and Noridian Healthcare Solutions, DME MACs, effective 04/01/2026) — confirmed live against the CMS Coverage Database for this build. Pull the current version before dispensing at volume, because it details the specific documentation and medical-necessity elements those two DME MACs check against.

One more trap worth naming directly: add-on codes billed alongside L4360, L4361, L4386, or L4387 for components already built into those base codes — an interface pad or a joint mechanism the base code descriptor already includes — deny as unbundling. If a component is part of what the base HCPCS code already describes, it isn't a separate billable add-on regardless of how the supplier's own item catalog itemizes it internally.

ICD-10 specificity: laterality, encounter type, and the denials they prevent

Orthopedic medical-necessity reviews check diagnosis specificity harder than almost any other specialty, because laterality and encounter type (initial versus subsequent) are exactly the data points a payer's coverage rule or repeat-imaging policy is built around. An unspecified or non-lateralized code doesn't just look sloppy on the claim — it gives the reviewer nothing to check the medical-necessity question against, and it fails by default rather than passing on the benefit of the doubt. The complete crosswalk against every high-volume orthopedic procedure code is in our ICD-10 Specificity for Orthopedic Diagnoses: Laterality and Encounter Type guide. Four diagnosis families carry most of the volume, and all of the codes below were validated live against the FY2026 ICD-10-CM code set for this build.

The seventh character matters as much as the laterality digit and gets missed just as often: "A" (initial encounter) applies while the patient is receiving active treatment; a follow-up visit during routine healing, after active treatment has concluded, needs a "D" (subsequent encounter) or later character, not a repeated "A." Billing every visit in a fracture or sprain's recovery timeline with the initial-encounter character is a specificity error of its own, distinct from the laterality problem, and payers increasingly check for it on repeat-imaging claims specifically.

Prior authorization: spine and joint replacement

Spine procedures — discectomy, laminectomy, spinal fusion — carry the specialty's highest prior-auth denial rate, and lumbar fusion specifically is a useful example because Medicare's coverage criteria for it are published and specific rather than left to case-by-case judgment. LCD L37848, "Lumbar Spinal Fusion" (Palmetto GBA, effective 09/12/2024) — confirmed live against the CMS Coverage Database for this build — is the kind of criteria-specific document a payer's fusion denial should be checked against directly, not argued around in the abstract. Commercial payers publish their own equivalent medical policies for spinal fusion, typically built around comparable structural criteria; the LCD is a useful reference model even on a commercial claim, but it is never a substitute for that payer's own published policy.

Elective joint replacement and viscosupplementation injections commonly require documented failed conservative treatment — typically a 3–6 month trial of physical therapy, NSAIDs, and/or corticosteroid injection, depending on the payer — before authorization is approved. What separates an approved letter of medical necessity from a denied one is rarely the clinical appropriateness of the procedure; it's whether the letter maps directly to the payer's specific criteria list. A criteria-mapped letter states each element the policy requires and cites where the chart supports it: imaging findings by date, the specific conservative treatments tried and their duration, functional limitation measured against a named scale, and any criteria genuinely not yet met with a clinical explanation for why. A template letter that restates "patient has severe osteoarthritis and has failed conservative management" without those specifics reads, to a reviewer working from a checklist, as unaddressed criteria — even when the underlying case is clinically sound.

Missing or invalid modifier		29881 reported twice instead of 29880 once, or G0289/76942-style unbundling without the compartment or structure documentation to support 59/XSFor the compartment error, rebill with 29880 — this is a code correction, not an appeal. For a genuine G0289 different-compartment case, append XS with an operative note that documents both compartment locations explicitly.
Pro tip

Before appealing any bundling denial in orthopedics, check whether the underlying issue is actually a coding error rather than a payer mistake. 29881-billed-twice, 76942-with-20611, and unspecified-laterality denials are almost always correct on the payer's part — the fix is a corrected claim, not an appeal letter. Save appeal effort for the genuinely defensible cases: modifier 24/25/57/58/78/79 scenarios with real supporting documentation, and medical-necessity denials where the criteria actually were met and just weren't mapped clearly in the original submission.

Conclusion and next steps

Four fixes cover most of the recoverable revenue in this guide, and a practice can make all four this week without waiting on a system change. Document ultrasound guidance properly on every 20611 claim — saved images plus a written interpretation — and stop billing 76942 alongside it. Code laterality and encounter type on every knee, shoulder, and fracture diagnosis, not just the ones that have already been denied once. Audit global-period modifier use specifically for the 78-versus-79 distinction, because that's the pairing most likely to be silently underpaying or overpaying a claim rather than denying it outright. And confirm DWOPD is signed before every DME item ships, not requested retroactively once a denial arrives.

Beyond this week: build the specific LCD and NCCI checks named throughout this guide into your scrubber and order-entry workflow rather than relying on staff memory, and revisit your fracture-care and arthroscopy charge master against the compartment- and specificity-based rules above before your next denial batch comes back showing the same pattern.

Frequently asked questions

When is an E/M visit separately billable during a fracture-care global period?

Three modifiers cover it, and they're not interchangeable. Modifier 24 is an unrelated condition seen during the post-op window — a patient in a 90-day global for an ankle ORIF who comes in for a new shoulder injury. Modifier 25 is a significant, separately identifiable E/M on the same day as a minor procedure. Modifier 57 is the decision-for-surgery visit itself, the day of or the day before a major procedure. All three require a diagnosis on the E/M line that's distinct from the procedure's diagnosis, and the note has to independently support E/M-level work — not just restate the reason for the follow-up.

What's the actual difference between CPT 20610 and 20611?

20610 is arthrocentesis, aspiration, or injection of a major joint without imaging guidance. 20611 is the same service with ultrasound guidance, and it requires permanently recorded images and a separate interpretive report in the chart — without both, the documentation doesn't support 20611 even if ultrasound was used at the bedside. The imaging-guidance code 76942 is bundled into 20611 under NCCI and is never billed alongside it. Confirm the exact current-year indicator in the CMS NCCI PTP Edits Lookup Tool before building a scrubber rule on it.

Can we bill 29881 twice for a meniscectomy in both knee compartments?

No. 29881 reports a meniscectomy in one compartment, medial or lateral. When both compartments are treated in the same knee, the correct code is 29880, reported once — not 29881 billed twice. Reporting 29881 twice for a bilateral-compartment case is one of the most common unbundling errors in knee arthroscopy coding and denies as a duplicate or bundled service.

What's the difference between modifiers 58, 78, and 79?

58 is a staged or planned return to the OR by the same physician for something related to the original procedure — a second-stage ligament reconstruction, for example. 78 is an unplanned return to the OR for a complication of the original procedure, related, and it resets nothing — it's reimbursed at the intraoperative-only rate and the original global period continues running. 79 is an unrelated procedure by the same physician during the global period, and it starts its own new global period. Mixing up 78 and 79 is the single most expensive modifier error in this group, because 78 pays less and doesn't restart the clock, while 79 does both.

Does CJR-X change how our orthopedic practice bills, or only the hospital's billing?

CJR-X's financial accountability sits with the hospital — it's an inpatient and outpatient MS-DRG and HCPCS-triggered bundle, not something your practice bills into directly. But it changes what happens around your claims: post-acute services, therapy, and follow-up care inside that 90-day episode window are being tracked against the hospital's bundle target, so coordination and avoiding duplicate billing for services the hospital's episode already covers matters more than it used to, even though your own CPT and E/M billing doesn't change.

When does a walking boot need to be coded A9283 instead of L4360–L4387?

A9283 applies only when the boot is used solely for pressure reduction or pressure-ulcer prevention — and under that use, it's typically non-covered by Medicare with no benefit category, not merely a different code. L4360, L4361, L4386, and L4387 apply when the boot is functioning as an orthopedic brace — immobilizing a fracture, sprain, or post-surgical joint. The split turns entirely on medical indication, not on the physical device, so the order and the diagnosis on the claim have to match the actual clinical reason the boot was dispensed.

Why do our joint replacement or spinal fusion prior authorizations keep getting denied?

Almost always because the submitted letter of medical necessity restates the diagnosis instead of mapping the chart against the payer's specific coverage criteria — imaging findings, a documented failed-conservative-care trial of a specific duration, functional limitation scores, and any conservative options still untried. A generic letter reading "patient has severe osteoarthritis and needs a joint replacement" fails against a criteria list even when the surgery is clearly appropriate. Pull the payer's actual medical policy or, for Medicare, the relevant LCD, and write the letter against its specific bullet points.
