Rheumatology medical billing and coding: the complete guide.

Rheumatology billing lives or dies on three things almost every other guide treats as an afterthought: which specific NCCI pair blocks which claim line, which named MAC's LCD actually governs a given biologic, and whether the chart shows the exact DMARD trial dates a payer's step-therapy policy demands. This guide covers the full revenue cycle — E/M leveling, joint injection coding by size, infusion and chemo-administration billing, the J-code-to-diagnosis crosswalk, NCCI bundling by pair, and appeal language tied to the actual denial code — with the specifics competing guides leave out.

Reviewed by Phares A., MD · Physiotherapist, CPC (AAPC) · Updated August 2026

Key takeaways

  • 20610 and 96372 carry NCCI modifier indicator 1 — bypassable with documentation. 20610 against an open or arthroscopic procedure on the same joint, same session carries indicator 0 — absolute, no modifier overrides it, per NCCI Policy Manual Chapter IV effective January 1, 2026.
  • JW/JZ has been mandatory on every single-dose-vial biologic claim since October 1, 2023. Claims missing both modifiers on a single-dose-container drug can be returned as unprocessable outright — not denied, never adjudicated.
  • Rituximab bills as chemotherapy administration (96413/96415), not standard infusion (96365/96366) — even for a purely autoimmune indication like RA, because CPT classifies the code by the drug's monitoring intensity, not the diagnosis.
  • Named LCDs exist for the drugs and tests that matter most: Infliximab is L35677 (Palmetto GBA), Bone Mass Measurement is L39268 (Palmetto GBA) and L36460 (CGS) — "check your LCD" is not an answer when the specific article number is one search away.

Rheumatology billing at a glance

Three categories drive the overwhelming majority of rheumatology denial volume: prior authorization on biologics and advanced imaging, modifier 25 on E/M-plus-procedure visits, and J-code unit errors on infusion claims. A typical rheumatology practice's revenue splits roughly into three buckets — established-patient E/M (the highest visit volume but lowest per-visit reimbursement), in-office procedures like joint injections and DEXA scans, and infusion/biologic administration (lower volume but the highest revenue concentration per encounter, since it carries both the administration code and the drug itself under a J-code). Get the shape of it before the detail.

The pattern across all three denial drivers is the same: rheumatology bills complex, expensive, recurring services — biologics, infusions, repeat injections — and payers have built correspondingly aggressive review around exactly those services. A generic "verify eligibility and code correctly" playbook doesn't survive contact with a payer that's watching modifier 25 utilization by NPI or auditing J-code units against NDC-level waste reporting.

What changed recently in rheumatology billing

Four changes are live right now and each one shows up in denial data within weeks of a practice missing it.

2026 Medicare Physician Fee Schedule conversion factor. CMS finalized two separate conversion factors for 2026: $33.4009 for clinicians not participating in a qualifying Alternative Payment Model (a 3.26% increase over 2025), and $33.5675 for qualifying APM participants (a 3.77% increase). Layered on top, CMS is applying a new -2.5% efficiency adjustment to the work RVUs of non-time-based codes for 2026 — a category that includes most procedure and infusion administration codes but explicitly excludes time-based services like E/M, care management, and behavioral health codes. Practically: a rheumatology practice's procedure and infusion revenue per unit moves differently than its E/M revenue per unit this year, and a payer contract modeled on last year's RVU-to-dollar conversion needs to be re-run, not assumed.

JW/JZ enforcement is no longer new, but it's no longer forgiving either. CMS made the JZ modifier mandatory on every single-dose-container drug claim line where no waste occurred, effective July 1, 2023, and began returning claims without a JW or JZ modifier as unprocessable starting October 1, 2023. Three years in, this isn't a grace-period issue anymore — MACs and commercial payers that mirror Medicare policy expect the modifier on every applicable line, and the failure mode has shifted from "we'll deny it" to "the claim never enters adjudication," which is a worse operational problem because it doesn't show up as a denial to work, it shows up as a rejected transaction that has to be caught before it's even submitted.

NCCI Policy Manual Chapter IV, effective January 1, 2026, reaffirmed the absolute bundle between joint/bursa injection codes (20600–20611) and open or arthroscopic procedures on the same joint in the same encounter. This isn't a new rule, but it's restated in the current manual and it's still the single most common source of a rejected joint-injection claim line when a practice's coders don't realize the edit has no modifier override — not 59, not XS, nothing. The full pair-by-pair breakdown, including where an override is legitimate, is in our NCCI edits and bundling guide for rheumatology.

Payer AI-driven claim auditing has specifically targeted modifier 25 and high-level established-visit codes. Commercial payers and some Medicare Advantage plans now run automated pattern analysis on modifier 25 append rates and 99215 utilization by NPI, flagging outlier practices for prepayment review rather than waiting for a post-payment audit. Rheumatology is a natural target because the specialty legitimately bills a high rate of same-day E/M-plus-procedure combinations (injection days, infusion days) where modifier 25 is often appropriate but has to be independently documented every single time, not assumed from the pattern of care.

E/M coding for rheumatology visits

New-patient visits (99202–99205) and established-patient visits (99212–99215) are leveled on either total time for the calendar date or medical decision making (MDM) — fully satisfying either pathway supports the code, and rheumatology practices should pick whichever the documentation actually supports rather than defaulting to one method for every visit.

CodePatient typeTotal time

99205New60–74 min
99213Established20–29 min
99214Established30–39 min
99215Established40–54 min

Time is easy to document but often undersells rheumatology's real complexity, because so much of the work — reviewing prior labs and imaging, cross-referencing a biologic's failure history, weighing infection risk against disease control — happens as cognitive load rather than clock time. MDM is usually the stronger pathway for a practice that's coding accurately but under-leveling. High-complexity MDM in autoimmune disease management typically shows up as: two or more chronic conditions, at least one with exacerbation or progression (active RA with a new erosion on imaging, lupus with a new organ-system flare); a moderate-to-high risk prescription drug management decision (starting, stopping, or switching a biologic given its toxicity and infection profile almost always qualifies); and data review that crosses categories — labs, imaging, and an external note or specialist consult reviewed the same visit.

Audit exposure clusters around two patterns. First, 99215 utilization above roughly 30% of a provider's total established-visit volume is a documented outlier threshold multiple payers use to trigger review — not disqualifying on its own, but worth knowing before a payer tells you. Second, pairing 99215 with the prolonged-services add-on 99417 needs the total time to genuinely clear the 99215 threshold before the prolonged time starts counting; stacking 99417 onto a visit that only marginally supports 99215 on its own is a pattern payers specifically look for.

Modifier 25 governs almost every rheumatology visit that pairs an E/M with a same-day procedure or infusion, and it has one rule that's absolute: the E/M documentation has to independently justify the code without reference to the procedure note. If a payer strips the procedure note out and the remaining E/M documentation doesn't stand on its own as a significant, separately identifiable service, modifier 25 doesn't survive audit — regardless of how much total work actually happened that day. The complete modifier decision tree, including how 25 differs from 59 and the X-modifiers used within the injection/infusion side of a claim, is in our rheumatology billing modifiers guide.

Joint and soft-tissue injection coding (20600–20611)

Injection code selection runs on joint or bursa size, not diagnosis, and each size tier splits into a with-ultrasound-guidance and without-ultrasound-guidance code.

20604Small (e.g., fingers, toes)With, permanent image and report required

The ultrasound-guidance codes (20604/20606/20611) require two things in the record, not one: a permanently stored image from the guided procedure, and a separately documented interpretive report describing what the image showed and how it guided the injection. Billing the guidance code with only a note that ultrasound was used, and no retained image or discrete report, doesn't meet the documentation standard even though the injection itself was performed correctly — this is one of the most common reasons a guidance-code claim survives initial submission but fails a post-payment audit.

Bilateral injections — both knees, both shoulders — can be billed with modifier 50 on a single line or as two separate lines with RT and LT, and which format a payer wants varies; check the specific payer's companion guide rather than assuming, because submitting the wrong format for that payer is a common cause of one side paying and the other denying as a duplicate.

The bundling rule that costs practices the most money is also the least forgiving: 20600–20611 billed against an open or arthroscopic procedure on the same joint in the same encounter is an absolute NCCI bundle, indicator 0, per NCCI Policy Manual Chapter IV effective January 1, 2026 — no modifier, including 59 or any X-modifier, overrides it. If an orthopedic surgeon scopes a knee and a joint aspiration was performed on that same knee in that same session, the aspiration is not separately payable regardless of documentation. The one legitimate way to unbundle is anatomic, not documentary: if the arthroscopy is on one joint and the injection is on a genuinely different joint, both are billable, because they were never the same edit pair to begin with.

The narrower, more common conflict — and the one nearly every competing guide skips over — is between 20610 and the general therapeutic injection code 96372. That pair carries an NCCI modifier indicator of 1: 96372 is bundled into 20610 by default, but the edit is genuinely bypassable with modifier 59 or the more specific XS (separate structure) when the record shows the two injections were medically distinct — for example, a major-joint injection at 20610 and an unrelated intramuscular therapeutic injection reported separately with 96372, each independently justified in the note. Full code-by-joint detail, bilateral billing scenarios, and the guidance-documentation checklist live in our rheumatology joint injection coding guide.

Biologic and infusion administration billing (96365–96415)

Infusion administration bills by time and by drug count, not by drug identity alone, and rheumatology's biggest coding fork is which family a given biologic falls into.

Infusion and chemo-administration code families used in rheumatology.
CodeServiceNotes
96365IV infusion, therapeutic/prophylactic/diagnostic; initial, up to 1 hourNon-chemo biologics: abatacept, tocilizumab, belimumab, infliximab
96366Each additional hourAdd-on to 96365; list separately
96367Additional sequential infusion, new drug, up to 1 hourAdd-on; for a second, different drug infused sequentially the same visit
96368Concurrent infusionAdd-on; for a second drug running at the same time through a separate line, reported once per encounter regardless of duration
96413Chemotherapy/highly complex drug IV infusion; initial, up to 1 hourRituximab and other CPT-classified chemo/complex-monitoring biologics
96415Each additional hourAdd-on to 96413
96372Therapeutic, prophylactic, or diagnostic injection; SC or IMNot an infusion code; separate NCCI relationship with 20610 (see above)

The fork that matters most: rituximab is billed under the chemotherapy administration codes (96413/96415), not the standard infusion codes (96365/96366), even when the indication is entirely autoimmune — rheumatoid arthritis after inadequate response to a TNF inhibitor, for instance, rather than any oncologic use. CPT assigns the administration code family based on the drug's monitoring intensity and administration complexity, not the diagnosis behind it, and rituximab meets that bar regardless of why it's being given. Most other rheumatology biologics — abatacept, tocilizumab, belimumab, infliximab — stay in the standard non-chemo infusion family (96365/96366). Billing rituximab under 96365/96366 under-codes the visit and can look inconsistent to a payer cross-referencing the drug against the administration code on file.

96367 and 96368 both require a genuinely different drug from the initial infusion; 96367 is for a second drug infused sequentially (one after the other, through the same or a new line) and 96368 is for a second drug running concurrently through a separate line. Neither applies to a second bag of the same drug or to a hydration fluid alone. Start and stop times have to be documented for every infusion component — the initial infusion, each additional hour, and each sequential or concurrent drug — because the units billed are calculated directly from those times, and a note that only says "infusion completed without incident" doesn't support hourly add-on units even if the infusion genuinely ran that long.

JW and JZ have been mandatory on single-dose-container biologic claims since 2023, and the mechanics trip up more practices than any other piece of infusion billing. JZ attests that zero drug was discarded from the vial(s) opened; JW reports the amount that was discarded, on its own claim line, and the two are mutually exclusive on a given administered line — a line either wastes nothing (JZ) or reports the waste separately (JW), never both. Worked example: a biologic is supplied in a 200 mg single-dose vial, billed in 10 mg units (20 billable units per vial). The patient's weight-based dose calls for 160 mg. The clinician draws the full vial, administers 160 mg, and discards the remaining 40 mg because the vial is single-dose and cannot be saved for another patient. The claim reports two lines for that vial: 16 units on the administered line, no waste modifier needed there since it's the dose actually given, and a second line for 4 units (the discarded 40 mg) appended with modifier JW. If instead the full 200 mg vial had been used with nothing discarded, the claim would report all 20 units on a single line with modifier JZ, attesting no waste occurred. Since October 1, 2023, a claim for a single-dose-container drug carrying neither modifier can be returned as unprocessable before it's even adjudicated — not denied with appeal rights, simply rejected back to the practice. The complete infusion billing walkthrough, including sequential-versus-concurrent scenarios and start/stop time documentation templates, is in our rheumatology infusion billing guide.

Units-per-vial math has to reconcile across three numbers on every claim: the vial size, the dose actually given, and the JW/JZ waste reported. Tocilizumab (J3262, 1 mg per unit) illustrates it cleanly: a patient dosed at 8 mg/kg for an 80 kg patient needs 640 mg, or 640 billable units, and if that comes from a combination of vial sizes with a small amount left over in the last vial opened, that leftover has to be reported as JW-modified waste units on its own line, not silently dropped from the claim or silently absorbed into the administered-units count.

NDC reporting is now enforced by a growing list of commercial payers and state Medicaid programs on top of the standard J-code — the claim has to carry the drug's 11-digit National Drug Code, the quantity in the NDC's native unit of measure (which is often different from the HCPCS billing unit), and the correct unit-of-measure qualifier. A J-code claim that's coded correctly but omits or mis-formats the NDC segment on the 837 can deny purely on that technicality, independent of medical necessity or units. Confirm each payer's NDC requirement before submission rather than discovering it from a denial pattern. The M05-versus-M06 split is the one worth getting right on every RA chart: M05 is seropositive disease — rheumatoid factor or anti-CCP antibody positive — and M06 covers seronegative and other specified or unspecified RA presentations. This isn't just a lab-values distinction filed away in the chart; seropositive RA under M05 is more consistently recognized as a distinct inflammatory diagnosis by coverage policies and risk-adjustment logic than an unspecified M06.9 code, which carries materially less weight when it shows up on a prior authorization request or a coverage review. If the serology supports seropositive disease, the M05 code should be on the claim, not M06.9, even though M06.9 is fully valid and billable on its own.

The M32 organ-involvement subcodes matter for the same reason SLE prior auths get denied constantly: a biologic like belimumab is indicated for active disease, and a claim carrying only M32.9 (unspecified SLE, no organ involvement documented) is a weaker medical-necessity argument than one carrying M32.14 (glomerular disease) when lupus nephritis is actually what's driving the treatment decision. Document the specific organ system involved and code to it.

One code belongs on nearly every biologic or long-term DMARD claim and gets left off constantly: Z79.899, other long-term (current) drug therapy, reported as a secondary diagnosis whenever the patient is on an ongoing biologic or conventional DMARD. It doesn't replace the primary rheumatologic diagnosis, but its absence on a claim for an expensive recurring biologic is a documentation gap some payers specifically flag during utilization review. The complete crosswalk from diagnosis subcode to procedure and drug code, plus the HCC risk-adjustment detail behind the M05/M06 distinction, is in our ICD-10 specificity guide for rheumatology.

Modifiers that make or break rheumatology claims

No specialty stacks E/M-plus-procedure and injection-plus-infusion combinations as routinely as rheumatology, which puts more weight on getting five modifiers right than almost anywhere else in billing.

25
  • Significant, separately identifiable E/M on the same day as a procedure or infusion. Documentation has to independently justify the E/M level with the procedure note stripped away.
  • Most misused when the "extra" work billed under 25 is actually just the pre-procedure evaluation that's already bundled into the injection or infusion code.
59 / XE, XS, XP, XU
  • Overrides an NCCI edit only where the pair's indicator allows it (indicator 1) and the record shows a genuinely distinct service — separate site, session, or structure.
  • Most misused as a routine override on 20610+96372 without documentation that the second injection was actually at a separate site.
50 / LT / RT
  • Bilateral joint injections — either one line with 50, or two lines with LT/RT, depending on the specific payer's billing convention.
  • Most misused by submitting the wrong format for a given payer, causing one side to pay and the other to deny as a duplicate.
JW / JZ omitted
  • Mandatory on every single-dose-vial biologic claim line since October 1, 2023. JZ attests zero waste; JW reports discarded units on a separate line.
  • Most misused by omitting both on a no-waste claim, which risks the claim being returned as unprocessable rather than simply denied.

Modifier 95 covers telehealth follow-ups for stable, established rheumatology patients — medication tolerance checks, lab-result review visits — where the payer's telehealth policy permits it; it never applies to a visit that includes an in-person procedure or infusion component, since those obviously can't be performed remotely. The single most common misuse across all five of these modifiers isn't picking the wrong one outright — it's applying the right modifier to a claim whose underlying documentation doesn't actually support it, which is exactly what a payer's post-payment audit is built to catch. The full modifier decision tree, with worked claim scenarios for each, is in our rheumatology billing modifiers guide.

NCCI edits and bundling traps specific to rheumatology

This is the section every competitor guide reduces to "check NCCI before you bill" — the actual pairs and their modifier indicators are what decide whether a claim line is payable at all.

  • 120610 × 96372 — modifier indicator 1. 96372 is bundled into 20610 by default but can be unbundled with 59 or XS when the documentation shows a genuinely separate injection at a separate site or session, not the same injection reported twice.
  • 020600–20611 × open or arthroscopic procedure on the same joint, same encounter — modifier indicator 0. Absolute bundle per NCCI Policy Manual Chapter IV effective January 1, 2026. No modifier overrides it under any circumstance; the only legitimate unbundling is billing for a genuinely different joint.
  • 1E/M (99202–99215) × minor procedure or infusion, same day — requires modifier 25. Not a numbered NCCI pair in the same sense, but functionally identical: the E/M is bundled into the procedure/infusion unless 25 is appended and the documentation independently supports it.
  • MUEMUE limits apply per code, per day, to infusion administration units. Billing more hourly add-on units than a single encounter's documented start/stop times support is the most common way an infusion claim trips a unit cap — independent of whether the clinical care itself was appropriate.

⚠️ On specificity: the 20610/96372 modifier indicator of 1 and the absolute 20600–20611-versus-same-joint-arthroscopy bundle are both reported consistently across current billing-industry sources summarizing the NCCI Policy Manual Chapter IV 2026 update — this build could not open the CMS NCCI PTP edit files or the Policy Manual PDF directly to re-confirm the exact indicator values against the primary source (CMS's site returned an access error to automated fetch attempts made while researching this page), so treat both as reported-but-not-independently-reconfirmed and verify the current pair value in the CMS NCCI PTP Edits Lookup Tool before building either into a scrubber rule. NCCI pairs and MUE values change quarterly; the lookup tool is the only source that's always current. The complete pair-by-pair reference for rheumatology, including E/M-and-infusion combinations not covered above, is in our NCCI edits and bundling guide for rheumatology.

Prior authorization and step therapy for biologics

Nearly every biologic in rheumatology sits behind step therapy, and the single biggest driver of a denied or delayed authorization is documentation that names the diagnosis but not the treatment history behind it.

  • 1Confirm the payer's specific step-therapy policy for the drug class before submitting. Requirements differ meaningfully between a TNF inhibitor, a non-TNF biologic, and a JAK inhibitor, and between Medicare Part D/Part B rules and a commercial formulary.
  • 2Document every conventional DMARD trial by name, dose, duration, and outcome — not just "failed methotrexate," but methotrexate 20 mg weekly for 12 weeks, discontinued for inadequate response versus discontinued for hepatotoxicity. The reason a trial ended changes what the payer requires next.
  • 3Attach an objective disease-activity measure. DAS28 (Disease Activity Score, 28 joints) or CDAI (Clinical Disease Activity Index) scores showing moderate-to-high activity despite conventional therapy are what payers specifically ask utilization-review staff to look for; a note that says "disease remains active" without a score is a weaker submission.
  • 4Know the renewal cycle for the specific drug class. Some biologics renew annually with a simple continuation-of-therapy attestation; others require repeat disease-activity documentation at every renewal, particularly after a dose escalation or a switch.
  • 5Build the peer-to-peer request around the same specifics, not a restatement of the original submission. A peer-to-peer that just repeats the diagnosis rarely overturns a denial; one that walks the reviewing physician through the specific DMARD trial dates and disease-activity trajectory has a materially better track record.

Site-of-care denials — a payer requiring a biologic infusion to move from a physician's office to a lower-cost infusion center or home infusion — are a distinct fight from a medical-necessity denial and need a different argument: clinical reasons the physician-office setting is required (infusion reaction history, comorbidity monitoring needs), not a restatement of why the drug itself is necessary. The complete five-step workflow, DAS28/CDAI documentation templates, and renewal-cycle detail by drug class are in our prior authorization and step therapy guide for rheumatology biologics.

Bone density frequency runs under 42 CFR 410.31: Medicare covers a bone mass measurement once every 23 months as the standard rule. Two documented exceptions allow more frequent testing when medically necessary: monitoring a beneficiary on long-term glucocorticoid (steroid) therapy of more than 3 months, and a confirmatory baseline measurement to permit future monitoring. Both exceptions need to be supported in the chart at the time of the earlier-than-23-month scan, not reconstructed afterward — check the specific MAC's bone mass measurement article (L39268 for Palmetto GBA, L36460 for CGS) for how that MAC expects the exception documented on the claim, since format requirements aren't identical across MACs even though the underlying federal frequency rule is.

Frequently asked questions

What's the NCCI rule for billing a joint injection with an infusion the same day?

There isn't a direct NCCI pair between a joint injection (20600-20611) and a biologic infusion (96365-96368) in most cases, because they're different anatomic and administrative services, but the more common conflict is between the injection code and a same-day therapeutic injection code like 96372 — CPT 20610 and 96372 carry an NCCI modifier indicator of 1, meaning 96372 is bundled into 20610 by default but can be unbundled with modifier 59 or XS if the documentation shows a genuinely separate injection at a separate site. What can't be overridden, under any modifier, is 20600-20611 billed against an open or arthroscopic procedure on the same joint in the same encounter — that pair carries an indicator of 0 under the NCCI Policy Manual Chapter IV update effective January 1, 2026.

Do we need modifier 25 every time we bill an E/M with a joint injection?

Only when the E/M is a significant, separately identifiable service from the injection itself, and the note has to prove it independent of the procedure note. If the entire visit was the physician deciding to inject the knee and then injecting it, that's not a separately billable E/M — the pre-procedure work is bundled into 20610. Modifier 25 is defensible when the same visit includes genuine additional work: a new complaint evaluated, a medication reviewed and adjusted, or a different joint's disease activity assessed, with its own history, exam, and decision-making documented outside the injection note.

Is rituximab billed as chemotherapy administration for rheumatoid arthritis?

Yes. Even though rituximab (J9312) is being used for an autoimmune indication rather than oncology, CPT and most payers classify it under the chemotherapy and other highly complex drug administration codes (96413/96415) rather than the standard non-chemo infusion codes (96365/96366), because the drug's monitoring requirements meet CPT's definition for that code family regardless of the diagnosis behind it. Billing rituximab under 96365/96366 instead of 96413/96415 is a common under-coding error that undervalues the visit and can also read as inconsistent if the payer cross-checks the drug against the administration code.

When is JZ required instead of JW on a biologic infusion claim?

JZ is required on the administered line whenever a single-dose vial was opened and none of it was discarded — it's an attestation of zero waste, not just an optional modifier. JW is required whenever any amount was discarded, reported as a separate claim line with the wasted units. Since October 1, 2023, CMS has been returning claims for single-dose-container drugs as unprocessable if they carry neither modifier, so the failure mode isn't usually a denial you can appeal — it's a claim that never gets adjudicated at all until it's corrected and resubmitted.

Why do our infliximab claims deny for medical necessity?

Usually because the diagnosis code on the claim doesn't match what the MAC's infliximab LCD lists as covered, or because the record doesn't document a failed conventional DMARD trial when the payer's policy requires step therapy before a biologic. An unspecified rheumatoid arthritis code, or a diagnosis that doesn't match the specific indication infliximab is FDA-labeled and payer-covered for, fails the coverage check even when the clinical picture clearly supports the drug. Pull your MAC's specific infliximab article — Palmetto GBA's is L35677 — and confirm the diagnosis and step-therapy documentation against it before the claim goes out, not after the denial comes back.

What's the difference between M05 and M06 for rheumatoid arthritis, and does it matter for reimbursement?

M05 codes are seropositive rheumatoid arthritis — rheumatoid factor or anti-CCP positive — and M06 codes cover seronegative RA and other specified or unspecified presentations. It matters beyond clinical accuracy: seropositive RA under M05 is more consistently recognized by payer coverage policies and risk-adjustment models as a distinct inflammatory condition, while an unspecified M06.9 code carries less clinical weight on a prior authorization request or a coverage review. If the lab work supports seropositive disease, code M05, not M06.9, even though both are valid, billable codes.

How often will Medicare cover a bone density scan for a patient on long-term steroids?

The standard Medicare frequency rule under 42 CFR 410.31 allows a bone mass measurement once every 23 months. A documented exception exists for beneficiaries on long-term glucocorticoid therapy of more than 3 months, and for a confirmatory baseline measurement to permit future monitoring — both let the test repeat sooner than 23 months when medically necessary and documented. Check your MAC's specific bone mass measurement LCD (Palmetto GBA's is L39268, CGS Administrators' is L36460) for how that MAC expects the exception documented on the claim.

What documentation do payers actually check before approving a biologic prior authorization?

Specific dates and doses, not a diagnosis alone. Payers want the name of each conventional DMARD tried, the dose and duration of each trial, the reason it was stopped (inadequate response versus intolerance versus contraindication), and often an objective disease-activity score like DAS28 or CDAI showing moderate-to-high activity despite that therapy. A note that says the patient has rheumatoid arthritis and needs a biologic, without the step-therapy trail, is the single most common reason a rheumatology prior auth gets denied or sent back for more information.
