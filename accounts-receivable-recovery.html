Accounts receivable recovery: working down old claims without writing off money you could still collect.

Every practice accumulates some unpaid claims. The question that actually matters is not how large the balance looks on a report, but how much of it is realistically recoverable, and that answer changes completely depending on why each claim is still open, how old it is, and whether the payer or the patient owes it.

Key takeaways

  • Not all old A/R is equally dead. A claim still inside its appeal window is far more recoverable than one past it, regardless of age.
  • Segment before you work. A blended pile of claims wastes effort on the wrong ones; sort by age, reason and dollar value first.
  • Payer A/R and patient A/R need different playbooks. One follows denial codes and appeal deadlines; the other follows relationship, volume and payment plans.
  • A backlog is a symptom, not just a problem. Fix the upstream cause or the same claims pile up again next quarter.
  • A specific write-off reason is worth more than a clean balance sheet. It is the data that tells you what to fix upstream.

Start with aging, not age alone

Standard aging buckets — 0–30, 31–60, 61–90, 91–120, and 120-plus days — exist for a reason: they roughly track when a claim moves from "still in normal process" to "something has gone wrong" to "running out of time to fix." Every practice management system and clearinghouse reports A/R this way, and it's the correct starting lens for a weekly review. But age by itself is a blunt instrument. A 120-day claim that is still inside its appeal window is a very different problem from a 45-day claim past its appeal deadline. The number that should actually drive priority is not how old a claim is, it's how much runway remains before the deadline that determines whether it can still be recovered at all — and money left sitting in A/R past the 90-to-120-day range generally becomes progressively harder to collect, because that's the window where timely filing and appeal deadlines tend to close.

The aging report itself is only useful if it's pulled and reviewed on a fixed schedule, not glanced at occasionally when the total balance starts to look uncomfortable. A practice that runs the report weekly catches a claim drifting from the 31–60 bucket into 61–90 while there's still time to act on it; a practice that checks quarterly finds out about the same claim after it's already deep in the 120-plus bucket with its appeal window closed. The report is the same in both cases — what differs is when someone looked at it, and that timing is entirely within the practice's control even when the underlying payer delay isn't.

It also helps to separate the aging report by payer and by claim type before drawing conclusions from the totals. A practice with a heavy Medicaid or workers' comp mix will naturally carry a higher average age than one that's mostly commercial payers with fast adjudication, and blending them into one aggregate number obscures which segment is actually the problem. Aging by payer usually reveals that most of the balance sitting in the older buckets traces back to two or three payers with consistently slow or difficult processes, which is exactly the information needed to decide where to focus follow-up staff time.

It's also worth aging the backlog separately from new claims when reviewing performance, rather than blending a legacy cleanup project into the same numbers used to judge day-to-day performance. A practice working through an inherited six-figure backlog will show a worse aggregate days-in-A/R than one starting clean, and that's not a fair comparison unless the backlog is reported as its own line. Separating "new claims aging normally" from "legacy backlog being worked down" gives a much clearer read on whether the current process is actually healthy, independent of how much old debris is still being cleared out of the system.

Segment the backlog before working it

Working a backlog in date order, oldest first, feels intuitive but is usually the wrong approach. A better first pass sorts the whole balance three ways: by dollar value, since a handful of large claims often account for a disproportionate share of the total; by denial reason, since claims denied for the same fixable cause can often be corrected and resubmitted as a batch rather than individually; and by remaining window, since a claim about to lose its appeal eligibility needs to move ahead of a claim with more time left, regardless of which one is older. This sorting takes a few hours up front and routinely saves far more than that in wasted effort on claims that were never going to be collectible.

Where the denial reason itself is the issue — a specific CARC or RARC code repeating across a batch of claims — that's a coding and submission problem more than an A/R problem, and it's worth fixing at the source rather than re-chasing the same error every cycle. We cover the individual codes and root-cause fixes in our denial management guide rather than duplicating that detail here.

Three lenses for sorting a backlog before assigning work, and what each one surfaces.
Sort byWhat it surfacesWhy it matters
Dollar valueThe handful of large claims driving most of the total balanceRecovering ten high-value claims can move the needle more than working two hundred small ones
Denial reasonRepeated, fixable errors spread across many claimsA batch fix and resubmission is far faster than working each claim as a one-off
Remaining windowWhich claims are about to lose appeal or resubmission eligibilityA claim with days left on its window has to jump the queue ahead of an older claim with months left

Once the backlog is segmented, assign it by owner rather than leaving it as one shared list. A shared queue with no individual accountability is where claims quietly stall — everyone assumes someone else is working it, and the claim ages another cycle without anyone touching it. Assigning specific payers or specific dollar-value tiers to specific staff members, with a defined number of accounts worked per week, turns the backlog into a tracked project with visible progress rather than an open-ended task that never quite gets prioritized against the day's other work.

Write-offs: what's genuinely uncollectible versus what just needs more work

Not every dollar is worth the staff time to pursue, but the line between a legitimate write-off and a premature one is easy to blur when a balance has simply been sitting untouched for months. A legitimate write-off has a specific, documentable cause: a contractual adjustment required by the payer contract, a timely filing deadline that has genuinely expired, or a patient balance confirmed uncollectible after a documented attempt. A premature write-off has a different, much fuzzier cause: the claim is old, nobody has looked at it in a while, and it's easier to clear it off the report than to figure out whether it's actually still recoverable. Those look identical on a balance sheet and are completely different in what they cost the practice.

What separates a healthy write-off process from a sloppy one is specificity: recording the actual reason — timely filing expired, patient uncollectible after three attempts, provider write-off per contract — rather than lumping everything into one generic adjustment code. That reason data is what eventually tells you whether the real problem is a slow eligibility process, an undertrained coder, or a genuinely uncollectible patient population, and each of those has a different fix. The full breakdown of what a documented write-off policy should specify, including approval thresholds and audit risk, is in our write-off policy guide.

The audit risk of an undocumented or inconsistent write-off process is easy to underestimate because it rarely surfaces until a payer audit, a compliance review, or new ownership asks to see the reasoning behind a year of adjustments. A practice that can point to a written policy, a consistent approval threshold, and a specific reason code on every write-off has a defensible answer. A practice that wrote off balances inconsistently — sometimes with documentation, sometimes without, sometimes at a manager's discretion and sometimes automatically after a set number of days — has no clean answer to "why was this balance written off but not that one," and that gap is exactly what an auditor looks for.

What a written write-off policy should specify, at minimum.
ElementWhy it belongs in the policy
Approval thresholdSets a dollar amount above which a write-off needs a second sign-off, so no single person can quietly clear large balances
Required documentationSpecifies what has to be on file — the denial reason, the collection attempts made, the deadline that closed — before a balance can be written off
Reason code taxonomyA fixed, specific list of write-off reasons rather than one catch-all code, so the reason data is actually usable for trend analysis
Review cadenceA regular schedule for reviewing write-offs in aggregate, so a growing category of premature write-offs gets caught before it becomes a habit

None of this needs to be complicated to be effective. A one-page policy that names the approval threshold, requires a specific reason code, and gets reviewed quarterly closes most of the audit exposure a practice actually carries. What creates risk isn't the absence of a sophisticated system, it's the absence of any consistent, written standard at all — which is the state most practices are actually in until someone deliberately fixes it.

Pro tip

Before writing off any batch of old claims, run them through one more check: group by denial reason and see if a single fixable issue — a missing modifier, an outdated payer ID, an eligibility mismatch — explains a large share of them. Practices are frequently surprised to find that what looked like an unworkable backlog was actually one repeated, correctable mistake spread across dozens of claims.

Payer A/R versus patient A/R: two different problems

A payer balance and a patient balance both show up on the same aging report, but they behave nothing alike and shouldn't be worked with the same playbook. Payer A/R comes with a denial reason code, a defined appeal or resubmission window, and a portal or phone line that will tell you exactly what's wrong if you ask the right question. Patient A/R has none of that structure: smaller balances spread across far more accounts, no standardized reason a balance is unpaid, and a relationship and reputation stake that a payer call never carries — a poorly handled patient collection can cost the practice a patient, not just the balance.

Treating patient balances like small payer claims — generic statements, no context, escalating straight to a collections letter — tends to produce worse recovery and worse patient retention than an approach built for how patients actually respond: clear estimates before the balance exists, payment plans instead of one lump demand, and multiple contact channels rather than a single mailed statement. The full approach, including when a balance should move to third-party collections rather than staying in-house, is in our patient-responsibility collections guide.

Payer A/R versus patient A/R at a glance.
CharacteristicPayer A/RPatient A/R

Payer follow-up: the calls and portal checks that move a claim

A generic "just checking on my claim" call to a payer produces almost nothing actionable, because it doesn't ask for anything specific enough to get a specific answer. A follow-up that actually moves a claim forward asks for the claim status, the specific hold or pend reason, and an expected resolution date, and it happens on a defined schedule rather than whenever someone remembers to look. Claims that stall past a second follow-up without resolution need to become a formal appeal or a documented escalation, not another routine check-in that produces the same non-answer as the last one.

Portal checks and phone calls serve different purposes and both have a place in a follow-up workflow. A portal check is fast and works well for a straightforward status update on a large volume of claims, which is why it should usually be the first-line check at the earlier follow-up interval. A phone call becomes worth the time investment once a claim has stalled past that first check, or when the portal status itself is ambiguous, because a representative can surface the specific pend reason and next step that a portal status code often doesn't spell out on its own.

The structured cadence we use — when to make the first check, when to escalate, and exactly what to ask for on the call or in the portal so the follow-up actually produces new information — is in our payer follow-up guide.

Stopping the backlog from rebuilding

Clearing an existing backlog without addressing why it built up in the first place buys a few months of clean numbers before the same pile reappears. The recurring causes are usually upstream of the claim itself: eligibility not verified before the visit, a scrubber that is not catching payer-specific rejection patterns, or a denial queue that is not worked on a fixed schedule, so claims sit for weeks before anyone looks at them. A practice that reviews its top denial reasons monthly and assigns denial follow-up as a specific, tracked responsibility, not a task that happens whenever someone has spare time, is the practice whose A/R balance stays low rather than the one that needs a periodic backlog cleanup project.

It's worth distinguishing a one-time cleanup from an operational fix, because they solve different problems and a practice needs both. The cleanup addresses the existing pile of old claims — triaging what's recoverable, writing off what isn't, and closing the backlog down to a manageable baseline. The operational fix addresses why the pile formed in the first place, so the baseline stays low going forward instead of drifting back up over the following two or three quarters. Skipping the operational fix and only doing the cleanup is the single most common reason a practice ends up doing the same backlog project again eighteen months later.

Step by step: running the A/R recovery process

  1. Pull and segment the aging reportRun the report weekly, break it out by payer, and sort the balance by dollar value, denial reason and remaining window rather than working it in date order.
  2. Triage each bucket against its actual deadlineFor claims in the 61-90 and 91-120 buckets especially, check the specific appeal or resubmission deadline rather than relying on the bucket label alone to set priority.
  3. Assign owners and a weekly quotaSplit the segmented backlog across staff by payer or dollar tier, with a defined number of accounts each owner works per week, so nothing sits in a shared, ownerless queue.
  4. Work payer claims through a structured follow-up cadenceCheck status and hold reason at defined intervals rather than ad hoc; escalate to a formal appeal once a claim stalls past the second follow-up.
  5. Work patient balances with the patient-appropriate playbookPayment plans, multiple contact channels and clear communication before defaulting to a collections letter or agency referral.
  6. Document every write-off with a specific reasonRecord the actual cause — timely filing expired, patient uncollectible, contractual adjustment — against a documented policy and approval threshold, not a generic adjustment code.
  7. Feed denial patterns back upstreamWhere a batch of write-offs traces to one repeated, fixable error, correct it at the coding or submission stage so the same claims don't keep landing back in the backlog.

Building a weekly A/R review into practice operations

A/R recovery works best as a standing weekly process, not an occasional project someone tackles when the balance finally looks alarming. That weekly review should pull the current aging report, flag anything that crossed into a new bucket since the last review, confirm nothing inside an appeal window is about to expire, and assign specific accounts to specific staff rather than leaving the whole list as a shared, ownerless queue. Practices that run this review consistently rarely accumulate the kind of six-figure backlog that takes a dedicated cleanup engagement to unwind, because nothing sits untouched long enough to become a genuine write-off candidate in the first place.

The reporting side of this matters as much as the workflow side: a days-in-A/R trend that's creeping upward, even while still under target, is an earlier and more useful warning sign than the aggregate dollar balance, because it surfaces a slipping upstream process before it becomes a cash problem serious enough to notice from the top-line number alone. A monthly summary that tracks days-in-A/R by payer, the dollar value moving through each aging bucket, and the top recurring denial reasons gives ownership and management a consistent view of whether the trend is improving or slipping, rather than relying on a single balance figure that can hide a lot of movement underneath it.

Do
  • Pull the aging report weekly and act on it the same week, not the week after.
  • Sort a backlog by dollar value, denial reason and remaining window before assigning work.
  • Record a specific reason on every write-off, tied to a documented policy.
  • Run payer and patient balances through separate playbooks built for how each one actually gets resolved.
  • Feed repeated denial reasons back to coding and submission so the same errors stop recurring.
Don't
  • Don't work a backlog in pure date order without checking which claims still have a live deadline.
  • Don't write off a balance just because it's old rather than because it's genuinely uncollectible.
  • Don't leave the backlog as a shared queue with no individual owner or weekly quota.
  • Don't run a generic "checking on my claim" payer call that produces no new information.
  • Don't treat a patient balance like a small payer claim; the recovery approach and the stakes are different.

Frequently asked questions

How old does a claim have to be before it's basically uncollectible?

There is no universal number, but recovery odds drop sharply after 90 days and drop again after 120, mainly because timely filing and appeal windows close during that period, not because the money itself becomes harder to justify. A claim still inside its appeal window at 150 days is more recoverable than a claim past its window at 60 days. Track the deadline, not just the age.

Should we write off old balances or keep trying to collect them?

Write off a balance once you have confirmed there is no further legitimate recovery path: the appeal window is closed, the patient is confirmed uncollectible after a documented attempt, or the amount is below what it would cost in staff time to keep pursuing. Keep the reason code specific rather than lumping everything into one generic write-off category, since that reason data is what tells you which upstream process to fix.

What is a realistic days-in-A/R target?

Under 40 days on average is a reasonable target for most specialties, though the achievable number depends on payer mix. What matters more than hitting an exact number is the trend: a rising days-in-A/R average, even if still under 40, is an early warning that something upstream, eligibility, coding or submission timing, is slipping before it shows up as a cash problem.

Can old A/R actually be recovered, or is a backlog mostly a lost cause?

A meaningful share of an old backlog is usually recoverable, particularly claims denied for a fixable reason like a missing modifier or an eligibility mismatch rather than a true coverage exclusion. The recovery rate on a backlog is rarely zero and rarely 100%; the value of a dedicated review is finding out which claims in your specific backlog are which, rather than assuming the whole balance is dead.

Do you take over A/R that's already old, or only new claims going forward?

Both. When we start an engagement we inventory the existing backlog, separate what is realistically recoverable from what should be written off, and work the recoverable portion in parallel with taking over new claims. You are not choosing between a clean start and a resolved backlog.

Sitting on an A/R backlog you haven't had time to triage?

We inventory what's actually recoverable, work it alongside your new claims, and give you a monthly report on where the balance stands.
