Endocrinology billing and coding: the complete 2026 guide.

Endocrinology bills a strange mix of durable medical equipment, timed behavioral-education benefits, chronic-disease management codes, and image-guided biopsy work, and the single most expensive mistake in the specialty isn't a wrong CPT code at all. It's billing a continuous glucose monitor through the wrong benefit path. CGMs split unpredictably between the DME benefit and, on many commercial plans, the pharmacy benefit, depending on the specific device and the specific payer, and a claim sent down the wrong path denies cleanly and entirely avoidably. This guide covers that problem in depth, plus every core endocrinology procedure family: insulin pumps, diabetes self-management training, medical nutrition therapy, thyroid FNA coding, chronic and principal care management, ICD-10 specificity, modifiers, NCCI/MUE bundling, and the prior-authorization load that now includes GLP-1 agonists.

Reviewed by Phares A., MD · Physiotherapist, CPC (AAPC) · Updated September 2026

Key takeaways

  • CGM billing splits between the DME benefit and the pharmacy benefit, and getting it wrong is a clean, avoidable denial. Medicare treats every therapeutic CGM as DME under Part B, full stop, but commercial and Medicare Advantage plans vary by carrier and sometimes by specific device, and nobody in this niche publishes the routing problem clearly.
  • DSMT and Medical Nutrition Therapy are both fully payable the same year, but never the same day. Same-date billing for the same beneficiary is one of the most common, entirely preventable denials in diabetes education.
  • Thyroid FNA (10005–10012) already includes the imaging guidance. Billing 76942 or 76536 separately for the same lesion the same session is a textbook unbundling denial, not a gray area.
  • GLP-1 prior authorization now runs on documented step therapy, not diagnosis. A PA submitted with only a type 2 diabetes diagnosis and no metformin trial history is close to an automatic initial denial regardless of clinical appropriateness.
  • An endocrinology practice dispensing CGMs and pumps in-house is itself a DMEPOS supplier and needs its own CMS-855S enrollment, site visit, and three-year revalidation cycle, separate from the practice's physician credentialing entirely, and a real planning constraint most practices discover only after trying to bill their first device claim.

Endocrinology billing at a glance

Four things decide most of endocrinology's revenue outcomes: which benefit category a diabetes device falls under, whether a timed behavioral-education service is documented against the right hour allotment and referral, whether a thyroid procedure code matches what the operative note actually describes, and whether a prior authorization was built around the payer's actual step-therapy ladder instead of the diagnosis alone. Layered on top of all four is a specialty-wide pattern this guide keeps returning to: the same clinical service frequently has more than one legitimate billing path (DME vs. pharmacy, DSMT vs. MNT, CCM vs. PCM, CGM supply vs. RPM), and the practices losing the most revenue aren't billing incorrectly so much as they're not confirming which path applies before the claim goes out. Here's the shape of it before the detail.

Continuous glucose monitors: the benefit-path problem

This is the section every other endocrinology billing guide underexplains, and it's the one that costs practices and patients the most money. A continuous glucose monitor is not billed the same way twice across payers, and the difference isn't medical necessity, it's which insurance benefit category the claim has to travel through, and that category is decided by the payer and sometimes by the specific device, not by anything the clinician controls. Every other trap in this guide, a bundling edit, a missing modifier, a frequency cap, is at least consistent once you know the rule. This one isn't: the same device, the same diagnosis, and the same documentation can be billable through one channel and unbillable through another, purely because of which plan the patient happens to carry, which is exactly why it survives as a persistent revenue leak long after a practice has otherwise cleaned up its coding.

Under Original Medicare, the rule is simple and absolute: a therapeutic CGM is durable medical equipment, billed under Part B through a DME supplier, never through Part D. Medicare's DME MAC Glucose Monitors LCD (L33822, jointly maintained by CGS Administrators and Noridian Healthcare Solutions as the DME MACs, effective 10/01/2024) and its companion policy article (A52464) govern this directly. There is no pharmacy-benefit pathway for a Medicare beneficiary's CGM at all; a retail pharmacy attempting to adjudicate a Dexcom or Libre sensor as a Part D drug claim for a Medicare patient is using the wrong channel outright.

Medicare even splits CGM devices into two further sub-categories that carry different device codes, supply codes, and modifier logic:

The device and supply codes above cover only the DME side of a CGM claim. There is a second, entirely separate billing path for the physician's clinical work around a CGM, sensor placement, data interpretation, and reporting, using its own CPT family that has nothing to do with the DME HCPCS codes and is not mutually exclusive with them:

Modifier 25 applies here the same way it applies to any other same-day procedure: append it to a same-day E/M code only when the visit note documents work genuinely separate from the CGM setup or interpretation itself.

Two modifiers decide which coverage pathway a Medicare CGM DME claim is actually resting on, and using the wrong one doesn't just risk a denial, it documents the wrong medical-necessity theory on the claim itself:

  • KXInsulin-treated pathway. Attests the beneficiary is insulin-treated and that the LCD's insulin-treated coverage criteria are met and documented.
  • KSProblematic-hypoglycemia pathway. Attests the beneficiary is not insulin-treated but has a documented history of problematic hypoglycemia meeting the LCD's specific threshold, per the current DME MAC criteria, either recurrent (more than one) Level 2 hypoglycemic events (glucose under 54 mg/dL) with a documented history of treatment adjustment, or at least one Level 3 event (an episode characterized by altered mental or physical status requiring third-party assistance to treat).

⚠️ A frequent and costly misunderstanding on this pathway: GLP-1 agonists, SGLT2 inhibitors, and metformin do not count as insulin for purposes of the KX pathway. A patient on Ozempic or Metformin alone, with no insulin and no documented problematic hypoglycemia, does not meet Medicare's CGM coverage criteria under either pathway, this is a real and common source of denials our research surfaced repeatedly in DME billing guidance, and it is worth flagging explicitly at order entry rather than discovering it on appeal.

The initial visit note needs
  • The diabetes diagnosis, coded to the specificity the chart actually supports.
  • Insulin treatment documented in the medication list (supporting Z79.4), or the specific qualifying hypoglycemic event with its Level 2/Level 3 detail.
  • Confirmation the patient received and completed device training, reflected in the prescription itself.
  • The visit modality (in-person or a Medicare-approved telehealth encounter) stated explicitly.
The 6-month follow-up note needs
  • Explicit confirmation of adherence to the CGM regimen, not just "diabetes stable," which doesn't speak to device use at all.
  • Confirmation the broader diabetes treatment plan is still being followed.
  • Any change in insulin status or hypoglycemia history since the last visit, since a change can shift which coverage pathway (KX vs. KS) actually applies going forward.

Both visits run on a fixed calendar, not a clinical-judgment one: the initial visit has to fall within six months before the CGM order, and the follow-up has to recur every six months after that. A chart that satisfies the initial visit's requirements but lets a six-month adherence visit slide, because the patient is doing clinically well and nobody flagged the calendar, is the single most common way a CGM that was correctly ordered and correctly covered on day one quietly loses its coverage basis by month seven, with no clinical change to explain it and nothing on the claim itself signaling the problem until it denies.

Commercial and Medicare Advantage plans are where the real routing chaos lives, and this is the part almost nobody publishes clearly. Unlike Original Medicare's single, consistent DME pathway, commercial payers make an independent benefit-design decision about whether a CGM adjudicates as a pharmacy claim (filled at a retail or mail-order pharmacy against the device's NDC number, subject to a pharmacy copay tier) or a DME/medical-benefit claim (billed by a DME supplier using HCPCS codes, subject to the medical deductible and coinsurance). Some plans route every CGM through pharmacy; some route every CGM through DME; and a meaningful and growing number split it by brand, covering one manufacturer's sensor through the pharmacy benefit and a competing brand only through DME, or vice versa. ⚠️ This split is described consistently across DME billing and patient-advocacy sources reviewed for this build, but there is no single canonical payer-agnostic source enumerating which specific commercial plans route which specific device through which benefit, that has to be confirmed per plan, per device, at the payer's own provider portal or by calling the specific plan, before the first order goes out.

The practical failure mode: a biller who successfully got a patient's Dexcom covered through the DME benefit at their last payer submits the same way at the new payer, whose plan design routes that device through pharmacy instead. The DME claim denies, or a pharmacy claim submitted to a DME-only plan rejects outright at the point of sale. Neither denial says "wrong benefit category" in plain language on the remit, it reads as a generic non-covered or not-medically-necessary denial, which sends the practice down an appeal path that was never going to work, because the claim needs to be resubmitted through the other channel entirely, not argued harder on the channel it was submitted through. Building a per-payer CGM routing table into intake, checked at every new-payer verification, not assumed from the last patient, is the single highest-leverage fix available in endocrinology billing, and it costs nothing but a phone call or portal check to implement.

Building the fix into intake doesn't require new software, just a disciplined checklist run at every new-payer verification rather than assumed from the last patient seen:

  • 1Confirm the benefit category first, before the device is selected. Call the payer or check the provider portal for whether CGM sensors adjudicate as pharmacy or DME for this specific plan, not "this payer" broadly, since it can vary by product line within the same carrier.
  • 2Confirm it again per device, not just per plan. A plan that covers one manufacturer's sensor through pharmacy may cover a competing brand only through DME; don't extrapolate from one device's routing to another.
  • 3Document the routing decision in the patient's chart or the practice management system, tied to the specific plan and device, so the next renewal doesn't require re-discovering it from scratch.
  • 4Re-check at every payer change, including a mid-year plan-design change with the same carrier, benefit-design updates can move a device's routing without the patient's plan name changing at all.

A worked scenario: one diabetes patient across a quarter

Tables convey the code families; they don't always convey how the pieces stack for one real patient across real encounters. Here's a representative quarter for a Type 2 diabetes patient with a thyroid nodule, insulin-treated, managed by an endocrinology practice, not a template to copy line for line, but a picture of how the categories in this guide actually interact in sequence.

  • Week 1Initial endocrinology consult. New-patient E/M coded on medical decision making given the diabetes management complexity plus an incidental thyroid nodule found on exam. Diagnosis: E11.65 (type 2 diabetes with hyperglycemia) and E04.1 (nontoxic single thyroid nodule) as separate, both-relevant diagnoses, not combined into one vague code. The visit also documents insulin initiation (supporting Z79.4 going forward) and orders a diagnostic thyroid ultrasound.
  • Week 2Thyroid ultrasound (76536) performed as a distinct encounter, not bundled with any biopsy, since no FNA is being done this visit. Findings support proceeding to biopsy.
  • Week 3Ultrasound-guided FNA of the nodule: 10005, with the cytopathologist billing 88172 separately for on-site adequacy assessment. No separate 76942 or 76536 billed against this same lesion this same session. Modifier 25 is not needed on an E/M this visit unless a genuinely separate, significant E/M service is also documented, if the visit is biopsy-only, no E/M is billed at all.
  • Week 4CGM initiated. The insulin-treated pathway is confirmed and documented (Z79.4 on the chart, insulin dose in the med list); DME claim goes out for E2103/A4239 (non-adjunctive) with modifier KX, after confirming this specific commercial plan routes this specific device through the DME benefit rather than pharmacy. The practice separately bills 95250 for the in-office sensor placement and training, and will bill 95251 once the data is reviewed and interpreted, no more than once per month.
  • Week 6DSMT referral and first session. A written referral is filed from the treating endocrinologist naming the specific training needed; the first individual hour is billed G0108, on a different calendar date than any MNT visit, against the program's on-file ADA/ADCES accreditation.
  • Week 8Cytology returns benign (Bethesda II). No further biopsy needed; the nodule diagnosis (E04.1) continues supporting routine surveillance imaging on its own established schedule rather than repeat biopsy.
  • Monthly, ongoingPrincipal Care Management for the diabetes management specifically (99424, 30+ minutes physician time), separately from the CGM's own 95251 interpretation time and separately from any month DSMT hours are also billed, each activity's time logged and documented on its own.

Nothing in that sequence required an unusual code or an aggressive interpretation of any policy, it required knowing which benefit category each piece belongs to, confirming it before submission rather than assuming it, and keeping each timed service's documentation genuinely separate from the others. That discipline, repeated across a full patient panel, is most of what separates a clean-claim endocrinology practice from one perpetually working a denial queue.

Insulin pump billing and how it interacts with CGM

External insulin infusion pumps are billed under E0784 (external ambulatory infusion pump, insulin), a DME Part B HCPCS Level II code. The supply structure around it is heavily bundled, and billing outside that bundle is one of the more mechanical, easily-audited errors in the specialty, unlike the CGM benefit-path problem, which is genuinely ambiguous across payers, pump supply bundling is a documented, static rule that simply has to be followed correctly rather than investigated per plan.

Per the DME MAC's External Infusion Pumps policy article (A52507, paired with LCD L33794, jointly maintained by CGS Administrators and Noridian Healthcare Solutions), the supply structure is:

Coverage for the pump itself starts well before the supply codes matter, with a lab-based qualification standard that's easy to miss if the ordering workflow doesn't build it in. Per CMS's insulin pump National Coverage Determination framework, a qualifying patient generally has to meet the "insulinopenic" standard: a fasting C-peptide level at or below 110% of the lower limit of normal for the lab's specific reference range, tested only when the fasting blood glucose at the time of the draw was 225 mg/dL or lower (a C-peptide drawn during hyperglycemia above that threshold is not considered valid for this purpose), or alternatively, documented beta-cell autoantibody positivity as a substitute pathway for identifying type 1 diabetes. For a patient with significant renal impairment (creatinine clearance at or below 50 mL/minute), the threshold is adjusted upward to 200% of the lower limit of normal, since reduced kidney clearance raises measured C-peptide independent of actual beta-cell function. ⚠️ This build confirmed the general shape of this criterion against CMS national coverage decision memoranda but did not independently re-verify every numeric threshold against the current live decision memo text; confirm the exact current figures before building an automated coverage check around them, since these are the kind of precise lab thresholds where a stale number produces a wrong answer with confidence.

Medicare's insulin pump coverage criteria also include a self-testing frequency requirement, historically stated as an average of at least four blood-glucose checks per day, and a patient using an integrated CGM is generally treated as meeting that testing-frequency requirement automatically through the CGM's continuous data stream, rather than needing separate fingerstick logs. ⚠️ Confirm the exact current wording of this provision against your jurisdiction's live DME MAC policy article before building it into a coverage checklist, since infusion pump policy language is revised periodically and this build relied on a policy-article summary rather than the full current PDF text.

Where pump and CGM billing intersect: a growing share of pumps are now sold as part of an automated insulin delivery (AID) system, Tandem's t:slim X2 with Control-IQ+, Medtronic's MiniMed 780G, Insulet's Omnipod 5, and comparable hybrid closed-loop systems, where the pump's algorithm adjusts insulin delivery in real time using an integrated or connected CGM sensor. The pump itself still bills under E0784 (or its equivalent tubeless-pump code depending on the specific system) with its A4224/A4225 supply structure; the CGM sensor component bills under its own device and supply codes (E2102/A4238 for an adjunctive-integrated sensor, or E2103/A4239 if the specific sensor is coded non-adjunctively) as a logically separate DME claim, even though the patient experiences it as a single integrated system. ⚠️ This build could not independently confirm, against a primary CMS source, exactly how every current AID system's integrated sensor is coded when bundled with the pump versus billed as a stand-alone CGM, manufacturer billing-and-coding guides describe this device-by-device, and the specific adjunctive/non-adjunctive classification has shifted as devices have been reclassified, so confirm the current classification for the specific pump-and-sensor combination against the device manufacturer's current reimbursement guide and the payer's own policy before billing an AID system claim.

The denial pattern to watch for: billing the pump supply allowance and the CGM supply allowance as though they were a single bundled claim, or omitting the CGM component entirely because it "comes with" the pump clinically. They are two separate DME benefit claims with two separate code families, two separate modifier sets, and in commercial plans, potentially two separate benefit-routing decisions layered on top of each other, a plan that routes the pump through DME without hesitation may still route its companion sensor through the pharmacy benefit, which means one patient's AID system can legitimately generate claims down two different channels at once.

A second AID-specific trap sits in the coverage-criteria timeline. Both the pump and the CGM component carry their own initial-qualification and continued-use documentation requirements on their respective LCDs, and they don't automatically renew on the same calendar. A practice that tracks only the pump's periodic re-certification visit, and lets the CGM's separate six-month adherence-visit requirement lapse, ends up with a fully functional AID system where the pump supplies keep paying and the sensor supplies quietly stop, a gap that's easy to miss because the patient's therapy hasn't changed at all, only the paperwork behind one half of it. Tracking both LCDs' visit calendars independently, rather than assuming one covers the other, is the fix.

E/M coding: chronic care management and principal care management

Endocrinology is a chronic-disease-management specialty more than a procedural one, and a meaningful share of its recurring, billable revenue sits outside the office-visit E/M codes entirely, in the time-based chronic and principal care management codes. Office-visit E/M levels (99212–99215 for established patients) are billed the same way here as in any other specialty, on total time or medical decision making, whichever level is fully satisfied, and this guide won't re-derive that general structure; what's specific to endocrinology is how much of the actual clinical workload happens outside that visit entirely. Missing this category isn't a coding error so much as a revenue-capture gap, the clinical work of monthly diabetes and thyroid management is frequently already happening; it just isn't being billed.

This category is worth naming as a revenue-capture issue specifically, not just a coding one. A patient with insulin-treated type 2 diabetes, a thyroid nodule under surveillance, and an active CGM already generates real clinical coordination work between visits, reviewing device downloads, adjusting insulin doses by phone or portal message, coordinating the next imaging study, that a practice is often already performing and simply not billing, because that work doesn't fit neatly into an office-visit E/M code. CCM and PCM exist specifically to capture that between-visit time, and a practice that has never built a workflow for logging and billing it isn't avoiding a coding risk; it's leaving a predictable, monthly, already-performed service unbilled.

PCM is the code family built for exactly what an endocrinology practice does most: managing one high-risk chronic condition, diabetes, a thyroid disorder, an adrenal or pituitary condition, on an ongoing basis, distinct from the broader multi-condition CCM model built for primary care. The distinction matters for concurrent billing: CCM and PCM cannot be billed by the same practitioner for the same patient in the same calendar month. They can be billed concurrently by two different practitioners, a primary care physician billing CCM for the patient's overall chronic-condition burden while the endocrinologist bills PCM for diabetes specifically, but only when the conditions being addressed are genuinely distinct, each practitioner maintains its own care plan (PCM's can be disease-specific rather than comprehensive), and the clinical staff time behind each claim doesn't overlap. Billing both from the same practice, or for the same underlying condition, is a duplicate-service pattern that gets caught.

CGM data and Remote Physiologic Monitoring (RPM) are a separate overlap trap worth naming explicitly, because the two look almost identical from the patient's chair. RPM (99453 setup, 99454 device supply with data transmission, 99457/99458 monitoring/treatment management time) requires its own qualifying device and its own data-transmission threshold, per the current RPM billing structure, at least two separate days of readings transmitted within the 30-day period, and RPM time cannot be double-counted against time already billed under a CGM professional-interpretation code (95251) or under CCM/PCM for the same monitoring activity in the same period. A practice cannot bill a patient's CGM data stream simultaneously as a DME supply claim, a 95251 interpretation, and an RPM data-transmission code for the same underlying data in the same month; each code family requires its own distinct qualifying activity and time, not three billing labels stacked on one clinical task. Choose the single most appropriate pathway for how the data is actually being used clinically that month, and document which one it is.

Time counted toward CCM or PCM cannot also be counted toward the same month's E/M visit, DSMT, or MNT time, each service requires its own, non-overlapping block of clock time, separately documented. A practice that logs one lump "diabetes management" time entry and tries to apply it across an E/M note and a PCM claim in the same month is building a claim that doesn't survive an audit of either service. The full time-tracking and documentation workflow for making CCM/PCM sustainable in an endocrinology practice is covered in our E/M coding guide.

Diabetes Self-Management Training (DSMT)

DSMT is a distinct, defined Medicare benefit, not a loosely-billed patient-education add-on, with its own hour structure, its own referral requirement, and its own frequency limits that are unrelated to the E/M visit schedule.

The referral is the single most common point of failure. It has to come from the practitioner actually managing the patient's diabetes and has to identify what training is being ordered; a generic "diabetes education" order without that specificity is a frequent audit finding even when the training itself was delivered exactly as intended. Because DSMT is a defined national Medicare benefit governed by statute and regulation rather than a locally-administered coverage policy, there is no MAC-specific Local Coverage Determination to check the way there is for CGM or imaging, the hour and referral rules above apply uniformly nationwide under Original Medicare.

Program accreditation is a separate, upstream requirement that has nothing to do with any individual claim and everything to do with whether the practice can bill G0108/G0109 at all. The DSMT program itself, not the individual clinician delivering the training, must hold current recognition from the American Diabetes Association or accreditation from the Association of Diabetes Care and Education Specialists before any claim can be submitted. A copy of that accreditation certificate, with a cover letter on the organization's letterhead, has to be on file with the local MAC; claims submitted before accreditation is in place are not billable, though some MACs allow a short retroactive billing window, commonly cited around 30 days, once accreditation actually comes through. A practice standing up a new DSMT program has to treat this accreditation timeline as a hard prerequisite in the launch plan, not paperwork that can be completed in parallel with the first cohort of patients.

⚠️ Billing-reference sources consistently describe a same-day unit ceiling on DSMT specifically, up to 6 units (3 hours) of individual G0108 and up to 12 units (6 hours) of group G0109 per day, with no rounding permitted on partial 30-minute increments, though this build could not independently confirm the current numeric ceiling against CMS's own primary MUE table. Treat it as the working assumption for scheduling a single day's training load, but verify the live figure before building it into a hard system edit.

DSMT's benefit structure is fundamentally different from a standard E/M visit: it's a timed, curriculum-based education service with its own annual cap, not a medical-necessity-driven, unlimited-frequency service the way office visits are. Billing it like an E/M code, assuming more sessions simply means more revenue with no ceiling, runs straight into the hour cap and produces frequency denials once the 10-hour initial or 2-hour follow-up allotment is exceeded within its benefit period. Full workflow detail, group-versus-individual documentation requirements, and the complete referral template are in our DSMT billing guide.

Medical Nutrition Therapy (MNT) and its overlap with DSMT

Medical Nutrition Therapy is a separate, also-defined Medicare benefit, most often delivered by a registered dietitian, and it is frequently confused with DSMT because both address diabetes self-care and both are timed, referral-gated services covered the same benefit year. The two exist for genuinely different purposes even though they overlap clinically: DSMT teaches the broader skill set of living with and self-managing diabetes day to day, while MNT is specifically a nutrition assessment and intervention delivered by a dietitian, with its own initial-versus-follow-up code structure mirroring the individual-versus-repeat-visit pattern seen throughout this guide's other timed benefits.

A practice that schedules a combined "diabetes education and nutrition" visit on a single day, intending to bill both G0108/G0109 and 97802/97803 for that date, will have one of the two claims deny; the fix is scheduling logistics, not a coding workaround, put the two services on different calendar days, even a day apart, and both are billable in full.

For an endocrinology practice that manages diabetes heavily, MNT is worth building into the same intake workflow as DSMT precisely because the two benefits are complementary rather than competing: a patient can receive up to 10 hours of DSMT and a full course of MNT in the same benefit year, as long as the scheduling respects the same-day restriction and each referral is tracked and filed separately. The most common operational failure isn't a coding mistake at all, it's a shared scheduling calendar that doesn't flag the same-day conflict until after both appointments are already booked.

Thyroid biopsy and FNA coding

Thyroid nodule workup is endocrinology's highest-volume procedural category, and the coding decision comes down to two questions the operative note has to answer clearly: what imaging guidance was used, and was the specimen obtained by fine needle aspiration for cytology or by a larger core needle for histology. Get those two questions answered precisely in the note, and the correct code family follows directly; the errors in this category almost never come from genuine coding ambiguity, they come from operative documentation that describes the clinical findings thoroughly but never states the needle type or confirms which lesion was targeted when more than one nodule was present.

The bundling trap that catches practices most often: ultrasound guidance (76942) and diagnostic thyroid ultrasound (76536) are not separately billable against the same lesion in the same session as an ultrasound-guided FNA, because 10005 already includes the guidance and imaging interpretation as part of its own descriptor. Pathology coordination: when a cytopathologist or cytotechnologist provides an immediate, on-site adequacy assessment of the aspirate during the procedure, confirming there's enough cellular material before the patient leaves, rather than finding out days later the sample was non-diagnostic, that evaluation is reported separately using 88172 (first evaluation episode at the site) and +88177 (each additional evaluation episode at the same site, for example after a repeat pass). This is billed by the pathology side of the encounter, not the proceduralist, and coordinating the timing and site-of-service billing agreement between the endocrinology practice and the reading pathologist up front avoids duplicate-billing confusion when both parties' claims land on the same date of service.

What happens after the biopsy also shapes what's billable next. Thyroid cytology is reported using the Bethesda System for Reporting Thyroid Cytopathology, a six-tier diagnostic categorization (nondiagnostic, benign, atypia/follicular lesion of undetermined significance, follicular neoplasm/suspicious for follicular neoplasm, suspicious for malignancy, and malignant) that drives the next billable event far more than the biopsy CPT code itself does. A nondiagnostic result routes back to a repeat FNA, appropriately billed again as a new 10005/10006 episode rather than treated as a correction of the first claim. A result in the indeterminate middle categories frequently triggers molecular testing on the same aspirate, a lab-billed service coordinated with, but separate from, the proceduralist's own claim. Documenting the Bethesda category in the chart isn't just a clinical best practice, it's the evidence that justifies why a second procedure, a molecular test, or a surgical referral followed the first biopsy, which matters directly if a payer questions the medical necessity of a repeat FNA within a short window of the first one.

When cytology or surgical pathology confirms malignancy, the coding shifts from the E04/E05/E06 structural-and-functional families to C73 (malignant neoplasm of the thyroid gland) for the active-treatment period, and then to Z85.850 (personal history of malignant neoplasm of the thyroid) once treatment is complete and the patient enters surveillance. This transition matters operationally, not just semantically: coding a post-thyroidectomy surveillance visit with an active-malignancy code when the patient is actually in remission can misstate the encounter's own medical-necessity basis, while coding an active-treatment visit with the history code can undersupport a legitimately higher level of care. Post-thyroidectomy patients also generate a predictable, ongoing E/M and lab-coordination pattern, periodic TSH-suppression monitoring and thyroglobulin surveillance, that is billed as standard E/M and lab-ordering work rather than under any thyroid-cancer-specific procedure code; the ICD-10 code carried on those claims should reflect the surveillance status (Z85.850) rather than defaulting to the original malignancy code indefinitely.

ICD-10 specificity: diabetes and thyroid disorders

Diabetes coding is entirely built around the fifth and sixth character: type, complication category, and specific manifestation. An unspecified diabetes code doesn't just under-communicate the patient's condition, it frequently fails medical-necessity checks for the very tests and devices this guide covers, because a payer's coverage policy for a CGM, a pump, or a repeat nephropathy workup checks against the specific complication code, not the bare disease category.

The type itself is a separate decision from the complication, and getting it wrong at the category level undermines everything built on top of it. E10 is type 1; E11 is type 2; the less-common categories matter just as much when they genuinely apply and are frequently miscoded into E11 out of habit: E08 (diabetes due to an underlying condition, such as a pancreatic disorder), E09 (drug- or chemical-induced diabetes, including steroid-induced hyperglycemia that resolves when the drug is withdrawn), and E13 (other specified diabetes, including genetic-syndrome-associated forms). A patient with drug-induced hyperglycemia from long-term glucocorticoid therapy coded as E11 rather than E09 is coded to the wrong category entirely, not just imprecisely, the two describe genuinely different clinical entities, and the distinction can matter for both medical necessity and for how the condition is expected to resolve.

Modifiers

Endocrinology's modifier logic centers on device coverage pathway attestation and procedure-versus-visit separation, which is a different center of gravity from a procedural specialty's component-split modifiers.

KX / KS
  • KX, insulin-treated CGM coverage pathway; attests the LCD's insulin-treated criteria are documented.
  • KS, non-insulin-treated, problematic-hypoglycemia CGM pathway; attests the specific hypoglycemia threshold is documented instead.
  • These describe two mutually exclusive coverage theories, never both on the same claim, and never chosen without checking which one the chart actually supports.
CG / KF
  • CG, policy criteria for initial and continued CGM coverage have been met (non-adjunctive CGM claims).
  • KF, identifies an FDA Class III device; required on adjunctive CGM claims always, and on non-adjunctive claims only when the specific device carries that classification.
25 / 59 & X-modifiers
  • 25, a significant, separately identifiable E/M service the same day as a minor procedure like a thyroid FNA; the note has to show E/M work genuinely distinct from the biopsy decision itself, not just "patient was also seen."
  • 59 / XS, distinct lesion or structure, most often two separately documented thyroid nodules biopsied in the same session; XS is generally the more defensible choice since the distinction is anatomic.
GA / GY / GZ used interchangeably
  • GA (ABN signed and on file, service may not be covered) is not the same as GY (statutorily excluded, never covered under any circumstance) or GZ (expect denial, no ABN obtained). Appending GA when no ABN was actually signed shifts financial liability to the patient without the documentation to support it, a compliance problem, not a shortcut. Reaching for GY on a device that's simply borderline on this patient's specific criteria, rather than statutorily excluded outright, misclassifies the denial reason entirely.

Modifier 95 (synchronous telemedicine service) applies wherever this guide's telehealth-eligible services, DSMT, MNT, CGM interpretation, or an E/M visit supporting CCM/PCM, are delivered via real-time audio-video rather than in person; audio-only delivery under the current flexibility uses modifier 93 instead, as noted earlier in this guide. The two are not interchangeable, and using 95 for a service that was actually audio-only misstates the modality on the claim, which matters directly if a payer's telehealth policy pays differently by modality or requires the specific modifier to match documented consent language in the chart.

The single most common modifier error in the specialty isn't malicious upcoding, it's picking KX by default because it's the more familiar modifier, on a patient whose chart actually documents the KS pathway (or vice versa), because the biller never confirmed which coverage theory the visit note supports before the claim went out. Confirm the pathway from the documentation first; the modifier is a statement about what's in the chart, not a switch that makes the claim more likely to pay. The same discipline applies to every modifier in this list: each one is a factual claim about what the record contains, and treating any of them as a lever to try before checking the chart is how a single modifier error turns into a pattern an auditor can find across dozens of claims at once, rather than one isolated mistake.

NCCI edits and MUE bundling

The same two CMS values that govern bundling in every other specialty govern it here: the NCCI Procedure-to-Procedure modifier indicator, and the MUE Adjudication Indicator (MAI) attached to per-code unit caps. Neither is a guess, and neither should be treated as a fixed fact recalled from memory, both are pair-specific and code-specific values that change quarterly.

The NCCI modifier indicator attached to a Column 1/Column 2 code pair:

  • 0Never bypassable. No modifier overrides it, however well documented. The clearest endocrinology example this build could confirm through billing-reference sources: ultrasound guidance (76942) is not separately payable against an ultrasound-guided FNA (10005) for the same lesion, because the guidance is already part of 10005's own descriptor, this isn't a modifier-bypassable edit, it's a code-definition overlap.
  • 1Bypassable with documentation. An NCCI-associated modifier (59 or a specific X-modifier) can override the edit, but only where the record shows the second service was genuinely separate, a second, distinctly documented thyroid nodule biopsied in the same session is the clearest endocrinology example.
  • 9Edit deleted. The pair no longer applies.

The MUE Adjudication Indicator (MAI) attached to a per-code, per-day unit cap:

  • 1Claim-line edit. Units above the cap deny that line, but genuinely repeated services can sometimes be split across lines with documentation.

Payer-specific nuances

Everything this guide has said about the CGM benefit-path problem gets sharper, not simpler, once Medicare Advantage and Medicaid managed care enter the picture, because both layer their own network and utilization-management rules on top of the underlying Original Medicare or state Medicaid framework.

Medicare Advantage plans routinely require an in-network DME supplier for CGM and pump claims, on top of whatever pharmacy-versus-DME routing decision the plan has already made for that device. A patient whose device and coverage pathway are both correct can still see a clean denial, or a full out-of-pocket charge, simply because the claim went through a DME supplier outside that specific MA plan's network, a failure mode entirely independent of medical necessity or even benefit-category routing, and one more reason a per-plan verification step belongs in intake rather than being assumed from the device or the diagnosis alone. Some MA plans have moved the other direction on cost-sharing specifically to compete on this benefit, waiving the standard 20% coinsurance for in-network CGM supplies as a supplemental benefit, which makes getting the network determination right worth real money to the patient, not just an administrative formality.

⚠️ Public comment filed with CMS in 2025 specifically raised uneven enforcement of diabetes-supply benefit rules between the DME and pharmacy channels as an active policy concern, which this build cites as evidence the routing problem this guide centers on is recognized industry-wide, not a minor edge case, but it does not by itself establish what any specific plan's current rule is. Nothing here substitutes for calling the specific plan.

DSMT program accreditation carries a similar cross-payer wrinkle: while ADA recognition or ADCES accreditation is a Medicare-specific requirement tied directly to G0108/G0109 billing, a number of commercial payers independently look for the same accreditation as evidence the program meets a recognized quality standard before they'll credential it for reimbursement, even though their own billing codes and referral rules may otherwise differ from Medicare's. A program built to Medicare's accreditation standard from the outset generally clears this bar for commercial payers too, which makes it worth pursuing even for a practice whose patient mix is currently mostly commercial rather than Medicare.

Medicaid managed care organizations add a further layer: a state's fee-for-service Medicaid program may cover DSMT, CGM, or a GLP-1 indication a certain way, while a managed care organization operating that state's Medicaid managed care product applies its own prior-authorization and network rules on top of the state's baseline coverage policy. Treating "Medicaid covers this in our state" as a single fact, without checking whether the specific patient is in fee-for-service Medicaid or a specific MCO plan, is a common and avoidable source of surprise denials in states with heavy Medicaid managed care penetration.

Prior authorization: CGMs, insulin pumps, and GLP-1 agonists

Prior authorization touches almost every high-cost item in endocrinology, and the burden has grown fastest around GLP-1 agonists, not devices, though devices carry their own PA requirements that are just as easy to miss, and a PA denial behaves nothing like a coding denial: there's no claim to correct and resubmit, only a request that has to be rebuilt with the documentation the payer actually required the first time.

CGMs and insulin pumps under Medicare Advantage plans frequently require prior authorization even where Original Medicare's DME LCD criteria would otherwise cover the device automatically once documented; failing to obtain that separate MA-plan authorization before dispensing is a well-documented, avoidable failure point distinct from the DME coverage-criteria documentation covered earlier in this guide. Commercial plans layer their own PA requirements on top of, and sometimes with different criteria than, the DME MAC LCD framework, a device that clearly meets Medicare's insulin-treated criteria may still need a separate commercial PA built around that specific plan's own medical policy.

GLP-1 agonists (semaglutide, tirzepatide, and the broader class) now carry some of the heaviest prior-authorization burden in outpatient medicine, and it is highly topical for exactly the reason every endocrinology practice already feels: demand driven by both diabetes and weight-loss indications has pushed payers toward aggressive utilization management. The step-therapy pattern that shows up most consistently across payer PA policies reviewed for this build:

  • 1Metformin first, almost universally. Most commercial payers require a documented trial of metformin at a maximally tolerated dose, with a noted inadequate response, intolerance, or contraindication, before a GLP-1 is authorized for the diabetes indication.
  • 2A second agent, frequently. Many plans additionally require a documented trial of one more class, commonly an SGLT2 inhibitor, a sulfonylurea, or a DPP-4 inhibitor, before approving a GLP-1.
  • 3Tirzepatide-specific step-up, on some plans. A subset of payer policies require documented trial and failure of a standard GLP-1 (semaglutide or dulaglutide) before authorizing tirzepatide specifically, treating it as a step above rather than a first-line equivalent.
  • !Indication matters as much as the drug. Medicare Part D covers semaglutide and tirzepatide for the type 2 diabetes indication, not the weight-loss indication; a prescription written or coded toward obesity rather than diabetes for a Medicare beneficiary follows an entirely different, far less certain coverage path. ⚠️ Anti-obesity medication coverage policy is actively shifting, some state Medicaid programs are adding weight-loss GLP-1 coverage while others are removing it in the same period, and Medicare Part D plans are newly permitted, but not required, to cover anti-obesity medications following the removal of the statutory weight-loss-drug exclusion. Confirm the current policy for the specific plan and the specific indication at the time of the prescription; this is not a rule that holds still year over year.

Medicaid coverage of GLP-1s for the weight-loss indication is genuinely fragmented by state, and it moves in both directions. As of early 2026, roughly a dozen state Medicaid programs cover GLP-1 agonists for weight loss, while other states have never added that coverage and at least one state (Pennsylvania) eliminated its existing weight-loss GLP-1 Medicaid coverage effective January 2026. The diabetes indication is far more consistently covered across state Medicaid programs than the weight-loss indication, which means the practical prior-authorization strategy for a Medicaid patient often depends entirely on whether the chart genuinely supports the diabetes indication rather than obesity alone, not because one indication is clinically more legitimate, but because it's the indication with a real coverage path in more states. ⚠️ State Medicaid GLP-1 policy is one of the fastest-moving areas in this entire guide; confirm the specific state's current policy at the time of prescribing rather than relying on last year's coverage list, since programs have moved in both directions within the same 12-month period.

The operational fix mirrors the CGM lesson above: a prior authorization submitted with only the ICD-10 diagnosis and no documented medication history is close to a guaranteed initial denial for this drug class, regardless of how clearly indicated the drug is clinically. Building the step-therapy documentation, prior agent, dose, duration, and documented outcome, into the chart before the PA is submitted, rather than reconstructing it after a denial, is the difference between an approval on first submission and a multi-week appeal cycle the patient feels directly in a treatment delay.

When a PA is denied anyway, the escalation path matters as much as the initial submission. A peer-to-peer review with the plan's medical director is frequently the fastest route to reversal when the step-therapy record genuinely exists but wasn't captured clearly in the original submission, scheduling it promptly, with the prescribing endocrinologist prepared to speak to the specific prior agents tried, resolves more denials than a written appeal alone. Where the plan's own policy is being applied incorrectly (denying a documented tirzepatide step-up requirement that the plan's own published policy doesn't actually require, for example), citing the plan's own medical policy document by name and section in the appeal is more effective than a general clinical argument. And where state law provides a prior-authorization exemption or expedited-review right for certain chronic-disease medications, that exemption has to be raised explicitly, plans do not volunteer it. Full payer-by-payer PA workflow detail, including how to structure the clinical documentation for a first-pass approval, is in our prior authorization guide.

Credentialing and DMEPOS enrollment for endocrinology practices

Every other section of this guide assumes the DME claim is going out correctly once the coverage criteria, codes, and modifiers are right. This section addresses a prior question that competitor content almost universally skips: whether the practice is even legally positioned to submit that claim under its own name in the first place. This is the gap most competitor content skips entirely: an endocrinology practice that dispenses CGMs, insulin pumps, or their supplies directly from the office, rather than routing every patient to an outside DME supplier, is itself acting as a DMEPOS supplier, and that carries its own enrollment track separate from the practice's standard Medicare and commercial credentialing.

Any entity, including a physician practice, that sells or rents Part B-covered items to patients has to hold DMEPOS billing privileges to bill Medicare for those items, which means a separate enrollment application and its own supplier number, distinct from the practice's physician NPI enrollment. Two requirements that ordinarily apply to DMEPOS suppliers carry a specific, narrow exception for physicians and qualifying nonphysician practitioners that's worth knowing precisely rather than assuming applies broadly:

  • 1The $50,000 surety bond that DMEPOS suppliers ordinarily must post is waived for a physician or nonphysician practitioner furnishing DME items only to their own patients as part of their professional service, not to the general public or to patients of other referring practices. A practice that expands into dispensing more broadly than its own patient panel should confirm whether it still qualifies for this exception before assuming it does.
  • 2DMEPOS accreditation (the quality-standards accreditation ordinarily required of DME suppliers) also carries an exemption for certain eligible professionals, including physicians, dispensing under qualifying conditions.

⚠️ Both exceptions are conditioned on specific, narrow criteria in federal regulation (42 CFR 424.57) rather than a blanket exemption for "physicians" as a category, this build confirmed the existence and general shape of both exceptions through CMS supplier-standards guidance but did not independently verify every current qualifying condition against the live regulation text. Confirm the practice's own eligibility for each exception, in writing, before assuming either applies, since incorrectly billing as an exempt supplier when the practice doesn't actually qualify is an enrollment and compliance problem far more serious than a single claim denial.

What doesn't have an exception: the DMEPOS supplier billing privileges themselves. A practice cannot bill Medicare Part B for a CGM, a pump, or their supplies under the physician's standard enrollment alone, the DMEPOS enrollment (Form CMS-855S) has to be completed and approved first, before the first device claim goes out, and it comes with obligations that continue well past the initial approval:

  • 1An unannounced site visit is part of Medicare's validation process for every new DMEPOS enrollment, conducted separately from any accreditation survey and focused specifically on compliance with the DMEPOS Supplier Standards, this happens before the practice's first device claims are approved to pay, not as a formality after the fact.
  • 2Revalidation runs on a three-year cycle after the initial enrollment, with a 60-day window to submit complete revalidation information once CMS sends the request, plus a fresh site visit at that revalidation and again at any change of ownership or tax ID. Missing a revalidation deadline risks the DMEPOS billing privileges being deactivated, which stops device claims from paying even though the underlying physician enrollment is untouched.
  • 3Each physical location that furnishes DMEPOS to patients needs its own CMS-855S enrollment, a practice with more than one office location dispensing CGMs or pumps directly enrolls each location separately, not once for the group as a whole (locations used purely as warehouses or repair sites are excepted).

Practices that have historically referred every device to an outside DME supplier and are now considering bringing that revenue in-house should treat this enrollment lead time, and the ongoing three-year revalidation obligation it creates, as a real planning constraint, not a formality that can be completed the week dispensing starts. The upside is real: a practice that refers every CGM and pump order to an outside supplier is handing that supplier the device and supply margin entirely, on top of losing visibility into whether the order was ever actually fulfilled or billed correctly at all. Bringing dispensing in-house, once the enrollment and accreditation groundwork is done, converts a referral the practice currently gets no revenue from into a second billable claim stream sitting directly alongside the professional CGM interpretation codes and the E/M or PCM visit that ordered the device in the first place, the same clinical encounter, captured more completely.

Pull back from the code-by-code detail and one thread runs through nearly every section of this guide: endocrinology rarely fails on medical necessity in the way a payer's denial letter implies. It fails on routing, the wrong benefit category, the wrong network, the wrong same-day combination, the wrong modifier attesting to a coverage theory the chart doesn't actually support. None of that requires better clinical documentation to fix; it requires confirming the routing before the claim goes out, every time, for every payer, rather than assuming this patient's plan works the way the last one did. That single habit, built into intake rather than left to the biller discovering it on a remittance advice, is worth more to an endocrinology practice's clean-claim rate than any single coding correction in this guide.

Frequently asked questions

Why do continuous glucose monitor claims deny even though the patient clearly qualifies?

Almost always because the claim went down the wrong benefit path, not because the patient failed medical necessity. Medicare treats every therapeutic CGM as durable medical equipment billed under Part B, never the pharmacy benefit, and requires the correct device code (E2102 adjunctive or E2103 non-adjunctive), the matching supply code (A4238 or A4239), and the KX or KS modifier depending on whether the patient is insulin-treated. Commercial payers are less consistent: some plans route the same Dexcom or Libre prescription through the pharmacy benefit at a retail pharmacy, others require DME supplier billing, and sending the claim through the wrong one produces a rejection or a full-price patient charge that looks like a coverage denial but is really a routing error. Confirm the plan's CGM benefit path before the first fill, not after the denial.

Is a continuous glucose monitor billed through the pharmacy benefit or the DME benefit?

Under Original Medicare, always DME, billed under Part B through a DME supplier against the Glucose Monitors LCD, never through Part D. Commercial and Medicare Advantage plans vary by carrier and sometimes by specific plan design: some adjudicate CGM sensors as a pharmacy claim using the device's NDC number at a retail or mail-order pharmacy, others require DME channel billing with HCPCS codes, and a growing number split it by device, covering one brand through pharmacy and a competing brand through DME. There is no universal rule for commercial payers, so this has to be confirmed per plan before the first order goes out, not assumed from how the patient's last payer handled it.

Can DSMT and Medical Nutrition Therapy be billed on the same day?

No. Medicare covers both Diabetes Self-Management Training and Medical Nutrition Therapy in full, without reducing either benefit, but only if they are not furnished on the same date of service for the same beneficiary. They also require separate referrals from the treating practitioner. Scheduling both benefits on different days, even the same week, protects both claims; billing them same-day is a routine and entirely avoidable denial.

What's the difference between CPT 10005 and CPT 60100 for a thyroid biopsy?

10005 is a fine needle aspiration performed under ultrasound guidance, reported once for the first lesion with add-on code 10006 for each additional lesion in the same session; the guidance and imaging documentation are bundled into 10005 itself, so billing ultrasound code 76942 or 76536 separately for the same lesion is an unbundling error. 60100 is a percutaneous core needle biopsy using a larger cutting needle for histology rather than cytology, and it is not interchangeable with the FNA family. Choose the code based on what the operative note actually describes: needle gauge, technique, and whether the specimen went to cytology or histology, not on which code the payer historically paid faster.

Do we need a physician referral for diabetes self-management training?

Yes. Medicare requires a written or electronic referral from the physician or qualified nonphysician practitioner who is medically managing the beneficiary's diabetes before DSMT can be billed under G0108 or G0109. Without that referral on file, the claim is not billable regardless of how much genuinely useful training was delivered. The referral has to name the specific training needed; a generic diabetes-education order without documented content doesn't satisfy the requirement on audit.

Can chronic care management and principal care management be billed for the same patient in the same month?

Not by the same practitioner for the same patient in the same calendar month. Two different practitioners can bill both concurrently, for example a primary care physician billing CCM for the patient's full chronic-condition burden while an endocrinologist bills PCM for diabetes alone, but only when the conditions being managed are genuinely distinct, each practitioner maintains its own care plan, and the clinical staff time behind each claim doesn't overlap. Billing both from the same practice for the same condition in the same month is a duplicate-service problem waiting to be caught.

Why are GLP-1 prescriptions denied even with a type 2 diabetes diagnosis?

Diagnosis alone rarely clears prior authorization for this drug class. Most commercial payers require documented step therapy first, typically metformin at a maximally tolerated dose with a noted inadequate response, intolerance, or contraindication, and frequently a second agent such as an SGLT2 inhibitor or sulfonylurea, before a GLP-1 is approved, and some plans require trial and failure of a standard GLP-1 before authorizing tirzepatide specifically. A prior authorization submitted with only the diabetes diagnosis and no documented medication history is close to a guaranteed initial denial regardless of how clearly indicated the drug is clinically.

What's the difference between the KX and KS modifiers on a CGM claim?

KX attests that the beneficiary is insulin-treated and that the DME LCD's coverage criteria for an insulin-treated patient are documented in the record. KS attests the opposite pathway: the beneficiary is not insulin-treated but has a documented history of problematic hypoglycemia meeting the LCD's specific threshold. Appending the wrong one doesn't just risk a denial, it misrepresents which coverage pathway the claim is actually relying on, which is exactly the kind of claim a post-payment audit is built to catch. Confirm which pathway applies before the modifier is chosen, not after.
