Our complete care management guide

Care management documentation that holds up when someone asks to see it.

Medicare does not give care management a required template, which is why the notes vary so widely and why auditors are good at spotting the weak ones. What decides an audit is simple: does the record show, for this patient, why the service is needed and what was actually done each month? This page covers the care plan, the goals, the time log and the call note.

Key takeaways

  • Patient-specific beats polished. Auditors look for this patient’s symptoms, thresholds and actions. A note that describes the program in general terms, or that is cloned across patients, is the weak one.
  • Every goal needs a named measure, a baseline, a target and a date. “Improve pain” is not a goal.
  • Log every minute at the time. Date, staff, credential, activity, condition, minutes counted, and whether it was a live call. Notes written from memory at month end are where minutes get inflated.
  • Separate counted from uncounted work. Care plan work counts; detailed menu writing or scheduling does not.
  • Medicare does not require a provider signature on the CCM care plan, but a provider-approved plan, signed or attested in the EHR, is the best evidence of oversight, so we recommend it.

The care plan: what goes in it

One care plan per patient. For PCM it covers the one qualifying condition; for CCM it covers every qualifying condition. It lives in the practice’s EHR, the patient receives a copy, and it is updated as goals change. Problems, medications and medication allergies have to be recorded in the certified EHR’s structured fields; the rest of the plan can be an attached document as long as it is electronic and can be shared with the care team, including by fax where that is the only option.

Care plan sections and what to write in each.
SectionWhat to write
Problem listEach qualifying condition with its ICD-10 code, and related problems that affect it.
Expected outcome and prognosisOne or two sentences per condition, for example “stable to improved function with conservative care; prognosis fair.”
Measurable goalsFor each condition: a named measure, a baseline number, a target number and a date.
Symptom managementWhich symptoms are tracked, how often, what the patient does when they change, and when staff escalate to the practitioner. Patient-specific.
Medication managementCurrent medications for the condition; adherence review by staff; any change made only by the practitioner.
Planned interventions and who does themPractitioner, dietitian, physical therapist, nurse, with frequency.
CoordinationOther practitioners, therapy, imaging, community resources.
Functional and psychosocial assessmentBrief: mobility, sleep, mood screen, support at home.
Review scheduleFor example, reviewed monthly by care staff and at each office visit by the practitioner.
Practitioner approvalName, date and an approval in the EHR. Recommended practice, not a Medicare requirement for CCM.

For complex CCM, the care plan needs substantial revision by the practitioner’s team, not staff alone, and the service needs the practitioner’s own moderate or high complexity decision-making, so it rarely fits a staff-led model. We do not recommend it for that reason.

If a program uses a separate protocol document, such as a dietary or exercise protocol, attaching the PDF to the care plan is acceptable. CMS has said the format of the care plan matters less than the care planning process, provided the plan is captured electronically and shared in a timely way. Best practice is to attach the file and type a short summary into the EHR (goals, interventions, who does what and the review date), so the care team sees it without opening the attachment, and to give the patient a copy.

Writing measurable goals

The most common weakness in care plans is the goal. A goal that names no instrument, no baseline and no date cannot show progress, which is the one thing a monthly care management service has to show.

Weak goals and the version that works. Bracketed numbers are placeholders for the patient’s own values.
WeakStronger
“Reduce BMI from 35 to 30 over the year.”“Weight: 5% loss in 6 months (from [242] lb to [230] lb), reviewed monthly; long-term goal BMI 30 by [date]. Tied to the pain condition: to reduce knee joint load.” An interim target is easier to show progress against every month.
“Improve pain interference from 4/5 to 3/5 over 12 weeks.”“Pain interference: PEG score from [7.0] to [5.0] or lower by [date], 12 weeks”, or a PROMIS-29 pain interference T-score from [64] to [59] or lower. Name the instrument; a 5-point rating with no source is not a recognized scale.
(missing)“Function: PROMIS-29 physical function T-score up by at least [3] points by [date]”, or “walk 20 minutes, 5 days a week, by [date].”
(missing, where relevant)“Sleep: pain interference with sleep from [7] to [4] out of 10 by [date].”

The PEG is a three-item scale of pain intensity, interference with enjoyment of life and interference with general activity, each scored 0 to 10, validated in patients with chronic musculoskeletal pain (Krebs and colleagues, Journal of General Internal Medicine, 2009). It is short enough to ask weekly, which is why it works as a monthly care management measure.

Writing symptom management that is about this patient

The second weakness is symptom management that reads as a description of the program. Replace program language with this patient’s tracking schedule, triggers and escalation thresholds. A template to adapt:

Patient records pain interference ([PEG]), [activity], [sleep] and [weight] in the [app name] at least [3] times a week. Care staff review entries before each monthly care management call and at least [weekly].

Self-management plan: [patient-specific steps, for example: anti-inflammatory eating pattern agreed with the dietitian; walking program per physical therapy; sleep routine; pacing on high-pain days]. Patient contacts the clinic for [specific triggers].

Staff escalate to [Dr. name] the same day if pain interference rises by [2] or more points from baseline, the patient reports a fall, new weakness or numbness, or a medication side effect; emergencies are directed to 911.

Monthly care management call by [name], [credential], under the general supervision of [Dr. name]: review goals and barriers, update the care plan, review medication adherence and report concerns to [Dr. name]. Staff do not change medications.

Five changes that make a note audit-ready

  • Make it patient-specific. Auditors look for this patient’s symptoms, thresholds and actions.
  • Fix the medication language. “Ensures the patient follows the medication plan” becomes “reviews medication adherence and reports concerns to the practitioner.” Only the practitioner changes medications.
  • Use plain credentials. Registered Dietitian Nutritionist (RDN), or the licence title. Marketing descriptions such as “integrative” or “functional” add nothing to medical necessity and invite questions.
  • Link each focus area to a goal. A lifestyle framework is fine as counseling structure, but sleep belongs in the plan only where there is a sleep goal.
  • Keep nutrition tied to the condition. Weight or gastrointestinal symptoms belong in a PCM plan only where they affect the pain condition; in CCM, list them under the condition they belong to.

Three rules hold the log together. The same minute never counts toward two codes. Time before consent and the initiating visit never counts, which means the draft care plan built before enrollment is unbilled work. And the minutes the log counts must equal the minutes on the claim, which is why the month-end close in the SOP page reconciles the two.

The call note

A call that runs longer than its care management content should be logged in segments, counting only the segment that is care management. A 60-minute call can be 35 counted minutes and 25 uncounted minutes of detailed meal planning, and the log says so. Describe the care management purpose of what was counted, not the menu: “reviewed adherence to the activity goal, barriers: night eating; updated plan” is a care management minute, a recipe discussion is not.

Pro tip

Auditors are not hunting for long notes. They are looking for notes that could only have been written about one patient. A short note with a named measure, a baseline, a date and the specific barrier discussed is stronger than a long note that would fit any patient with the same diagnosis.

Cloned notes: the pattern auditors flag

Copying the same wording across patients is the fastest way to turn a compliant program into an audit finding. The two places it happens are the provider enrollment note and the symptom management section. Build templates with brackets that force the writer to supply the patient’s own facts, train staff not to leave a bracket unfilled, and include a sample of notes in the monthly chart audit specifically to compare them against each other.

Frequently asked questions

Does Medicare require a provider signature on the care plan?

For CCM codes 99490 and 99439, Medicare does not require a practitioner signature on the care plan, and care team staff may develop and update it while the practitioner stays involved, according to contractor guidance. We still recommend that the practitioner reviews and approves it with a signature or an EHR attestation, because it is the best evidence of oversight. Complex CCM is different: the plan needs substantial revision by the practitioner’s team.

Can the care plan be a PDF attached to the chart?

Yes. CMS has said the format matters less than the process, provided the plan is captured electronically and shared in a timely way. Problems, medications and allergies still have to be in the EHR’s structured fields. Best practice is to attach the file and type a short summary so the care team can see the essentials without opening it.

How specific do goals have to be?

Specific enough to show progress month to month: a named measure, a baseline value, a target value and a date. A PEG score from 7.0 to 5.0 by a stated date works; “reduce pain” does not.

Can minutes from several staff members add up?

Yes. Clinical staff minutes from the practice and from an outside team working under the same billing practitioner add together toward the same staff code, as long as no minute is counted twice. Non-clinical staff minutes never count, and staff and practitioner codes of the same service cannot be mixed in one month.

What if a call runs long?

Log it in segments and bill only the counted segment. If the extra time is not care management, for example detailed menu writing, it stays in the note as uncounted time.

How long do we keep these records?

Follow your state’s medical record retention rule and your payer contracts, which are often longer than Medicare’s minimum. The time log and call notes are part of the patient record.

Confirm before you rely on this. Medicare sets no required template for these notes, and these are documentation examples, not legal advice. The billing practitioner is responsible for the content of every note. Verify current requirements with your Medicare Administrative Contractor and your payers before relying on this page.
