Split-night sleep study billing: when one night covers both diagnosis and titration.

A split-night study only earns its billing code if the chart proves two things happened in the right order and within the right windows: enough diagnostic recording to confirm moderate-to-severe obstructive sleep apnea, and enough time left in the night to actually titrate CPAP once the switch was made. Get either one wrong — or leave it undocumented — and a payer has grounds to deny the titration code outright. This guide covers the AASM's conversion criteria, why a successful split-night study is billed as a single code rather than two, when modifier 52 belongs on the claim, and what separates a night that qualifies from one that needs to be repeated.

Reviewed by Phares A., MD · Physiotherapist, CPC (AAPC) · Part of our sleep medicine billing and coding guide

Key takeaways

  • Two conditions gate the conversion, both required. Moderate-to-severe OSA observed during a minimum of 2 hours of diagnostic recording, and at least 3 hours remaining to complete CPAP titration (Kapur VK et al., AASM Clinical Practice Guideline for Diagnostic Testing for Adult OSA, J Clin Sleep Med. 2017;13(3):479–504).
  • A qualifying split-night study bills as 95811 alone — never 95810 plus 95811 on the same date of service. CMS guidance treats 95811 as inclusive of the diagnostic work performed earlier in the same session (CMS Article A57496, First Coast Service Options).
  • Missing the 3-hour titration window doesn't make the night billable as a partial split-night study. It makes it a diagnostic-only study (95810), full stop — with a separate titration night to schedule later.
  • Modifier 52 belongs on a truncated study, not a workaround for one that never met conversion criteria. It flags reduced recording time on the single code actually performed — it doesn't rescue an unsupported 95811 claim.

What a split-night study actually is

A split-night polysomnogram is one continuous overnight study, in one lab visit, that does two jobs instead of one. The first portion runs as a standard attended diagnostic PSG — full montage, no positive airway pressure. If the technologist confirms significant obstructive sleep apnea partway through the recording, the study switches protocols mid-night: the patient is fitted with a CPAP interface, and the remainder of the recording becomes a titration study, with pressure adjusted in real time to find the setting that controls the airway.

The alternative is two separate visits: a full diagnostic night, followed at a later date by a dedicated titration night once the diagnosis and severity are established. Split-night protocols exist because that second visit is expensive, delays treatment, and isn't always necessary — when severity is obvious early, there's no clinical reason to make the patient come back for a night that only adjusts a pressure setting. The AASM's 2017 clinical practice guideline reflects that trade-off directly: it suggests using a split-night protocol over a full-night diagnostic protocol when clinically appropriate, but explicitly as a weak recommendation — useful, not universally required, and conditioned on the patient not having factors likely to interfere with same-night diagnosis and titration (Kapur VK et al., J Clin Sleep Med. 2017;13(3):479–504).

That conditional framing is the reason billing for a split-night study is stricter than billing for either component alone. The code that gets submitted has to match a specific clinical event — a documented mid-night conversion that met defined criteria — not just "we started diagnostic and ended up titrating."

The AASM criteria that have to be met before the study can convert

The 2017 AASM clinical practice guideline states the conversion criteria as a footnote to its diagnostic-testing algorithm, and both conditions have to be true before a technologist switches protocols:

  • 1A moderate-to-severe degree of OSA is observed during a minimum of 2 hours of diagnostic PSG recording. The 2-hour clock runs on actual diagnostic recording time, not time in bed or total study duration to that point.
  • 2At least 3 hours of recording time remain available to complete CPAP titration. This is checked at the moment the conversion decision is made, working backward from the study's planned end time — not from how much time was originally scheduled for the whole night.

The guideline defines "clinically appropriate" as the absence of any condition the clinician identifies as likely to interfere with successful same-night diagnosis and titration — a severe insomnia complaint, an unusually long sleep-onset latency, or a comorbidity that makes a truncated diagnostic window unreliable are the kinds of things that argue for a full diagnostic night instead.

Standard adult OSA severity classification by AHI, the scale the "moderate-to-severe" conversion threshold is measured against.
SeverityAHI (events/hour)
Normal<5
Mild5–14.9

⚠️ The 2017 guideline's own text states the threshold as "a moderate to severe degree of OSA" rather than restating a specific AHI number at that footnote; the moderate/severe AHI bands above are the standard severity classification used throughout sleep medicine, not a number quoted verbatim from that footnote. Older material — including the AASM's 1997 practice parameters — is sometimes cited for a flat AHI ≥40 threshold in the first 2 hours, with a lower AHI of 20–40 left to clinical judgment. Treat AHI ≥40 as a legacy reference point, not the current controlling standard, and document the actual AHI observed and the clinician's rationale rather than relying on a single cutoff number.

Documentation that survives a payer's medical-necessity review states three things explicitly, with times attached: the clock time and elapsed diagnostic recording time at which moderate-to-severe OSA was confirmed, the AHI or RDI value at that point, and the amount of recording time remaining in the scheduled study when the decision to convert was made. A technologist's note that simply says "patient converted to titration" without those three data points is the single most common reason a split-night claim comes back requesting records — and the reviewer request that follows it.

How a valid split-night study is billed — and what happens when it isn't

Once the conversion criteria are met and documented, the entire night bills under a single code, not two.

Relevant polysomnography CPT codes and how each applies to a split-night scenario. Codes are paraphrased, not reproduced from the CPT Professional edition; confirm exact descriptors there before billing.
CodeDescribesUse for split-night?
95810Attended, in-lab polysomnography, no positive airway pressure, age 6 and olderDiagnostic-only night when conversion criteria are not met and documented
95811Attended, in-lab polysomnography with initiation of CPAP or bilevel PAP therapy, age 6 and olderYes — bills the entire night, diagnostic and titration portions together, when conversion criteria are met
95807Sleep study, attended, simultaneous recording of ventilation, heart rate, and oxygen saturation (not full PSG)Not applicable to a split-night PSG

CMS guidance is explicit that the diagnostic and titration codes aren't billed side by side for one continuous study: "CPT code 95811 alone should be billed for split night studies as CPT code 95811 in this instance is inclusive of CPT code 95810" (CMS Article A57496, First Coast Service Options, "Billing and Coding: Polysomnography and Sleep Testing"). The same article notes 95810 and 95811 are treated as mutually exclusive for the same date of service — submitting both is billing for two distinct procedures when the patient underwent one. Whichever code is the lower-valued of the pair is the one a payer's system denies when it catches the combination, so the practical effect of billing both is a guaranteed partial denial, not extra reimbursement.

⚠️ CMS Article A57496 is First Coast Service Options' jurisdiction (Florida, Puerto Rico, U.S. Virgin Islands). This billing logic — one code for a qualifying split-night study, no separate diagnostic-plus-titration submission — is standard across Medicare Administrative Contractors and commercial payers as an industry norm, but confirm the exact article or payer policy for your own MAC jurisdiction and any commercial contracts before relying on it in an appeal.

When conversion criteria aren't met, the night doesn't become a "partial" split-night claim — it reverts to whatever was actually completed. If only the diagnostic portion was done according to protocol, that's a 95810 diagnostic study, and a separate titration night gets scheduled and billed under 95811 on its own date of service once the diagnosis is confirmed.

Modifier 52: when reduced services applies to a split-night study

Modifier 52 tells a payer that the procedure represented by the code was performed, but at a reduced scope relative to what the code normally describes. For polysomnography, that most often means the total recording time fell meaningfully short of a complete study — commonly referenced against a 6-hour benchmark — or a titration segment ended before therapeutic control of the airway was demonstrated (CMS Article A57496).

  • 1Equipment failure or early termination mid-titration. The patient met conversion criteria, titration began, but the study had to stop early — a device malfunction, a medical event, or the patient pulling off the equipment before an effective pressure was found. Bill 95811 with modifier 52, and document the actual recording duration and why it ended.
  • 2A diagnostic-only night that ran well short of a full study. If the diagnostic portion itself is truncated — not converted to titration, just cut short — 95810 with modifier 52 reflects the reduced scope, again with the stop reason and elapsed time documented.
  • 3Not a substitute for meeting conversion criteria. Modifier 52 reduces payment for a service that was genuinely performed at a reduced scope; it isn't a way to bill 95811 for a night where the 2-hour/3-hour conversion criteria were never actually met. That's a coding-accuracy problem, not a reduced-services one, and modifier 52 doesn't fix it.

The chart note that supports modifier 52 needs the same specificity as the conversion note: exact start and stop times for recording, the reason the study ended when it did, what was and wasn't accomplished (was an effective pressure identified, or did the study end before one was found), and whether a repeat study is planned. A modifier appended without that narrative is a modifier a reviewer has no reason to accept.

Two patients, one criterion, two outcomes

The 2-hour/3-hour rule is easiest to apply with real numbers next to it. Both patients below start an 8-hour scheduled diagnostic study; what happens next depends entirely on when moderate-to-severe OSA is confirmed.

Patient A is the textbook case the guideline is written for: enough diagnostic time to confirm severity with room to spare, and a titration window comfortably above the 3-hour floor. That's a clean single-code 95811 claim, and the chart note only needs the standard elements — conversion time, AHI at conversion, remaining time — to support it.

Patient B illustrates the trap. The diagnosis isn't in doubt — five hours of recording is more than enough to confirm moderate-to-severe OSA. What fails is the second condition: only about an hour remains, well under the 3-hour floor needed to actually titrate and demonstrate an effective pressure. Even if the technologist attempts titration in that final hour because the patient is already hooked up and awake, that data doesn't convert the night into a billable split-night study. The correct claim is 95810 for the diagnostic work actually completed to standard, with a dedicated titration night billed separately once it's scheduled. Submitting 95811 here describes a titration study the criteria don't support — a claim a reviewer can deny on medical necessity even though genuine PAP data exists in the file.

Documentation that survives review
  • "Moderate-to-severe OSA (AHI 38) confirmed at 11:47 PM, 2h10m into diagnostic recording. 4h05m remained in the scheduled study. CPAP titration initiated per protocol." Every element of the conversion criteria is on the record with times attached.
Documentation that gets 95811 denied
  • "Patient converted to CPAP titration overnight." No AHI, no elapsed diagnostic time, no remaining-time calculation — nothing a reviewer can check the conversion criteria against, even if the conversion itself was clinically appropriate.

Frequently asked questions

What has to happen during the night for a study to legitimately qualify as split-night?

Two conditions, both required, per the AASM's 2017 clinical practice guideline for diagnostic testing of adult OSA: a moderate-to-severe degree of obstructive sleep apnea has to be observed during a minimum of 2 hours of diagnostic recording, and at least 3 hours of recording time have to remain available to complete CPAP titration once the switch is made. If either condition isn't met and documented, the night doesn't qualify as a split-night study regardless of what the sleep technologist decided to do in the lab.

Do we bill 95810 for the diagnostic portion and 95811 for the titration portion of a split-night study?

No. Only 95811 is billed. CMS guidance states that 95811 is inclusive of 95810 for a split-night study, because the titration portion supersedes the diagnostic portion performed earlier in the same session (CMS Article A57496, First Coast Service Options, "Billing and Coding: Polysomnography and Sleep Testing"). Billing both codes for the same night is billing for two procedures when the patient underwent one continuous study, and the lower-valued code is the one that gets denied when a payer's system catches the pair.

When does modifier 52 apply to a polysomnography or titration study?

Modifier 52 (reduced services) applies when the recorded study falls meaningfully short of a complete study — commonly cited as under 6 hours of total recording time, or a titration segment cut short before therapeutic control of the airway was demonstrated (CMS Article A57496). It's reported on the single code that was actually performed (95810 or 95811), not as a way to bill for a service that wasn't done. Documentation has to state the actual start and stop times, the reason recording ended early, and what was and wasn't accomplished before the study stopped.

If a patient doesn't meet split-night criteria until very late in the night, what gets billed?

If the AASM's 3-hour minimum remaining-time requirement can't be met — for example, moderate-to-severe OSA isn't confirmed until hour 5 of an 8-hour study, leaving only about 1 hour for titration — the night is billed as a diagnostic-only study (95810), not 95811. Any titration attempted in that remaining window is clinically reasonable but doesn't meet the documented conversion criteria, so it doesn't support billing the titration code; the patient typically needs a separate, dedicated titration night billed under 95811 on its own date of service.
