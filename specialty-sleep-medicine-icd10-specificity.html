Our complete sleep medicine guide

Sleep medicine ICD-10 coding: OSA severity, insomnia, and narcolepsy.

Sleep medicine has one of the flattest ICD-10 categories in outpatient billing and one of the most misunderstood: G47.33 is the entire obstructive sleep apnea code, with no mild/moderate/severe split, which surprises coders used to cardiology or oncology's deep specificity trees. What actually gates payment is the AHI value in the sleep study and whether the diagnosis on the claim matches what the payer's coverage policy requires for that specific service, a distinction this guide verifies code by code and policy by policy, against the live ICD-10 code set and the Medicare DME and sleep-study coverage articles that actually list the diagnoses.

Key takeaways

  • G47.33 is the only obstructive sleep apnea code that exists, verified against the current ICD-10-CM set, billable for HIPAA transactions, with no mild/moderate/severe variant. AHI-based severity is a clinical and coverage-determination fact, not a code choice.
  • A CPAP device has exactly one diagnosis that supports it. The DME MAC policy article for PAP devices names G47.33 as the sole covered diagnosis, and states outright that the code alone doesn't guarantee coverage, the AHI/RDI value from the sleep study still has to clear the LCD's threshold (LCD L33718, Article A52467, CGS Administrators / Noridian Healthcare Solutions).
  • Home sleep tests and in-lab studies don't share a covered-diagnosis list. Home sleep apnea test codes are tied to hypersomnia and sleep apnea diagnoses that exclude central sleep apnea; central apnea (G47.31) only clears coverage under the attended, in-lab codes (Article A57697, Noridian Healthcare Solutions).
  • The recurring denial pattern is symptom-only coding on a confirmation claim. R06.83 (snoring) or an unspecified sleep-disorder code supports the diagnostic workup; it does not support CPAP, oral appliance, or a repeat study once the sleep study has already produced a specific, billable diagnosis.

Obstructive sleep apnea: one code, and why severity isn't a coding question

Pull the full G47.3 sleep apnea family from the current ICD-10-CM set and the pattern is immediate: there is exactly one code for obstructive sleep apnea, and it doesn't branch by severity the way, say, heart failure or COPD do. G47.33, “Obstructive sleep apnea (adult) (pediatric),” confirmed billable for HIPAA-covered transactions against the current code set, is it. There is no G47.331 for mild, no G47.332 for moderate, no G47.333 for severe. A coder who goes hunting for a severity-specific OSA code the way they would for, say, chronic kidney disease staging is hunting for something that doesn't exist in this category.

So where does AHI severity actually live? In the coverage policy, not the code set. The Medicare DME MAC LCD governing PAP devices (L33718, Positive Airway Pressure Devices for the Treatment of Obstructive Sleep Apnea, CGS Administrators and Noridian Healthcare Solutions, effective 01/01/2024) sets two AHI/RDI pathways for initial CPAP coverage under HCPCS E0601: an AHI or RDI of 15 or more events per hour with a minimum of 30 events qualifies on its own, while an AHI or RDI of 5–14 events per hour with a minimum of 10 events qualifies only with documented excessive daytime sleepiness, impaired cognition, mood disorder, insomnia, hypertension, ischemic heart disease, or a history of stroke. Both pathways bill under the identical diagnosis code, G47.33, the AHI number and the symptom documentation are what the LCD is actually checking, and neither one changes what gets typed into the diagnosis field.

That's the practical takeaway for a coder or biller: don't go looking for a "severe OSA" code that will never exist, and don't let a physician's note that says "mild OSA, AHI 8, with hypertension" talk you into thinking a different code applies. The code is G47.33 either way. The AHI and the comorbidity documentation are what belong in the chart to support the DME claim that follows, they just don't change the ICD-10 code itself.

The rest of the G47 family: insomnia, hypersomnia, narcolepsy, and parasomnias

Sleep medicine bills across the entire G47 category, not just the apnea subsection, and each branch has its own specificity trap. All codes below were confirmed against the current ICD-10-CM set and are billable for HIPAA transactions.

Narcolepsy is the family most likely to trip up a coder used to defaulting to "unspecified" when a detail is missing, because there isn't an unspecified narcolepsy code to fall back on, cataplexy status (present or absent) is baked into the fourth character of the code itself. A note that documents "narcolepsy" without stating whether cataplexy is present leaves the coder guessing between G47.411 and G47.419, and guessing wrong on that pair matters clinically as well as financially, because cataplexy status also affects which medications a payer's formulary criteria expect to see tried first.

Two adjacent codes worth knowing even though they sit outside G47 entirely: R06.83 (Snoring) and E66.2 (Morbid (severe) obesity with alveolar hypoventilation), both confirmed billable. R06.83 is the correct symptom code for a pre-diagnostic visit where snoring is the chief complaint and no sleep study has occurred yet; E66.2 covers obesity hypoventilation syndrome specifically and is frequently confused with the G47.34/G47.36 sleep-related hypoventilation codes, which describe a different (non-obesity-driven) mechanism. Confirm the documented etiology before choosing among the three.

Pro tip

When a chart says "sleep apnea" without stating central or obstructive, don't default to G47.33. Check whether the sleep study report is in hand: if it typed the apnea, use that finding; if it hasn't happened yet, G47.30 (unspecified) is the honest pre-diagnostic code and the one that supports ordering the study, not the one that supports billing CPAP.

Diagnosis-to-medical-necessity: what actually justifies each sleep study and CPAP

Sleep medicine's coverage rules are unusually well documented for a Medicare DME benefit, because the sleep-study and PAP-device policies were rewritten together and cross-reference the same AHI logic. Two Medicare coverage sources carry the actual diagnosis-to-service linkage: the sleep-study billing and coding article (A57697, Billing and Coding: Polysomnography and Other Sleep Studies, Noridian Healthcare Solutions) and the PAP-device policy article (A52467, Positive Airway Pressure (PAP) Devices for the Treatment of Obstructive Sleep Apnea, Policy Article, CGS Administrators / Noridian Healthcare Solutions, the two DME MAC contractors that administer all four DME jurisdictions).

95800, 95801, 95806, G0398, G0399, G0400Unattended home sleep apnea test (HSAT), in or out of a sleep lab facilityNarrower group: hypersomnia (G47.10, G47.13, G47.14, G47.19) and sleep apnea limited to G47.30 and G47.33, notably excludes G47.31 central sleep apnea

The gap that matters most operationally is the one between the in-lab and home-test coverage groups: central sleep apnea (G47.31) is covered under the attended in-lab codes but is absent from the home sleep apnea test group. That isn't an oversight, a home device generally can't measure the respiratory effort signal needed to reliably distinguish a central event from an obstructive one, so a patient with a clinical picture suggesting central apnea (Cheyne-Stokes breathing pattern, heart failure history, opioid use) should be routed to a facility-based study, not a home test kit. Ordering an HSAT for that patient risks both a clinically inconclusive study and a coverage mismatch if the claim goes out under G47.31.

For the CPAP device itself, the linkage is narrower still. Article A52467 states directly that G47.33 is the only diagnosis code supporting PAP device coverage, and adds the caveat that "the presence of an ICD-10 code listed in this section is not sufficient by itself to assure coverage", the LCD's AHI/RDI thresholds, the pre-test in-person evaluation, and (between day 31 and day 91 of therapy) a documented re-evaluation showing symptom improvement and adherence-data review all have to be present in the record as well. A claim with the right diagnosis code and a missing 31-to-91-day re-evaluation note is still an audit risk, even though the code itself is correct.

Common denial: symptom-only coding on a claim that needs a confirmed diagnosis

Do
  • Update the diagnosis on the claim the moment the sleep study report is in hand, move from G47.30/R06.83 to the specific, confirmed code before the next service (CPAP setup, re-evaluation) goes out.
  • Check the covered-diagnosis group for the specific CPT/HCPCS code being billed, not just the general disease category, home sleep test coverage and in-lab coverage are genuinely different lists.
  • Keep the AHI/RDI value and comorbidity documentation in the chart even though they don't change the code, they're what the DME LCD actually reviews on audit.
Don't
  • Bill a CPAP claim (E0601) against G47.30 (unspecified) or R06.83 (snoring) because the confirmed diagnosis wasn't updated in the system after the sleep study resulted, this is the single most common reason a DME sleep-therapy claim denies for medical necessity.
  • Order a home sleep apnea test for a patient with a clinical picture suggesting central sleep apnea and then bill it against G47.31, that diagnosis doesn't sit in the HSAT coverage group, and the claim is fighting the policy from the start.
  • Assume "narcolepsy" alone is enough documentation to code from, without a stated cataplexy status, the coder is choosing between G47.411 and G47.419 without support for either.

The mechanism behind this denial is straightforward once the coverage articles are in front of you: the DME MAC's claims processing system checks the diagnosis code on the CPAP claim against the single-code list in Article A52467. A symptom code or an unspecified sleep-apnea code isn't on that list, so the claim denies for medical necessity (typically CO-50) regardless of how clearly the chart documents a confirmed OSA diagnosis with a qualifying AHI, because the qualifying information never made it from the sleep study report into the diagnosis field on the claim. The fix is a workflow one, not a coding-knowledge one: the diagnosis code has to be updated in the billing system the same day the sleep study is read, before the CPAP order goes out, not batched for later.

Frequently asked questions

Does ICD-10 have separate codes for mild, moderate, and severe obstructive sleep apnea?

No. There is one ICD-10-CM code for obstructive sleep apnea, G47.33, confirmed billable for HIPAA transactions against the current code set, and it carries no mild/moderate/severe split. Severity is documented clinically as the apnea-hypopnea index (AHI) from the sleep study, and that AHI value is what a Medicare DME LCD checks to decide whether a CPAP device is covered, it is a coverage-determination detail, not a coding one. Writing "moderate G47.33" in a note is clinically fine; there is no corresponding code to bill differently based on that severity.

What diagnosis code does Medicare require to cover a CPAP device?

G47.33, obstructive sleep apnea, is the only ICD-10 code that supports medical necessity for a PAP device under the DME MAC policy for Positive Airway Pressure Devices for the Treatment of Obstructive Sleep Apnea (LCD L33718 and its Policy Article A52467, CGS Administrators and Noridian Healthcare Solutions, DME MACs). The article states plainly that the presence of that code alone is not sufficient, the sleep study also has to meet the AHI/RDI thresholds in the LCD, and the ordering practitioner needs a documented in-person evaluation before testing.

Can a home sleep apnea test be billed to work up suspected central sleep apnea?

Not under the coverage pattern documented in the current Billing and Coding: Polysomnography and Other Sleep Studies article (A57697, Noridian Healthcare Solutions). Home sleep test codes 95800, 95801, 95806, G0398, G0399, and G0400 are listed against hypersomnia and sleep apnea codes including G47.30 and G47.33, but not G47.31 (primary central sleep apnea), an unattended home device isn't built to reliably distinguish central from obstructive events. The attended in-lab codes, 95810 and 95811, carry the broader coverage that includes G47.31, which is why a suspected central-apnea presentation routes to a facility-based study rather than a home test.

What's the coding difference between narcolepsy with and without cataplexy?

G47.411 reports narcolepsy with cataplexy and G47.419 reports narcolepsy without cataplexy, both confirmed billable for HIPAA transactions. The distinction isn't cosmetic for billing: the Multiple Sleep Latency Test, CPT 95805, and the in-lab polysomnography codes each carry their own covered-diagnosis groups under Medicare's sleep-study coverage article, and G47.411/G47.419 sit in the same coverage group as the hypersomnia codes that typically justify ordering the MSLT in the first place, coding the wrong member of the pair, or defaulting to unspecified narcolepsy documentation, is a common reason an MSLT claim gets questioned.
