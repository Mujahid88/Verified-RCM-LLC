Mental health billing and coding: the complete 2026 guide.

Behavioral health denies at roughly 15–25% by industry benchmarking, against 8–12% for general medicine, and most of the gap comes down to three things every competing guide talks around instead of naming: which MAC-specific coverage article actually governs the diagnosis on your claim, which NCCI bundling edit is firing on same-day psychotherapy and testing codes, and which CARC/RARC pairing tells you exactly what record element clears the denial. This guide names the actual document IDs, the actual bundling logic, and the actual appeal language — not a generic "check your documentation" gesture.

Reviewed by Phares A., MD · Physiotherapist, CPC (AAPC) · Updated August 2026

Key takeaways

  • MAC-specific coverage articles govern psychotherapy and testing medical necessity — First Coast's A57520 (Psychiatric Diagnostic Evaluation and Psychotherapy Services, eff. 01/01/2025) and WPS's L34646 (Psychological and Neuropsychological Testing, eff. 10/31/2024) are two of them, confirmed live against the CMS Coverage Database for this guide. "The LCD says" without naming the document is not a real citation.
  • GT is not a live Medicare Part B modifier. CMS retired it for professional claims in the CY2018 fee schedule rule; it survives only for Critical Access Hospital Method II institutional billing. Practices still appending GT to CMS-1500 claims are coding to an eight-year-old rule.
  • The add-on psychotherapy codes (90833/90836/90838) are a bundling decision, not a documentation nicety. Billing a standalone psychotherapy code plus a separate E/M code, instead of the E/M-plus-add-on pairing, is one of the highest-volume avoidable denials in the specialty.
  • Every CARC in our denial table is paired with the specific record element that resolves it — not a reworded restatement of the denial reason, which is what most competing guides publish instead.

Mental health billing at a glance

Four things drive most of behavioral health's denial volume: unspecified diagnosis codes that miss a payer's covered-diagnosis list, same-day psychotherapy/E/M and testing code pairs that trip NCCI edits, telehealth modifier and place-of-service mismatches, and missing or exceeded prior authorization on testing and higher levels of care. Here's the shape of it before the detail.

Three mistakes cost more than the rest combined, and this guide is built around fixing them:

  • 1Billing a standalone psychotherapy code alongside a separate same-day E/M instead of the correct E/M-plus-add-on pairing (90833/90836/90838) — a bundling error that either denies outright or under-bills the encounter, depending on which code hit the clearinghouse first.
  • 2Defaulting to modifier GT, or to modifier 95 across the board, on Medicare telehealth claims instead of Medicare's actual POS-driven logic (POS 02/10 plus modifier 93 for audio-only) — a modifier-era mismatch that's purely a rejection, not a clinical judgment call.
  • 3Coding an unspecified diagnosis — F41.9 instead of a specified anxiety disorder, F32.9 instead of an episode-and-severity-coded depressive disorder — that isn't on the governing coverage policy's covered-diagnosis list, when a same-chart, more specific code would have cleared it.

What changed in mental health billing for 2026

2026 brought four changes worth building into your workflow now rather than discovering through a denial batch.

2026 changes affecting behavioral health billing.
AreaWhat changed
CoCM billing for FQHCs/RHCsThe blended HCPCS code G0512, which FQHCs and RHCs previously used to bill the Collaborative Care Model as a single flat-rate code, was discontinued effective January 1, 2026. FQHCs and RHCs now report the same component codes everyone else uses — 99492, 99493, 99494, plus G2214 — instead of one bundled code. E/M plus psychotherapy on the same day: choosing and combining codes

Psychiatric prescribers and other clinicians who both manage medication and provide therapy face this decision constantly, and getting it wrong is one of the three highest-cost mistakes named above. The E/M code (99202–99215 for office visits) is leveled on either total time or medical decision making — not both together, satisfying either pathway independently justifies the level. When psychotherapy is also performed in that same encounter by the same clinician, the psychotherapy time is billed as an add-on code, never as a second standalone psychotherapy code:

E/M-plus-psychotherapy add-on codes and their standalone equivalents.
Add-on (bill with an E/M)Approximate psychotherapy timeStandalone equivalent (no E/M that day)
90833~30 minutes90832
90836~45 minutes90834
90838~60 minutes90837

Modifier 25 goes on the E/M code, not the psychotherapy add-on, and it certifies the E/M was a significant, separately identifiable service from the psychotherapy — meaning the note has to show two distinguishable clinical threads: the medical decision-making or time spent on the E/M work (medication review, side-effect assessment, dosage change, risk assessment), and the psychotherapy time spent on the therapeutic intervention itself. A chart that blends the two into one undifferentiated narrative doesn't support the modifier even if the total time billed is accurate, because a payer reviewing the note can't see where the E/M ends and the therapy starts.

A worked example: a 30-minute follow-up where the psychiatrist spends roughly 10 minutes on medication review and dosage adjustment (supporting a 99213 or 99214 depending on complexity) and 20 minutes on structured psychotherapy addressing the patient's anxiety symptoms bills as the E/M code with modifier 25, plus 90836 for the ~45-minute-band add-on only if the psychotherapy portion meets that time threshold on its own — if the psychotherapy portion is closer to 20 minutes, that's the 90833 band (~30 minutes), not 90836. Round up carelessly and the code no longer matches the documented time, which is exactly what an auditor checks first.

The same-provider-same-day rule matters here too: these add-on codes exist specifically for one clinician performing both services in one encounter. If a different clinician at the same practice performs the psychotherapy later the same day, that's typically billed as a standalone psychotherapy code under that second clinician's own NPI, not as an add-on to the first clinician's E/M. Full documentation templates, worked time-splitting examples, and the complete modifier 25 decision tree for this exact scenario are in our guide to billing E/M and psychotherapy same day: 90833, 90836, 90838 and modifier 25.

Psychotherapy codes: evaluation through crisis

If your practice bills a different MAC jurisdiction, the equivalent article will carry a different document ID and possibly different covered-diagnosis specifics — find your own MAC's version before treating this citation as universal.

The full time-band rules, the one-time-code-per-claim logic, and how time is rounded and documented for 90832, 90834, and 90837 specifically get their own dedicated guide, because the time-band boundaries are where a large share of level-mismatch denials come from.

Interactive complexity (90785) is worth stating precisely because it's one of the most inconsistently documented add-ons in the specialty. It requires one of four specific qualifying factors, and the note has to name which one applied — not just check a box:

  • 1Managing maladaptive communication among participants that complicates delivering the service — e.g., a caregiver who repeatedly interrupts or contradicts the patient's own reporting, requiring active management within the session.
  • 2Caregiver emotional or behavioral interference with the treatment plan, such as a parent's high distress level that has to be actively managed before therapeutic work with the child can proceed.
  • 3Mandated reporting of sentinel events (e.g., abuse or neglect disclosure) requiring the clinician to initiate the reporting process within the visit — document that the disclosure occurred and that reporting steps were initiated during the encounter, not just referenced afterward.
  • 4Use of play equipment, physical devices, interpreter, or translator to overcome a communication barrier with the patient — name the specific tool or interpreter used and why it was clinically necessary, not just present in the room.

90785 is reimbursed modestly, and its main function on audit is proving the base service genuinely required extra clinical complexity — using it routinely without one of these four factors documented is a pattern payers flag quickly.

Psychological and neuropsychological testing

Testing billing splits into two distinct activities that get confused constantly: the evaluation and interpretation work performed by a qualified health professional, and the test administration and scoring work, which can be performed by either the QHP or a supervised technician.

Psychological and neuropsychological testing code family.
CodeActivityWho bills it

96132 / +96133Neuropsychological testing evaluation services, first hour / each additional hourQHP only

WPS Insurance Corporation's L34646, "Psychological and Neuropsychological Testing," confirmed live against the CMS Coverage Database for this guide, carries an effective date of 10/31/2024 and covers this exact code family for practices in WPS's jurisdiction. Practices outside WPS's territory need their own MAC's equivalent LCD — testing coverage policy, like psychotherapy coverage policy, is written per contractor, not nationally.

Time-unit documentation is the recurring failure point: every unit billed for 96131, 96133, 96137, or 96139 needs a start and stop time (or clearly stated total minutes) tied to the specific test administered, not a single bulk time entry for the whole testing session. Prior authorization behavior varies sharply by plan type — PPO plans more often authorize a total number of hours across the evaluation, while HMO and managed Medicaid plans more often require separate authorization for the evaluation/interpretation codes and the administration/scoring codes, sometimes issued as two different authorization numbers on two different dates. Billing 96138/96139 technician time against an authorization that only names the QHP evaluation codes is a common, avoidable denial.

When a payer's own utilization review staff misapplies the code family — a frequent pattern is denying 96132/96133 as "duplicate" against 96130/96131 because a reviewer doesn't recognize psychological versus neuropsychological testing as clinically distinct services — the appeal should cite the specific clinical indication for neuropsychological (versus purely psychological) testing directly, and attach the referral or order documenting why the neurocognitive component was medically necessary. Full prior-authorization workflows by plan type and the complete testing code reference are in our psychological testing billing and prior authorization guide.

Behavioral health integration and the Collaborative Care Model

Two structurally different models get billed under overlapping-sounding codes, and mixing them up is a leading cause of denial in integrated primary care settings.

General BHI — 99484 / G0323
  • Care manager works under a treating physician's direction; monthly time threshold, no mandatory psychiatric consultant.
  • G0323 is the RHC/FQHC equivalent of 99484, billed under a clinical psychologist's or clinical social worker's direction — unaffected by the 2026 CoCM billing change below.
CoCM — 99492 / 99493 / 99494 / G2214
  • Structured three-person model: treating physician, behavioral health care manager, and a psychiatric consultant who conducts systematic caseload review — without necessarily seeing the patient directly.
  • 99492 is the initial month, ~70 minutes; 99493 is a subsequent month, ~60 minutes; +99494 is an add-on for each additional 30 minutes; G2214 covers a lower-intensity initial or subsequent month, first 30 minutes.

The single most common CoCM denial is missing documentation of the psychiatric consultant's systematic caseload review — not a missing patient encounter, since the consultant often never sees the patient at all, but a missing record that the consultant reviewed the caseload registry, discussed specific patients meeting escalation criteria with the care manager, and that this review is dated and attributable to the named consultant. A care manager's time log alone, without the consultant review documented separately, does not support a CoCM claim.

For FQHCs and RHCs specifically: the blended flat-rate code these sites previously used to bill CoCM as one bundled service, G0512, was discontinued effective January 1, 2026. These sites now bill the same component codes as everyone else — 99492, 99493, 99494, and G2214 — rather than a single bundled rate. This is a distinct code family from G0323, the general BHI code these same sites use, which was not affected by the 2026 change. Care manager time logs need to track billable time against the correct code family from day one under the new structure, because reverting to the old bundled logic after January 1, 2026 produces a code that no longer processes. Full monthly time-threshold tables, the care manager time-log template, and the consultant documentation checklist are in our Collaborative Care Model billing guide: 99492–99494, G0323 and G2214.

Telehealth and virtual care modifiers

This is where industry guidance is most consistently wrong, and it costs real money in rejected claims. Four things govern a behavioral health telehealth claim, and they are not interchangeable:

Telehealth modifier and place-of-service logic by payer type.
ElementApplies toStatus
Modifier 95Commercial payers, most Medicaid programsReports a synchronous audio-video telehealth service — the standard commercial telehealth modifier
Modifier 93MedicareReports a synchronous audio-only telehealth service — Medicare's specific audio-only modifier, distinct from 95
POS 02MedicareTelehealth provided when the patient is not at home — pays at the facility rate
POS 10MedicareTelehealth provided when the patient is at home — pays at the non-facility rate, typically higher
Modifier GTCritical Access Hospital Method II institutional claims onlyRetired for all other Medicare Part B professional claims in the CY2018 PFS final rule — not a live modifier for a standard CMS-1500 behavioral health claim

The mechanism Medicare actually uses to identify a telehealth claim is the place-of-service code (02 or 10), not a modifier — the modifier (93 for audio-only, or no modifier appended for standard audio-video since Medicare doesn't require 95 the way commercial payers do) rides alongside it to specify the modality. Commercial and Medicaid claims work differently: they rely primarily on modifier 95 rather than a telehealth-specific POS distinction the way Medicare's 02/10 split works, though POS 02 is still commonly reported to commercial payers as well. The confusion in most competing guidance comes from treating GT, 95, and 93 as roughly interchangeable "telehealth modifiers" — they are not, and using the wrong one for the payer type produces a straightforward technical rejection, not a coverage denial, meaning it never even reaches medical necessity review.

Behavioral health telehealth coverage itself, unlike the general Medicare telehealth flexibilities that get extended and expire on a legislative schedule, is permanent: the Consolidated Appropriations Act, 2021 permanently removed geographic and originating-site restrictions specifically for behavioral/mental health services, so a Medicare patient can be seen by telehealth from home anywhere in the country without expiration risk. Audio-only coverage for behavioral health is also broadly available where clinically appropriate, though the specific claim still needs modifier 93 and the correct POS code to process correctly. The complete modifier-by-payer matrix, common rejection codes from mismatches, and a claim-scrubber rule set are in our telehealth modifiers guide: 95, 93, GT and POS codes.

Medical necessity in behavioral health is decided by diagnosis specificity more than almost any other factor, because coverage policies publish the exact codes that support each service, and an unspecified code frequently fails that test where a specific one would pass. Modifiers beyond telehealth: 25, 59, and the X-modifiers

Modifier 25 does the heaviest lifting in behavioral health outside telehealth, covered in depth in the E/M-plus-psychotherapy section above — the mechanics repeated briefly here: it attaches to the E/M code, not the psychotherapy add-on, and it certifies the E/M was significant and separately identifiable from the psychotherapy performed the same day, which the note has to demonstrate structurally, not just assert.

59 and the X-modifiers (XE, XS, XP, XU) apply less often in behavioral health than in procedure-heavy specialties, but they show up in a few real patterns:

  • XESeparate encounter. A patient seen for individual therapy in the morning and returns for a genuinely separate crisis encounter later the same day — two distinct sessions, not one long one split for billing purposes.
  • XPSeparate practitioner. A psychiatric diagnostic evaluation by one clinician and standalone psychotherapy by a different clinician at the same practice on the same date, each under their own NPI.
  • XUUnusual non-overlapping service. Testing administration (96138/96139) performed by a technician on the same day as an unrelated, non-overlapping evaluation and management visit with the prescriber.

What behavioral health does not have much legitimate use for is XS (separate structure) — a modifier built around distinct anatomic sites, which rarely maps cleanly onto psychiatric services the way it does onto a procedure-based specialty. Seeing XS appended routinely on a behavioral health claim is itself a signal worth a second look, more often a sign of a scrubber rule copied from a different specialty template than a genuine clinical distinction.

Audit-trigger patterns to watch for: routine use of 59 (rather than a specific X-modifier) to force through a same-day 90791-plus-90792 pair, or 59 appended to clear a testing-code bundling edit without a genuinely separate session or evaluator behind it. Payers track append rates by provider and flag outliers regardless of whether any single claim was individually correct. Modifier GT's narrow institutional survival — Critical Access Hospital Method II claims only — is covered in the telehealth section above; it does not belong in this category of distinct-service modifiers at all, since it identifies a service location and billing method, not a bundling override.

NCCI edits and bundling traps in behavioral health

This is the section every competitor guide skips or gestures at with "watch your bundling rules" and nothing else. Two values decide whether a bundled or capped behavioral health code is billable at all: the NCCI Procedure-to-Procedure modifier indicator, and the Medically Unlikely Edit Adjudication Indicator (MAI). Neither is a guess — both are pair-specific and code-specific values CMS publishes and updates quarterly.

Where bundling shows up most in behavioral health:

  • 190791 and 90792 billed same day, same patient. Both report the diagnostic evaluation service; billing both for the same episode is the behavioral health equivalent of billing a service twice, and most payer edits treat it accordingly — whether as an outright NCCI pair or as a payer-specific frequency/duplicate edit rather than a formal PTP pair varies, so check both your NCCI file and the specific payer's own edit before assuming an X-modifier clears it.
  • 2The E/M-plus-add-on structure is itself a de facto bundling rule. Billing a standalone psychotherapy code (90832/90834/90837) and a separate E/M code the same day, by the same clinician, instead of the E/M-plus-add-on pairing (90833/90836/90838), is functionally the same error as ignoring a Column 1/Column 2 edit — it reports overlapping time and clinical work as two independent services when CPT structure requires them reported as one combined claim.
  • 3Testing code administration-versus-evaluation pairs. Billing 96138/96139 (technician administration) and 96130–96133 (QHP evaluation/interpretation) for what's actually the same block of testing time, rather than genuinely separate administration and interpretation time, is a common trigger for a testing-family Column 1/Column 2 review even where no absolute bundling edit exists.

⚠️ This build could not open CMS's primary NCCI Procedure-to-Procedure Edits file or the NCCI Policy Manual PDF directly to confirm the specific modifier indicator (0 versus 1) attached to any individual behavioral health code pair — CMS's site returned an access error to every automated retrieval attempt made while researching this page, consistent with the access pattern noted elsewhere on this site. Treat every pair named above as a bundling risk pattern, not a confirmed indicator value, and look up the specific pair in the CMS NCCI PTP Edits Lookup Tool before building it into a scrubber rule. The same caveat applies to MUE values and MAI designations for the testing and CoCM code families — both change quarterly, and the CMS PFS Look-Up Tool and NCCI/MUE files are the only sources that are always current. The full bundling reference, organized by code family, is in our NCCI edits and bundling traps in behavioral health billing guide.

Enclosed clinical documentation from the [date] evaluation establishes this diagnosis and the medical necessity of the service billed. We request reprocessing of this claim." Payer coverage nuances: Medicare, Medicaid, and commercial parity

Behavioral health's coverage landscape is more fragmented across payer types than most specialties, and treating Medicare rules as a stand-in for commercial or Medicaid rules is a reliable source of denials.

Medicare expanded its provider scope significantly with LMFT and LMHC enrollment effective January 1, 2024, discussed above, and CoCM/BHI billing continues to evolve with the 2026 FQHC/RHC change. State Medicaid programs vary widely in session-limit policy — some cap outpatient psychotherapy visits per calendar year absent a specific exception request, others don't cap standard outpatient therapy at all but tightly limit testing hours or IOP/PHP days; there is no single "Medicaid rule" for behavioral health, only fifty-plus state-specific ones, and a practice billing across state lines needs a state-by-state reference, not a national assumption.

MHPAEA (the Mental Health Parity and Addiction Equity Act) requires that a plan's behavioral health utilization management — prior auth requirements, session limits, medical necessity criteria — not be more restrictive than its comparable medical/surgical utilization management. In practice this matters most when a payer's utilization review staff pushes back on session counts or level-of-care requests using criteria that appear stricter than what the same plan applies to comparable medical conditions; a parity-based appeal argument, citing MHPAEA directly and requesting the plan's own comparative analysis, is a legitimate escalation path when a UR denial pattern looks disproportionate, and it's an argument most billing staff never reach for because they don't know it exists as a formal complaint mechanism with both the plan and, if unresolved, the Department of Labor or state insurance regulator.

Carve-out behavioral health plans — where a separate vendor administers behavioral health benefits apart from the patient's medical plan — are, by a wide margin, the single most common root cause behind the PR-204 and eligibility-mismatch denials cited in denial research across the industry. The medical plan's ID card looks like a normal insurance card, the front-desk eligibility check runs against the medical payer, and the claim gets submitted to the wrong entity entirely. A payer-specific eligibility check that explicitly confirms behavioral health benefit administration — not just active medical coverage — before the first appointment is the actual fix, and it has to happen at intake, not at claim submission.

Prior authorization: testing, IOP/PHP, and higher levels of care

Prior authorization touches psychological/neuropsychological testing, Intensive Outpatient Programs (IOP), Partial Hospitalization Programs (PHP), and most inpatient behavioral health admissions. PPO plans, discussed in the testing section above, more often authorize testing as a single block of hours across the full evaluation; HMO and managed Medicaid plans more often split authorization by code family and sometimes by specific date, which means a single evaluation can require two or three separate authorization numbers tracked against different code groups.

IOP and PHP levels of care almost universally require concurrent review, not just an initial authorization — the payer authorizes an initial block of days or sessions, and continued treatment past that block requires a new clinical review submission showing continued medical necessity, typically including updated symptom severity, response to treatment so far, and a stated reason a lower level of care isn't yet appropriate. Missing a concurrent review deadline, even by a day, commonly results in a coverage gap for the days between the missed deadline and the next approved authorization, which is difficult or impossible to recover after the fact — concurrent review dates need to be tracked as aggressively as any other hard deadline in the revenue cycle.

When a payer denies continued IOP/PHP days or exceeds a stated session limit, documentation needs to establish both clinical necessity for continuing at that level of care and, where relevant, why a lower level of care would not be safe or effective yet — a chart that only restates ongoing symptoms without addressing the step-down question specifically tends to fare worse on review. Peer-to-peer review, when offered, should be scheduled with the actual treating clinician or a clinician with direct chart familiarity, not delegated to administrative staff reading from a summary; payer medical directors conducting peer-to-peer calls are trained to probe clinical specifics that a non-clinical staff member can't credibly answer, and a peer-to-peer that goes poorly because the wrong person took the call is harder to walk back than one that was never scheduled.

Conclusion

The highest-value fixes in this guide are the ones that prevent a denial rather than appeal one: coding to the specific diagnosis a coverage policy actually lists instead of an unspecified default, using the E/M-plus-add-on pairing instead of a standalone-plus-separate-E/M combination, and getting the Medicare telehealth POS-and-modifier logic right instead of defaulting to whatever a template from a different specialty happens to use. Where a denial does land, sorting it correctly between a genuine coverage dispute and a correctable claim error, and citing the specific MAC article or CARC-paired record element rather than a generic reason, is what actually moves the appeal.

Frequently asked questions

Can we bill 90791 and 90792 on the same day, or by two different clinicians on the same patient?

Not for the same episode of care by the same practice. Both codes report the same service — a psychiatric diagnostic evaluation — with 90792 adding medical services such as a prescriber's assessment. Most payers, and Medicare's own coverage articles including First Coast's A57520, treat the diagnostic evaluation as a once-per-episode service, so billing 90791 by a therapist and 90792 by a psychiatrist in the same intake window routinely denies the second claim as a duplicate. If both a therapist's psychosocial evaluation and a prescriber's medical evaluation are genuinely needed, document why they are clinically distinct and expect the payer to still question it.

When do we use 90833, 90836, or 90838 instead of billing 90832, 90834, or 90837 by themselves?

Use the add-on codes only when the same clinician performs a separately identifiable E/M service and psychotherapy in the same encounter. 90833, 90836, and 90838 attach to an E/M code (99202-99215) and report only the psychotherapy time on top of it — they are never billed alone. If no E/M was performed that day, bill the standalone code (90832, 90834, or 90837) instead. Billing a standalone psychotherapy code plus a separate E/M code the same day, instead of the correct add-on pairing, is one of the most common bundling errors in behavioral health billing.

Is modifier GT still valid for mental health telehealth billing?

Not for Part B professional claims. CMS eliminated the GT requirement for professional claims in the CY2018 Physician Fee Schedule final rule and replaced it with place-of-service code 02. GT survives only for distant-site telehealth billed under Critical Access Hospital Method II institutional claims, which is a narrow, facility-specific billing path most behavioral health practices never touch. If your scrubber or clearinghouse is still appending GT to a CMS-1500 professional claim, that claim is coded to a rule that has been gone since 2018.

Why does a psychological testing claim deny even though we had prior authorization on file?

Usually because the units or hours actually billed exceeded what the authorization approved, or because the codes billed don't match the code family the authorization covers — an authorization for evaluation and interpretation (96130/96132) doesn't automatically cover technician-administered testing time (96138/96139) if the payer issued separate authorization numbers for each. The other common cause is the rendering provider on the claim not matching the credentialed provider named on the authorization, which happens often when a supervised technician performs the testing but the claim doesn't reflect the supervising qualified health professional correctly.

What's the actual difference between the Collaborative Care Model and general behavioral health integration?

CoCM (99492, 99493, 99494, plus G2214 for a lower-intensity initial month) requires a structured three-person model: a treating physician, a behavioral health care manager, and a psychiatric consultant who reviews the caseload on a regular basis, even without seeing the patient directly. General BHI (99484, or G0323 for rural health clinics and FQHCs) is a lighter-touch model — a care manager working under a treating physician's direction, without the mandatory psychiatric consultant review. CoCM pays more because it requires more infrastructure and more documented psychiatric oversight; billing CoCM codes without a documented consultant caseload review is the single most common reason these claims deny.

Why does an unspecified diagnosis code like F41.9 trigger a denial when a code like F32.9 sometimes doesn't?

It depends entirely on what the specific payer's coverage policy lists as a supporting diagnosis for the service billed, not on some general rule about unspecified codes being invalid — F41.9 and F32.9 are both valid, billable ICD-10-CM codes. The problem is medical necessity: Medicare LCDs and commercial coverage policies for services like psychological testing or ongoing psychotherapy often list specific, coded diagnoses as covered indications, and an unspecified or symptom-level code frequently isn't on that list where a specific one would be. Check the diagnosis list in the governing coverage policy before defaulting to an unspecified code out of habit.

How do we appeal a CO-197 denial for missing prior authorization?

First confirm whether the payer allows retroactive or expedited review — many behavioral health carve-out plans do, especially when the service was urgent. If retroactive review is available, submit it with clinical documentation establishing medical necessity at the time of service, not after the fact. If the payer has no retroactive path, the claim is very likely uncollectable from the payer and becomes either a self-pay conversation with the patient (with proper notice) or a write-off — chasing a hard no-retro-auth denial past the first appeal rarely recovers the claim and the staff time is better spent preventing the next one with an eligibility and authorization check before the appointment, not after.

Do LMFTs and LMHCs bill Medicare the same way as psychologists and clinical social workers?

The billing mechanics are largely the same — CMS-1500 professional claims, the same psychotherapy CPT codes, NPI-based enrollment through PECOS — but marriage and family therapists and mental health counselors are a newer Medicare provider type, eligible to enroll and bill Part B only as of January 1, 2024. Confirm the specific clinician's enrollment and effective date before submitting claims under their NPI, and check whether your state's scope-of-practice rules and the specific payer's credentialing panel recognize the license type, because commercial payer recognition of these license types still varies more than it does for psychologists and LCSWs.
