Our complete sleep medicine guide

Polysomnography vs. home sleep apnea test billing: G-codes vs. CPT.

Sleep medicine's costliest coding mistake isn't a modifier or a bundling edit — it's submitting the wrong code family for where the test happened and who's paying for it. Medicare requires HCPCS G0398, G0399, or G0400 for a home sleep apnea test and rejects the equivalent CPT codes outright. CPT 95810 in-lab polysomnography is barred from anywhere but a facility-based sleep lab. And neither code set can paper over what a home test is structurally unable to do: diagnose narcolepsy, REM sleep behavior disorder, or periodic limb movement disorder. This guide walks through both code families, the payer-by-payer decision rule, and the clinical limitation that turns a coding question into a medical-necessity one.

Reviewed by Phares A., MD · Physiotherapist, CPC (AAPC) · Part of our sleep medicine billing and coding guide

Key takeaways

  • CPT 95810 is an in-lab-only code. Medicare's coverage articles require that polysomnography be performed in a facility-based sleep laboratory, not the home or a mobile unit — a site-of-service mismatch, not something a modifier fixes.
  • Medicare requires the HCPCS G-codes for home testing, full stop. Submitting CPT 95800, 95801, or 95806 to Medicare instead of G0398, G0399, or G0400 triggers an automatic denial — a code-set edit, not a coverage review.
  • Non-Medicare payers split. Most accept the CPT home-testing codes, but a real minority — commercial plans and some Medicaid programs — crosswalk to the G-codes instead. Confirm per payer before the claim goes out.
  • A home sleep test can't diagnose narcolepsy, REM sleep behavior disorder, or PLMD. Those monitors don't record EEG or stage sleep — ordering (or coding) one against those diagnoses is a medical-necessity mismatch before it's a billing problem.

Where the test happens decides which code family applies

Every sleep study code in Medicare's coverage policy sorts into one of two buckets before anything else about the patient matters: attended, facility-based polysomnography, or unattended, portable home testing. That split isn't cosmetic — it's the first fork in a decision tree that determines which code set is even eligible, before a payer's own preference for CPT versus HCPCS enters the picture at all.

The first fork: setting determines the eligible code pool, not the payer.
Where the test happenedEligible codesGoverned by
Facility-based sleep lab, technologist-attended95808, 95810, 95811 (CPT)Facility-only requirement — never billable for a home or mobile study
Home, unattended, patient self-applies the deviceG0398 / G0399 / G0400 (HCPCS) or 95800 / 95801 / 95806 (CPT), depending on payerPayer-specific mandate — this is the fork that causes most denials

The facility-versus-home fork is nearly universal across payers and rarely disputed. The second fork — which code set a given payer wants for the home test — is where claims actually go wrong, because it isn't consistent across the industry and can't be assumed from one payer's rule to the next. The rest of this guide works through both forks, then the clinical fork underneath them both: whether a home test was ever the right test to order.

CPT 95810: attended polysomnography, and why it can't leave the lab

CPT 95810 describes polysomnography of at least six hours, with continuous, simultaneous recording of sleep staging (EEG, EOG, submental EMG) plus four or more additional physiologic parameters — typically airflow, respiratory effort, oxygen saturation, ECG or heart rate, and limb movement. Its companion code, 95808, covers the same attended structure with sleep staging plus one to three additional parameters; 95811 adds CPAP or bilevel titration to the same in-lab framework, including split-night studies.

What all three share, and what makes this section short: Medicare's local coverage articles state the requirement in unambiguous terms. Per Noridian's coverage article on polysomnography and other sleep studies (Article A57697, effective 09/11/2025), documentation must confirm that polysomnography billed under 95808, 95810, or 95811 "was performed in a facility based sleep study laboratory and not in the home or a mobile facility." Every Medicare Administrative Contractor's version of this article carries the same restriction — it isn't jurisdiction-specific variation, it's a national norm implemented locally.

The reason isn't arbitrary. Attended in-lab PSG requires a technologist physically present through the night to correct a dislodged electrode, distinguish a genuine arousal from a sensor artifact, and — when the study is a split-night protocol — make a real-time clinical call to initiate CPAP titration once obstructive sleep apnea is confirmed. None of that is possible on hardware the patient applies alone in a bedroom. A claim for 95810 or 95811 with a place-of-service code indicating the patient's home, or with a chart note describing a portable monitor rather than a lab visit, is a documentation red flag independent of whether the sleep disorder itself was real and severe — the claim fails on where the test happened, not on medical necessity.

Home sleep apnea testing for Medicare: the mandatory G-codes

Home sleep apnea testing (HSAT) is where Medicare made a deliberate coding choice that trips up practices new to sleep billing: rather than accepting the AMA's CPT valuation for unattended home testing, CMS created its own HCPCS Level II codes and requires them exclusively for Medicare beneficiaries. The three codes map to the American Academy of Sleep Medicine's portable-monitor type classification, and the distinction between them is channel count and content — not device brand, not price point, not what the DME supplier calls it in a catalog.

HCPCS G-codes for Medicare home sleep apnea testing, by AASM portable monitor type.
CodeMonitor typeMinimum channel requirement
G0398Type II7 channels: EEG, EOG, EMG, ECG/heart rate, airflow, respiratory effort, and oxygen saturation
G0399Type III4 channels: 2 respiratory movement/airflow channels, 1 ECG/heart rate channel, and 1 oxygen saturation channel — the code billed for the large majority of Medicare home sleep tests
G0400Type IVMinimum 3 channels — the simplest, most commonly deployed device category, typically oximetry plus one or two additional signals

Medicare's coverage articles are explicit that these three codes — not their CPT counterparts — are the only acceptable billing path for a home test furnished to a Medicare beneficiary, performed with a Medicare-approved device and ordered for a patient with a documented comprehensive sleep evaluation and a high pretest probability of moderate to severe obstructive sleep apnea. Submitting CPT 95800, 95801, or 95806 to a Medicare Administrative Contractor for that same service will not route to a medical-necessity reviewer at all — it fails at the code-edit level, the same mechanical rejection a deleted or invalid code would trigger, because those CPT codes simply aren't in Medicare's accepted code set for this service. The correction is a clean resubmission with the matching G-code, not an appeal built around clinical justification the claim never needed to establish. Match the specific G-code to the channels the device actually recorded on the night of the study, confirmed against its own specification sheet rather than assumed from its marketed monitor class — a device that drops a channel mid-study effectively produced a lower-type record for that encounter, and coding to the nominal rating instead of the actual output is a gap an audit finds even though the claim paid.

Non-Medicare payers: CPT 95800, 95801, 95806 — and why you can't assume

Outside traditional Medicare, most commercial payers stayed with the AMA's CPT structure for home sleep testing rather than adopting CMS's HCPCS codes. The three CPT home-testing codes differ from each other in exactly the parameters they capture, not in setting — all three are unattended and all three can be billed for a study performed at home.

CPT home sleep testing codes. Descriptions paraphrased — confirm exact language against your current CPT Professional edition before building a charge master entry.
CodeWhat it capturesDistinguishing feature
95800Unattended recording of heart rate, oxygen saturation, respiratory analysis (e.g., airflow or peripheral arterial tone), and sleep timeThe only one of the three that includes a sleep-time component
95801Unattended recording of a minimum of heart rate, oxygen saturation, and respiratory analysis — no sleep-time requirementThe narrowest parameter set of the three; commonly used for peripheral arterial tone-based devices
95806Unattended recording of heart rate, oxygen saturation, respiratory airflow, and respiratory effort (e.g., thoracoabdominal movement)Matches a Type III portable monitor's typical output; the most frequently billed of the three commercially

None of the three CPT home-testing codes involves EEG-based sleep staging — that distinction, not the setting, is what actually separates every home-testing code (CPT or HCPCS) from true polysomnography. The distinction among 95800, 95801, and 95806 themselves is purely which parameters the device recorded: pick the code that matches what the device actually captured that night, not the diagnosis being ruled out.

The assumption that causes denials here isn't about which codes exist — it's assuming CPT applies just because the payer isn't Medicare. Some commercial payers and a number of state Medicaid programs crosswalk their own home-testing policy to Medicare's methodology and require the same G-codes rather than the CPT set, and Medicare Advantage plans split further still: some carry over traditional Medicare's G-code mandate as a utilization-management overlay, others accept the CPT equivalents. There is no default that holds across the industry. The only reliable workflow is checking the specific payer's current medical policy bulletin before the first claim for that payer goes out — not inferring it from what a different payer accepted last quarter, and not assuming a Medicare Advantage plan automatically follows fee-for-service Medicare's rule just because CMS regulates the plan.

⚠️ One more date worth building into a 2026-into-2027 workflow: the AMA CPT Editorial Panel approved deleting 95800, 95801, and 95806 effective January 1, 2027, replacing them with a new complexity-tiered family of six codes. All three remain fully billable for any 2026 date of service, and this build did not attempt to re-verify final code numbers or the crosswalk decision every payer will make for 2027 — that transition, along with modifier 52's 6-hour threshold on these codes and the CPT sleep-study family in full, is covered in depth in our sleep study and HSAT coding guide. Revisit payer-specific code selection again once the new family takes effect.

Do
  • Check the specific payer's current medical policy for home sleep testing before the first claim, every time a new payer enters the mix.
  • Match the G-code or CPT code to the channels the device actually recorded, not its nominal monitor-type rating.
  • Treat a facility-based study and a home study as two different code pools before ever asking which specific code within a pool applies.
Don't
  • Don't submit CPT 95800, 95801, or 95806 to Medicare under any circumstance — the G-code substitution is mandatory, not a preference.
  • Don't assume a Medicare Advantage or commercial plan follows Medicare's G-code rule just because it's regulated by CMS or isn't Medicare fee-for-service.
  • Don't bill 95808, 95810, or 95811 for a study that took place anywhere but a facility-based sleep lab.

Payer decision table: confirm before you submit

Reduced to a single reference, this is the check to run before a home sleep test claim leaves the building — and the reason it has to be a per-payer check, not a rule memorized once and applied everywhere.

The denial pattern to watch for either direction is the same shape: a code-set mismatch shows up as an automatic rejection with no reference to medical necessity, coverage criteria, or documentation quality at all — because the system never got far enough to evaluate any of that. If a home-testing claim denies immediately with no request for records and no reviewer notes, check the code set against the specific payer's policy before assuming the denial is about the test itself.

Pro tip

Build the payer's code-set requirement into the front-end eligibility and order-entry workflow, not into a coder's memory. A field on the sleep study order that captures "payer requires: CPT / HCPCS G-code / unconfirmed — verify" catches the mismatch before the study is even scheduled, which is cheaper than catching it after a denial and a resubmission cycle.

The clinical limitation that turns a coding choice into a medical-necessity problem

Everything above assumes the home test was clinically appropriate in the first place. It frequently isn't, and the reason is structural, not a documentation gap that better paperwork can fix.

The overwhelming majority of home sleep tests use Type III or Type IV portable monitors — billed as G0399 or G0400, or as CPT 95801 or 95806 — recording three to four channels of cardiorespiratory data: airflow or respiratory effort, oxygen saturation, and heart rate. None of that is EEG, EOG, or chin EMG, and without those three signals a study cannot stage sleep — it cannot distinguish REM from non-REM, cannot detect an arousal from the EEG record, and cannot establish sleep onset or sleep architecture at all. A Type II monitor (G0398) nominally adds an EEG channel to its seven-channel minimum, but it remains unattended, is rarely deployed in routine practice, and its automated, unattended staging isn't treated as equivalent to technologist-scored, attended in-lab polysomnography for diagnosing a central nervous system sleep disorder.

That gap matters because narcolepsy, REM sleep behavior disorder, and periodic limb movement disorder are diagnosed from exactly the data a home test doesn't collect. Narcolepsy requires overnight PSG followed by a next-day multiple sleep latency test (MSLT, CPT 95805) to document abnormally short sleep latency and early-onset REM periods — both readings that depend on EEG-based staging. REM sleep behavior disorder is defined by the loss of normal muscle atonia during REM sleep, visible only on EMG channels correlated against EEG-confirmed REM periods, ideally with synchronized video to document the enactment behavior itself. Periodic limb movement disorder is scored from anterior tibialis EMG channels correlated against the same staged sleep record, distinguishing pathological periodic movements from the incidental leg twitches every home cardiorespiratory-only device is blind to. An attended technologist also does work an algorithm can't: correcting a dislodged EEG lead mid-study, distinguishing a genuine cortical arousal from a movement artifact, and applying AASM scoring rules that assume real-time observation. None of that is available from a device the patient applied alone in a bedroom.

Medicare's own coverage policy reflects this split by diagnosis, not just by setting. Per Noridian's coverage determination (LCD L36861) and its companion billing article (A57697), multiple sleep latency testing (95805) is the code tied to hypersomnia and narcolepsy workups; general polysomnography codes (95782, 95807, 95808, and 95810) carry the broader coverage that includes periodic limb movement disorder, parasomnias, and REM sleep behavior disorder; and the home-testing codes — CPT 95800/95801/95806 and HCPCS G0398–G0400 alike — are approved specifically for hypersomnia and obstructive sleep apnea, not for the central-nervous-system-based disorders above. A home test ordered against a working diagnosis of narcolepsy or REM sleep behavior disorder isn't just clinically inadequate — it's coding a service against a diagnosis the payer's own policy doesn't recognize that code as answering, which is a medical-necessity mismatch that exists on the order sheet before the claim is ever built.

The practical takeaway for a practice's intake and ordering workflow: when the differential includes narcolepsy, REM sleep behavior disorder, restless legs with suspected PLMD, sleepwalking, or another parasomnia in addition to suspected obstructive sleep apnea, a home test is the wrong first test regardless of how strongly the patient prefers to avoid an overnight lab visit or how much faster scheduling a home device is. Route that patient to attended, facility-based polysomnography from the start, and document the differential driving that choice — it protects the claim and, more importantly, it's the only path that can actually answer the clinical question being asked.

Frequently asked questions

Why does Medicare deny CPT 95800, 95801, and 95806 for a home sleep apnea test?

Because Medicare doesn't recognize those CPT codes for home testing at all — it requires HCPCS Level II codes G0398, G0399, or G0400 instead, matched to the portable monitor's channel count. This isn't a medical-necessity judgment the claim fails on review; it's a code-set mismatch that the claims-processing system rejects automatically, the same way billing a deleted code would. The fix is resubmission with the correct G-code, not an appeal arguing the test was needed.

What's the actual difference between G0398, G0399, and G0400?

Channel count and content, tied to the AASM's portable monitor type classification. G0398 covers a Type II monitor recording a minimum of 7 channels, including EEG, EOG, EMG, ECG or heart rate, airflow, respiratory effort, and oxygen saturation. G0399 covers a Type III monitor recording a minimum of 4 channels: two respiratory movement or airflow channels, one ECG or heart rate channel, and one oxygen saturation channel — this is the code billed for the large majority of home sleep apnea tests. G0400 covers a Type IV monitor recording a minimum of 3 channels, typically oximetry plus one or two additional signals. Code to what the device actually recorded that night, not its marketed capability.

Can a home sleep apnea test diagnose narcolepsy or REM sleep behavior disorder?

No. The Type III and Type IV monitors billed as G0399, G0400, or the CPT home-testing codes don't record EEG, EOG, or chin EMG, so they can't stage sleep — and sleep staging is what a narcolepsy, REM sleep behavior disorder, or periodic limb movement disorder workup runs on. Those diagnoses route to attended, facility-based polysomnography (95808 or 95810), and a suspected narcolepsy workup adds next-day multiple sleep latency testing (95805). Ordering or billing a home-test code against one of these diagnoses is a medical-necessity mismatch before it's ever a coding question.

Do all non-Medicare payers accept the CPT codes for home sleep testing?

No, and that's the assumption that causes denials. Most commercial payers accept CPT 95800, 95801, and 95806 for home sleep apnea testing, but a real minority of commercial plans and some state Medicaid programs crosswalk their own policy to Medicare's methodology and require the G-codes instead. Medicare Advantage plans split further still — some mirror traditional Medicare's G-code mandate, others accept the CPT equivalents. Confirm the specific payer's current medical policy before the first claim goes out; claims history from a different payer doesn't transfer.
