Breast reconstruction billing: WHCRA-mandated staged coding.

Breast reconstruction is plastic surgery's single biggest billing opportunity, and also its easiest place to lose money to a wrongly coded stage or a denial that never should have been sent. A 1998 federal law removes almost all of the coverage ambiguity that trips up other reconstructive procedures — but only if the claim reflects it. This guide covers the Women's Health and Cancer Rights Act mandate itself, the CPT pathway for implant-based and flap-based reconstruction, how to bill surgery on the healthy breast without it reading as cosmetic, and the denial language WHCRA is built to overturn.

Reviewed by Phares A., MD · Physiotherapist, CPC (AAPC) · Part of our plastic surgery billing and coding guide

Key takeaways

  • WHCRA is triggered by mastectomy coverage, not a cancer diagnosis, and carries no time limit. Any plan that covers mastectomies must cover all stages of reconstruction, symmetry surgery on the other breast, prostheses, and complications including lymphedema — requested the same year or a decade later.
  • Implant-based reconstruction runs on two different pathways that use different codes — a direct permanent implant (19340/19342) versus a staged tissue expander (19357) exchanged later for the permanent implant (11970). Coding the wrong pathway is the most common accuracy error in this category.
  • Surgery on the healthy breast to match the reconstructed one is a WHCRA-mandated benefit, not an elective procedure — a mastopexy, reduction, or augmentation billed for symmetry needs a diagnosis code that documents the disproportion, not a cosmetic-surgery framing.
  • A denial calling a later stage cosmetic or citing a reconstruction benefit maximum should be met with the statute, not a medical-necessity appeal. WHCRA's "all stages, no time limit" language is a coverage-scope argument that resolves faster than re-litigating clinical necessity.

The federal law that decides this before the payer does

The Women's Health and Cancer Rights Act of 1998 (WHCRA), codified at 29 U.S.C. § 1185b under ERISA and 42 U.S.C. § 300gg-6 under the Public Health Service Act, is the single most useful fact in a breast reconstruction claim — more useful than any CPT code, because it pre-empts most of the coverage arguments a payer would otherwise raise. The trigger is simple: if a group health plan or individual health insurance policy covers mastectomies at all, it must also cover reconstruction connected to that mastectomy. The statute does not require a cancer diagnosis to apply — it applies to anyone who receives a covered mastectomy under a plan that covers mastectomies, for whatever underlying reason.

WHCRA's four coverage mandates (29 U.S.C. § 1185b; DOL/EBSA and CMS/CCIIO fact sheets).
MandateWhat it requires
1. Reconstruction of the mastectomy sideAll stages of reconstruction of the breast on which the mastectomy was performed — not just the first surgery.
2. Contralateral symmetrySurgery and reconstruction of the other breast to produce a symmetrical appearance, even though that breast was never diseased.
3. ProsthesesExternal breast prostheses and the fittings associated with them, for patients who do not pursue surgical reconstruction.
4. Treatment of complicationsTreatment of physical complications at all stages of the mastectomy, explicitly including lymphedema.

Two structural details matter as much as the four mandates themselves. First, the statute requires that deductibles and coinsurance for reconstruction be consistent with those applied to other benefits under the same plan — a plan cannot carve out a higher cost-sharing tier specifically for reconstruction. Second, WHCRA sets no time limit: a patient can request reconstruction, a revision, or a delayed symmetry procedure years after the mastectomy, and the plan cannot deny it solely because time has passed. Both points come from the DOL/EBSA and CMS/CCIIO WHCRA fact sheets and are worth having on hand verbatim when a denial letter cites either one.

⚠️ One coverage-scope exception is worth flagging: non-federal governmental self-funded plans (typically certain state and local government employee plans) may elect to opt out of WHCRA under a separate CMS opt-out procedure. It is uncommon, but it means "the plan covers mastectomies" does not universally guarantee WHCRA applies before you've confirmed the plan type — verify against the specific plan's opt-out status when a self-funded governmental plan denies a WHCRA-mandated benefit outright.

Implant-based reconstruction: two pathways, two code sets

The most common billing error in implant-based reconstruction isn't a wrong code in isolation — it's coding the wrong pathway. A permanent breast implant can reach the patient two different ways, and each has its own CPT logic. Confirming which one actually happened, from the operative note rather than the visit type, is the first step before any code gets selected.

Implant-based reconstruction CPT pathway. Descriptors paraphrased; confirm exact language in the current CPT Professional edition before billing.
CPT codeWhat it reportsWhen to use it
19340Immediate insertion of a permanent breast prosthesis (implant), no tissue expander involvedDirect implant placed in the same operative session as the mastectomy
19342Delayed insertion of a permanent breast prosthesis, no tissue expander involvedDirect implant placed on a date separate from the mastectomy, when no expander was used first
19357Breast reconstruction with tissue expander, immediate or delayed, including subsequent expansion(s)Stage one of two-stage reconstruction — a single global code covers the initial placement and every later saline-fill visit
11970Replacement of tissue expander with permanent prosthesisStage two — the expander-to-implant exchange, reported once the expander from 19357 is swapped out

19357 does not distinguish between immediate and delayed placement the way 19340/19342 do — the same code applies whether the expander goes in at the time of mastectomy or later, and it is a 90-day global code that bundles every subsequent expansion visit inside that window, so those fill visits are not separately billable E/M encounters. When the expander is later exchanged for the permanent implant, that exchange is reported with 11970, not with 19342 — a permanent implant that follows an expander is a replacement by definition, never a delayed direct insertion, even though both scenarios end with a permanent implant placed on a date after the mastectomy. If that exchange happens inside 19357's global period, append modifier 58 (staged or related procedure by the same physician during the postoperative period) to 11970 rather than letting it deny as included in the prior global.

Autologous reconstruction: pedicled versus free flap

Flap-based reconstruction uses the patient's own tissue instead of an implant, and the code selected depends on which flap and which vascular technique the operative note describes — not just "flap reconstruction" as a category. CPT overhauled this code family in 2021, retiring the old catch-all "other technique" code, and the current set is technique-specific.

Autologous flap breast reconstruction CPT codes. Descriptors paraphrased; confirm exact language in the current CPT Professional edition.
CPT codeFlap / techniqueNotes
19361Latissimus dorsi flap (pedicled)2021 descriptor dropped "without prosthetic implant" — an implant or expander placed with the flap is now reported separately with 19340/19342/19357, not bundled or blocked.
19367TRAM flap, single pedicle, including donor-site closurePedicled transverse rectus abdominis myocutaneous flap.
19368TRAM flap, single pedicle, with microvascular anastomosis ("supercharging")Used when the pedicled flap's blood supply is augmented with a microvascular connection.
19369TRAM flap, double pedicle, including donor-site closureTwo rectus muscles harvested for additional flap volume/perfusion.
19364Free flap (e.g., free TRAM, DIEP, SIEA, GAP)Includes flap harvest, microvascular transfer (one artery, two veins), donor-site closure, and shaping into a breast mound. Reported the same regardless of which specific free flap is used.

19366 — the old "other technique" free-flap code — was deleted from CPT in 2021; a claim or template still referencing it is coding from a retired code set. The more persistent complication is DIEP and SIEA flaps specifically: 19364 is the current CPT code for any free flap including DIEP/SIEA, but some commercial payers have historically required the non-CPT HCPCS Level II code S2068 ("breast reconstruction with deep inferior epigastric perforator flap or superficial inferior epigastric artery flap... unilateral") instead, treating it as a distinct, more specific microsurgical code for reimbursement purposes. ⚠️ Which code a given commercial payer wants for a DIEP/SIEA case is plan-specific and has shifted over time industry-wide; confirm against the individual payer's current reconstructive-surgery policy before submitting, rather than defaulting to 19364 across the board.

Contralateral symmetry: billing surgery on a healthy breast as reconstruction

A mastopexy, reduction, or augmentation performed on the breast that was never operated on is the procedure most likely to get auto-flagged as cosmetic by a claims system that reads the operative note in isolation — the CPT code alone doesn't say why the surgery happened. WHCRA's second mandate exists specifically to prevent that outcome: surgery on the other breast to produce a symmetrical appearance with the reconstructed side is a mandated reconstructive benefit, not an elective one, whenever it follows mastectomy coverage under the plan.

Contralateral symmetry procedure codes and the diagnosis linkage that documents medical necessity.
CPT codeProcedureTypical supporting ICD-10
19316MastopexyN65.1 (disproportion of reconstructed breast) or N65.0 (deformity of reconstructed breast), verified billable via the ICD-10 code-lookup connector
19318Reduction mammaplastyN65.1 / N65.0, plus a secondary code reflecting the reconstruction history (e.g., Z42.1, encounter for breast reconstruction following mastectomy; or the acquired-absence-of-breast code for the affected side, Z90.11/Z90.12)
19325Augmentation mammaplastyN65.1 / N65.0, same secondary linkage

The coding move that makes the difference is the diagnosis, not the procedure code — 19316, 19318, and 19325 are the same codes a cosmetic mastopexy or augmentation would use, so what separates a WHCRA-mandated symmetry claim from an elective one on the payer's end is N65.0/N65.1 linked to the reconstruction history, not a cosmetic-surgery code set or a self-pay framing. Medicare local coverage guidance on cosmetic and reconstructive surgery treats reducing the size of a normal breast to bring it into symmetry with a mastectomy-reconstructed breast as a reconstructive procedure considered medically reasonable and necessary, consistent with WHCRA's statutory mandate. ⚠️ That specific coverage-article language is sourced from a secondary citation of Medicare local coverage guidance; this build's direct fetch of the CMS coverage article timed out mid-verification, so confirm the exact article ID and jurisdiction against your MAC's current published policy before citing it by document number in an appeal.

Timing matters for the claim as much as the diagnosis code: a symmetry procedure performed in the same operative session as the mastectomy-side reconstruction is billed alongside it; one performed later, once the reconstructed breast's final size and shape are known, is billed on its own claim with its own documentation trail back to the original mastectomy and WHCRA-covered reconstruction. Either way, WHCRA's "no time limit" language applies here too — there is no statutory deadline by which the symmetry procedure must be requested relative to the mastectomy.

Nipple-areolar reconstruction and micropigmentation: the final stage

Nipple-areolar complex (NAC) reconstruction is typically the last stage of the pathway, performed once the breast mound has settled into its final shape, and it is covered under the same WHCRA "all stages" mandate as everything before it — it is not a separate cosmetic finishing touch subject to a different coverage standard.

  • 1CPT 19350 reports nipple and/or areola reconstruction — formation of the nipple mound by a flap technique (C-V flap, skate flap, or nipple sharing, among others) combined with creation of the areola by skin graft. The code bundles both the flap and the graft; billing them separately as 14000-series and 15200-series codes on top of 19350 is unbundling, not staged billing.
  • 2Micropigmentation/areolar tattooing is reported separately once the reconstructed nipple-areola has healed, using CPT 11920 (tattooing/intradermal introduction of pigment, area 6.0 sq cm or less) or 11921 (6.1–20.0 sq cm), with add-on code 11922 for each additional 20.0 sq cm when the treated area is larger. This is typically performed weeks to months after 19350, as its own encounter.

Both are downstream of the same mastectomy/reconstruction history that justified everything earlier in the pathway, so the same "no time limit" principle applies if a patient returns for NAC reconstruction or a pigmentation touch-up long after the implant or flap stage closed out.

Pro tip

Keep a single running problem list per patient that maps every stage back to the original mastectomy encounter — mastectomy date, expander placement, each expansion visit, the implant or flap exchange, the symmetry procedure, and NAC reconstruction. When a later stage is questioned, the fastest resolution is pointing the reviewer at that chain rather than re-explaining the clinical rationale for a procedure performed two years after the first one. WHCRA doesn't require the stages to be continuous or fast — it requires them to be traceable.

Worked example: the full staged pathway across encounters

A patient undergoes a unilateral mastectomy and elects two-stage implant reconstruction. Here is how the code sequence runs across the encounters that follow, each billed as its own claim tied back to the same mastectomy history:

Example staged reconstruction pathway (implant-based, two-stage) — illustrative sequence, not a substitute for coding the actual operative note.
EncounterProcedureCode(s)
1. Mastectomy + immediate expanderMastectomy (reported by the breast surgeon) with immediate tissue expander placement by the reconstructive surgeon19357 (090-day global; covers this placement and every later fill)
2–n. Expansion visitsIn-office saline fills over several weeks to monthsBundled into 19357's global period — not separately billable
Next surgical encounterRemoval of expander, insertion of permanent implant11970 (append modifier 58 if inside the prior global period)
Later encounterContralateral reduction mammaplasty for symmetry19318, linked to N65.1 and the reconstruction history
Final encounterNipple-areolar reconstruction, followed weeks later by areolar micropigmentation19350; then 11920 (or 11921 + 11922 if larger area) as a separate encounter

Every one of these encounters can legitimately occur months or years apart — WHCRA does not require the stages to be completed within a benefit year, and a plan cannot deny a later stage solely because it falls in a different plan year than the mastectomy or the previous stage. Each claim should carry documentation that ties it back to the original mastectomy: the operative report referencing the prior stage, and diagnosis coding that reflects the reconstruction history rather than treating the later stage as a freestanding elective procedure.

The denial WHCRA is built to overturn

The single most common denial in this category isn't a coding error — it's a payer applying the wrong framework to a stage that arrives later than expected. A symmetry procedure requested eighteen months after the mastectomy gets denied as cosmetic. A second reconstructive revision gets denied as exceeding a reconstruction benefit maximum. A nipple-areolar reconstruction requested years later gets denied because "the reconstruction episode is closed." All three share the same flaw: they treat a WHCRA-covered stage as if it were subject to ordinary benefit-design limits — a cosmetic-surgery exclusion, an annual or lifetime dollar cap on a specific benefit category, a plan-year cutoff — when the statute specifically overrides those limits for reconstruction connected to a covered mastectomy.

The appeal that resolves fast
  • Cite WHCRA by name (29 U.S.C. § 1185b), name the specific mandate the denied procedure falls under (reconstruction stage, symmetry, prosthesis, or complication treatment), and state plainly that the statute imposes no time limit and cannot be overridden by a plan-year or benefit-maximum provision. This is a policy-scope argument, and it asks the reviewer to apply the plan's own legal obligations correctly — it typically moves faster than a clinical appeal because it isn't asking anyone to re-judge medical necessity.
The appeal that stalls
  • Resubmitting with only a restated clinical justification — "patient needs symmetry for cosmetic balance" or "revision is medically necessary" — without ever naming WHCRA. That framing invites the reviewer to keep evaluating it as an elective or benefit-limited procedure, which is exactly the category the statute says it isn't.

⚠️ How a specific plan interprets "reconstruction benefit maximum" language against WHCRA can still vary by plan document and state insurance regulation, and self-insured ERISA plans are enforced differently (through DOL) than fully insured plans (through state regulators and CMS for non-federal governmental plans) — confirm the plan type and enforcement path before assuming identical appeal language will work identically across a payer's whole book of business.

Frequently asked questions

Does WHCRA require a cancer diagnosis before it applies to breast reconstruction?

No. The Women's Health and Cancer Rights Act is triggered by mastectomy coverage under the plan, not by a cancer diagnosis. Once a group health plan or individual policy covers mastectomies for any reason, it must also cover reconstruction, symmetry surgery, prostheses, and complications including lymphedema for anyone who receives a covered mastectomy under that plan. A denial that rests on "this wasn't a cancer case" is not a valid basis for refusing WHCRA-mandated coverage on its own.

Is there a deadline for requesting breast reconstruction after a mastectomy under WHCRA?

No. WHCRA sets no time limit on when a patient can request reconstruction, and a plan cannot deny coverage because years have passed since the mastectomy. This applies to every stage the statute covers: the reconstruction itself, a later revision, a delayed contralateral symmetry procedure, or nipple-areolar reconstruction requested well after the initial surgery. A denial citing a benefit-year cutoff or a "reconstruction window" conflicts with the statute's plain language and is a strong basis for appeal.

Can a payer deny a mastopexy or reduction on the unaffected breast as cosmetic?

Not when it is performed to achieve symmetry with a mastectomy-reconstructed breast. WHCRA's second mandate specifically requires coverage of "surgery and reconstruction of the other breast to produce a symmetrical appearance," and Medicare coverage guidance treats reducing a normal breast to match a reconstructed one as a reconstructive procedure, not a cosmetic one. The claim should carry a diagnosis code that documents the disproportion (such as N65.1 or N65.0) linked to the reconstruction, not a cosmetic-surgery code set, and a denial coded as cosmetic on this basis is a strong candidate for a statute-citing appeal rather than a medical-necessity argument.

What is the coding difference between CPT 19340/19342 and the CPT 19357-to-11970 pathway?

19340 and 19342 report direct insertion of a permanent breast implant with no tissue expander involved — 19340 when it happens in the same operative session as the mastectomy, 19342 when it happens on a later date. 19357 reports placement of a tissue expander (immediate or delayed) and includes every subsequent saline-fill visit inside its global period; the later swap of that expander for the permanent implant is reported with 11970, not 19342, because a permanent implant placed after a tissue expander is coded as a replacement, not a delayed direct insertion. Confirming which pathway a patient is actually on — direct implant versus staged expander — before selecting a code is the single most common accuracy check in implant-based reconstruction billing.
