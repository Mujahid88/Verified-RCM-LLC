Bunionectomy and hammertoe correction billing: global periods and toe modifiers.

Forefoot surgery billing turns on four things a payer's edit system checks before a human ever looks at the claim: which of the five current bunionectomy codes matches the osteotomy actually performed, whether the 90-day global period was respected, whether every line carries the specific toe it belongs to, and whether the multiple-procedure reduction was applied where it should have been. This guide covers the January 2026 overhaul of the bunionectomy code family (28292-28299), hammertoe correction under 28285 and 28286, the global-period rules that govern post-op E/M billing, the full T1-T9/TA toe modifier set, and a worked bilateral-plus-hammertoe example with the correct modifier stack.

Reviewed by Phares A., MD · Physiotherapist, CPC (AAPC) · Part of our podiatry billing and coding guide

Key takeaways

  • The bunionectomy code family was rebuilt effective January 1, 2026. CPT deleted 28290, 28293, and 28294, and revised 28292 and 28296-28299 to drop eponyms like Keller, McBride, Mayo, Mitchell, and Lapidus in favor of anatomic, any-method descriptions — the operative note now has to name the bone and the cut, not the technique's namesake.
  • Both bunionectomy (28292-28299) and hammertoe correction (28285) carry a 90-day global period. Routine post-op visits for that foot are bundled into the surgical payment; an unrelated E/M during those 90 days needs modifier 24 and documentation that proves it wasn't about the surgical site.
  • Toe-specific modifiers (TA, T1-T9) outrank LT/RT whenever a specific toe can be identified — required for hammertoe correction and any claim where two toe procedures on the same foot need to be told apart for the payer's edit system.
  • A bilateral bunionectomy plus a same-session hammertoe correction stacks three billing rules at once — bilateral pricing, toe-specific modifiers, and the multiple-procedure payment reduction — and getting the rank order wrong on any one of them shorts the claim.

The 2026 bunionectomy code overhaul: five codes, one clinical decision point

Effective January 1, 2026, CPT restructured the entire hallux valgus correction family. Codes 28290, 28293, and 28294 were deleted outright. The surviving codes — 28292 and 28296 through 28299 — were revised to remove the surgeon-eponym language (Keller, McBride, Mayo, Mitchell, Chevron, concentric, and Lapidus-type procedure) and replace it with a description built entirely around anatomic location and technique: which bone the osteotomy or arthrodesis is performed on, "any method." The change means a coder can no longer select the code from the technique name in the operative note alone — the note has to state where the correction was actually made.

Current CPT bunionectomy family (28292, 28296-28299), effective January 1, 2026. All five include sesamoidectomy "when performed" as part of the base code — it is not separately billable.
CodeAnatomic correctionTypical clinical fit
28292With resection of the proximal phalanx base, when performed, any methodCorrection centered at the base of the great toe's proximal phalanx
28296With distal metatarsal osteotomy, any methodMild-to-moderate deformity corrected at the metatarsal head (the former Mitchell/Chevron territory)
28297With first metatarsal and medial cuneiform joint arthrodesis, any methodFusion-based correction for hypermobility of the first ray (the former Lapidus territory)
28298With proximal phalanx osteotomy, any methodCorrection cut in the toe's own proximal phalanx (the former Akin territory), often paired with a metatarsal-level code
28299With double osteotomy, any methodTwo osteotomies performed to correct more severe angular deformity

28290, 28293, and 28294 — the simple exostectomy and implant-based Keller/McBride/Mayo-type codes — no longer exist as billable CPT codes for dates of service in 2026 and later. A claim, superbill template, or EHR order set still carrying one of those three deleted codes is a denial waiting to happen, and it's the single most common trap in this transition: a practice that hasn't updated its charge-capture templates since the changeover keeps generating claims on a code the payer's system no longer recognizes. Audit every bunionectomy order set and charge ticket in the practice for the deleted codes before the next chart closes, not after the first rejection comes back.

The upcoding/downcoding risk runs in both directions now. Because 28299 (double osteotomy) pays more than a single-site code like 28296 or 28298, a claim listing 28299 needs the operative note to document two distinct osteotomies, not one osteotomy described twice in different words. Conversely, a case that genuinely involved both a metatarsal-level correction and a phalanx-level correction should be coded to reflect that combined work rather than defaulting to the single code the practice bills most often out of habit.

Hammertoe correction: 28285 and the fifth-toe alternative

Hammertoe correction on the lesser toes (second through fifth) is a separate code family from the hallux valgus codes above, and the two are frequently confused because they're performed in the same operative session on the same foot.

Hammertoe and related lesser-toe correction codes.
CodeDescriptionNotes
28285Correction, hammertoe (e.g., interphalangeal fusion, partial or total phalangectomy)The standard hammertoe code; billed once per toe corrected, not once per procedure note
28286Correction, cock-up fifth toe, with plastic skin closure (e.g., Ruiz-Mora type procedure)Specific to a fixed, elevated fifth-toe deformity — not used interchangeably with 28285
28280Syndactylization, toes (e.g., webbing or Kelikian-type procedure)Creates a web between toes; a different clinical problem from hammertoe correction, occasionally performed in the same session

28285 is billed per toe corrected, not per operative note. A patient with hammertoe correction performed on the second and third toes of the same foot generates two line items — 28285 with the toe modifier for the second toe, and a second 28285 line with the toe modifier for the third toe — not a single unit with a note that says "toes 2 and 3." Diagnosis coding follows the same laterality logic: ICD-10-CM M20.41/M20.42 (other hammer toe, acquired, right/left foot) and M20.11/M20.12 (hallux valgus, acquired, right/left foot) both carry side-specific codes, and the unspecified-side versions (M20.40, M20.10) should be avoided whenever the operative side is documented, which it always is in a surgical chart.

The 90-day global period and modifier 24

Both the bunionectomy family and hammertoe correction are major procedures under the Medicare Physician Fee Schedule, carrying a 90-day global period. That period bundles the pre-operative visit, the surgery itself, and all routine post-operative care related to that foot into the single surgical payment — no separate E/M charge for the two-week suture check, the cast or boot adjustment, or the routine six-week follow-up.

Modifier 24 applies
  • An unrelated E/M by the same physician (or another physician of the same specialty in the same group) during the 90-day window, for a problem that isn't about the surgical foot.
  • Example: a bunionectomy patient's already-scheduled diabetes follow-up three weeks after surgery — unrelated to the foot, billable with 24 attached and documentation that shows the visit addressed the unrelated condition.
Modifier 24 doesn't apply
  • Routine wound checks, suture or pin removal, cast changes, or management of a surgical-site complication (delayed union, infection, hardware irritation) — all bundled into the global surgical package regardless of how the visit is documented.
  • An E/M visit billed the same day as the surgery itself, which belongs under modifier 57 or 25 logic depending on whether it was the decision-for-surgery visit, not modifier 24.

The distinction a payer's reviewer is actually checking for on a modifier-24 claim is whether the visit note stands on its own as unrelated — a separate chief complaint, separate history, and separate assessment that doesn't reference the surgical foot. A note that mentions the recent bunionectomy anywhere in the assessment, even in passing, gives the reviewer grounds to bundle the visit back into the global package.

Toe-specific modifiers: TA, T1-T9, and when LT/RT isn't enough

Foot and toe surgery has its own anatomic modifier set, and payer edit systems increasingly require the toe-specific version rather than the general foot-level LT/RT pair whenever a specific digit can be identified. The rule most billing teams get backward: when a toe-specific modifier applies, it replaces LT/RT on that line — it doesn't get appended alongside it.

HCPCS Level II toe and digit modifiers.
ModifierDigitFoot
TAGreat toe (first)Left
T1Second toeLeft
T2Third toeLeft
T3Fourth toeLeft
T4Fifth toeLeft
T5Great toe (first)Right
T6Second toeRight
T7Third toeRight
T8Fourth toeRight
T9Fifth toeRight

The error that costs the most reimbursement here isn't picking the wrong toe — it's confusing TA with T5. TA is the left great toe; T5 is the right great toe. Since a bunionectomy is overwhelmingly a great-toe procedure, that single digit transposition on a one-sided bunionectomy claim reports the correction on the wrong foot entirely, which either denies outright or, worse, pays and creates a mismatched medical record if it's never caught.

  • 1Use TA/T5-T9 for a single-foot bunionectomy, not LT/RT, whenever the payer's edit system recognizes the toe-specific set — check the individual payer's modifier policy, since acceptance isn't universal across every commercial plan.
  • 2Hammertoe correction always needs the specific digit modifier (T1-T4 or T6-T9) — it's a lesser-toe procedure by definition, so TA/T5 never apply to it.
  • 3Cap each toe modifier at one unit per date of service. Two separate procedures on the same toe on the same day still get one T-modifier per line, not one per procedure, and the toe-modifier maximum-unit edit will reject a duplicate.

Multiple-procedure billing on the same foot, same session

When more than one procedure with a multiple-surgery indicator is performed in the same operative session, the Medicare Physician Fee Schedule's standard multiple-surgery payment rule ranks the procedures by fee schedule value and pays the highest-valued one at 100%, with the second- and lower-ranked procedures paid at 50% of their own fee schedule amount (by-report pricing applies past the fifth procedure, never below 50%). Bunionectomy and hammertoe codes are both major musculoskeletal procedures and are billed under this standard reduction rule in the overwhelming majority of cases — ⚠️ confirm the exact multiple-surgery indicator for the specific codes and code combination against the current-year Medicare Physician Fee Schedule Relative Value File before assuming it applies identically for every payer, since some commercial and Medicaid plans apply their own reduction schedule instead of adopting Medicare's.

Bilateral pricing is a separate mechanism from the multiple-procedure reduction, and the two interact rather than replace each other. A bilaterally-eligible procedure reported with modifier 50 is priced at 150% of the single fee schedule amount and then, if other procedures were performed in the same session, that 150%-priced line is itself ranked against them under the standard multiple-surgery hierarchy. Two procedures performed on opposite feet in the same session — a right bunionectomy and an unrelated left hammertoe correction, say — are not a bilateral procedure and don't take modifier 50; they're two separate procedures subject only to the standard multiple-surgery reduction, each identified by its own foot and toe modifier.

Pro tip

Confirm the payer's bilateral billing preference before the first claim on a bilateral case goes out, not after it comes back short. Medicare wants one line with modifier 50 at 150%; a meaningful share of commercial and Medicaid payers still want two lines with LT and RT, each priced under the standard bilateral rule. Submitting the Medicare format to a payer that wants split lines — or vice versa — is one of the most common reasons a bilateral bunionectomy claim underpays without ever generating an outright denial, which means it often goes unnoticed until someone runs a fee-schedule variance report.

Frequently asked questions

What's the real difference between CPT 28296 and 28298 for a bunionectomy?

It's where the cut is made, not how severe the bunion is. 28296 corrects hallux valgus with a distal metatarsal osteotomy, any method — the classic Mitchell/Chevron-style correction at the metatarsal head, generally used for mild-to-moderate deformity. 28298 corrects it with a proximal phalanx osteotomy, any method — an Akin-style cut in the toe bone itself, typically added when the metatarsal correction alone won't fully realign the toe. Since the January 1, 2026 CPT revision, neither code is defined by the surgeon's chosen eponym anymore; both are billed by anatomic location of the osteotomy, so the operative note has to state which bone was cut, not just name a technique.

Do I bill a bilateral bunionectomy with modifier 50 or with LT and RT on separate lines?

Both conventions exist and the payer decides which one it wants. Medicare's standard approach for a bilaterally-eligible procedure is one line with modifier 50, priced at 150% of the single fee schedule amount. Many commercial and Medicaid payers instead want the same code reported on two lines with LT and RT, each priced under the standard multiple-surgery rule (100% and 50%). Billing it the wrong way for a given payer is a common reason a bilateral bunionectomy claim pays short or denies as a duplicate — confirm the payer's bilateral billing policy before the claim goes out, not after it's denied.

What global period applies to a bunionectomy or hammertoe correction, and when can I bill an E/M visit during it?

Bunionectomy codes (28292-28299) and hammertoe correction (28285) are major procedures carrying a 90-day global period, which bundles all routine post-operative visits related to that foot into the surgical payment. A separately billable E/M visit during those 90 days needs modifier 24 — unrelated evaluation and management by the same physician during a postoperative period — and the documentation has to show the visit addressed a problem genuinely unrelated to the surgery, not routine wound checks, suture removal, or management of the surgical site itself.

Can I bill a bunionectomy and a hammertoe correction from the same operative session without a bundling denial?

Yes, when the toe-specific modifiers on the claim make clear the procedures were performed on different anatomic sites. A hallux valgus correction (28292-28299) is on the great toe; a hammertoe correction (28285) is on a lesser toe (second through fifth). Appending the correct T-modifier or TA/T1-T9 combination to each line — not just LT/RT — is what distinguishes them for the payer's edit system. Multiple procedures in the same session are also subject to the multiple-procedure payment reduction, which is a separate issue from bundling: the claim can be unbundled correctly and still have its lower-valued line reduced.
