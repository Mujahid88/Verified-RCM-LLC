Google Business Profile management for medical practices.

For most practices the Google listing is the first thing a prospective patient sees, and often the only thing they read before choosing. It shows your hours, your reviews, your photos and your phone number in the moment someone is deciding. This page covers claiming and verifying it, what to publish, how to handle reviews without breaching HIPAA, and how group practices manage many locations.

Key takeaways

  • Local ranking is driven mainly by relevance, distance and prominence. You control relevance and prominence; distance you cannot change.
  • Responding to a patient review can itself disclose that someone is your patient. Never confirm treatment publicly.
  • Practitioner listings and practice listings are different entities. Both need to exist and be consistent.
  • Hours accuracy on holidays is a small detail with outsized effect, wrong hours generate one-star reviews from people who never became patients.

Getting the listing claimed and verified

Start at business.google.com. If a listing already exists for your address, and one usually does, generated automatically or created by a former employee or vendor, claim it rather than creating a duplicate. Duplicates split your reviews and confuse ranking. Verification is typically by postcard, phone, email or video, and healthcare listings are frequently routed to video verification, where you record the premises, signage and equipment.

Once verified, the fields that matter most are the primary category, the service area, hours including holiday hours, the phone number that a person actually answers, and the appointment link. Primary category has a disproportionate effect on which searches you appear in: "Medical clinic" and "Family practice physician" surface for different queries, and choosing the vaguer one to seem broader usually reduces visibility rather than widening it.

Pro tip

Seed your own Q&A section. Post the ten questions your front desk answers daily, parking, which insurances you take, whether you see walk-ins, what to bring to a first visit, and answer them from the business account. If you leave it empty, strangers will answer for you, often wrongly, and those answers appear directly in search results.

Reviews without a HIPAA breach

This is where practices get into genuine trouble. A public reply that references a patient's visit, condition or treatment confirms that they received care from you, and that confirmation is a disclosure of protected health information, even when the patient disclosed it first, and even when the review is unfair and you are simply correcting the record. Enforcement actions have followed exactly this pattern.

The safe pattern is a reply that thanks the reviewer for the feedback, states your commitment to patient experience, and moves the conversation to a private channel, without confirming or denying that the person is a patient or referencing any clinical detail. Every reply we publish for clients follows a template reviewed against that constraint. Requesting reviews is fine and worth systematising, what is not fine is offering anything of value in exchange, or filtering so only happy patients are asked.

Do and don't

Do
  • Ask every patient for a review through the same routine step, so the sample is not skewed.
  • Reply to every review within a few days using a compliance-reviewed template.
  • Keep NAP identical across your site, profile and directories.
  • Post real photos of the entrance, waiting area and parking.
  • Maintain a separate verified listing for each location and each practitioner.
Don't
  • Don't confirm someone is a patient, or mention any clinical detail, in a public reply.
  • Don't offer discounts, gift cards or anything of value for reviews.
  • Don't create multiple listings for one address to target more keywords.
  • Don't use a call-tracking number as the primary profile number unless the true number is also present.
  • Don't keyword-stuff the business name; it violates guidelines and risks suspension.

Frequently asked questions

Can we remove a negative review?

Only if it violates Google's policies, spam, conflict of interest, offensive content, or a review that is clearly not about a genuine experience with your business. Reviews that are merely negative or unfair cannot be removed, and attempting to have them removed by contacting the reviewer can itself create a HIPAA problem if it confirms they were a patient. The durable answer is a steady flow of genuine positive reviews that pushes a single bad one into context.

Should each doctor have their own listing?

Yes. Google treats practitioners as distinct entities from the practice, and patients frequently search by physician name. Each practitioner listing should point to the practice location, use the practitioner's own specialty category, and link to their bio page on your website. Where a physician practises at multiple locations, the listing follows their primary location to avoid duplicate-listing problems.

How often should we post updates?

Roughly weekly is enough to signal an actively managed listing without becoming a burden. Useful posts are practical rather than promotional: new provider joining, extended hours, a new insurance plan accepted, seasonal availability such as flu clinics, or holiday closures. Posts have limited direct ranking effect but they occupy space in your listing and answer questions before a patient has to call.

Our listing was suspended. What now?

Suspensions usually follow a guideline breach: a keyword-stuffed name, a virtual office or address that cannot be verified, a category that misrepresents the business, or a sudden batch of edits. The fix is to correct the underlying issue first, gather evidence that you operate at the address, signage, licence, utility bill, then file a reinstatement request. Filing before fixing the cause almost always fails and can make a second attempt harder.

Do you manage profiles for multi-location groups?

Yes. Groups need a location-group structure with consistent categories and naming, per-location hours and photos, distinct landing pages on your site for each location, and a review workflow that routes each review to the right practice manager while keeping responses compliant. We also monitor for duplicate listings, which multiply as staff and vendors create them over time.
