ENT (Otolaryngology) Medical Billing and Coding: The Complete 2026 Guide

ENT stacks a specific kind of billing difficulty: dense same-day bundling between diagnostic scopes and surgical ones, a mid-line-versus-paired-organ modifier trap that trips up modifier 50 constantly, and Medicare coverage for its highest-growth procedures — allergy testing, immunotherapy, hypoglossal nerve stimulation — written and revised separately by seven different Medicare Administrative Contractors. Every competitor guide we reviewed (Neolytix, AAO-HNS's own modifier pages, ENT-Cloud, AnnexMed) tells readers to "check NCCI edits" and "review your MAC's LCD" without publishing a single indicator value, code pair, or document ID. This guide names them: the actual balloon-sinuplasty-versus-FESS code pairs, all five current and five incoming Allergy Diagnostic Testing LCDs by MAC, the CGS immunotherapy LCD that quietly retired in October 2025, and the seven hypoglossal nerve stimulation LCDs governing medical necessity for one of ENT's fastest-growing revenue lines.

Reviewed by Phares A., MD · Physiotherapist, CPC (AAPC) · Updated August 2026

Key takeaways

  • Modifier 50 only belongs on genuinely paired structures. Both ears, both maxillary sinuses — yes. A midline procedure like septoplasty (30520) is not a bilateral organ, and appending 50 to it (or to a balloon dilation that should be LT/RT) is a documented overpayment pattern, not a coding shortcut.
  • Balloon dilation (31295–31297) bundles into FESS on the same sinus, same session. 31295 is a component of 31256 and 31267; 31296 isn't separately reported with 31276 on the same sinus. Both are billable only on a different sinus or the contralateral side, with LT/RT documenting the split.
  • 69436 requires the operative note to say "general anesthesia." Without that explicit statement, the claim is indistinguishable from 69433 on audit, regardless of what sedation was actually used.
  • Unspecified otitis media and sinusitis codes draw denials a specific code wouldn't. H66.90/H66.91/H66.92/H66.93 and J32.9 fail medical-necessity review far more often than a laterality- and type-specific alternative from the same family.
  • 95165 units are 1 cc aliquots, capped at 10 per vial for Medicare — not injections, not vials. This is the single most common allergy immunotherapy billing error we see.
  • Five MACs are replacing their Allergy Diagnostic Testing LCDs between 09/27 and 10/04/2026 — CGS, Wellpoint, Palmetto, WPS, and Noridian all confirmed live in the CMS Coverage Database as of this build. No other guide we found has caught this yet.

ENT billing at a glance

ENT's revenue mix is unusually broad for a single specialty: office E/M, a high volume of in-office procedures (scope exams, cerumen removal, tube placement), ASC and hospital-based surgery, an audiology component that behaves like its own mini-specialty inside the practice, and — increasingly — allergy testing and immunotherapy run as an in-house service line. That breadth is exactly why ENT denies differently than a single-modality specialty: the failure points are spread across bundling edits between scope codes, medical-necessity gates on elective functional surgery, and unit-counting rules on allergy and audiology services that don't exist anywhere else in a typical practice's billing.

Denial concentration in ENT tends to cluster around three points rather than spreading evenly across the claim volume: same-day procedure-plus-scope bundling (where the diagnostic-versus-surgical or balloon-versus-FESS distinction determines whether a second line is payable at all), elective functional surgery medical necessity (septoplasty and, increasingly, HGNS), and unit-counting on the ancillary services — allergy testing, immunotherapy, and audiometry — that most other specialties don't run at ENT's volume. A practice tracking its own denial data by these three buckets, rather than by generic CARC groupings alone, generally finds the fix concentrated in a small number of workflow changes rather than spread across the whole claims process.

What actually separates ENT revenue cycle work from a primary care panel is the sheer density of billable events packed into a single office visit slot. A family medicine practice bills mostly E/M; an ENT practice bills E/M plus a scope, or E/M plus cerumen removal, or a standalone procedure visit with no E/M at all, dozens of times a day — and every one of those combinations carries its own bundling and modifier-25 logic. Three structural differences drive most of the operational complexity:

  • 1In-office procedure volume. Scopes, tube checks, cerumen removal, and allergy testing turn a single appointment slot into two or three billable lines routinely, which multiplies the number of bundling decisions a coder has to get right every single day compared to a panel that's mostly E/M-only visits.
  • 2ASC and hospital site-of-service rules. FESS, tonsillectomy, septoplasty, and hypoglossal nerve stimulation implants routinely move between in-office, ASC, and hospital OR settings depending on complexity and payer requirements, and the facility-fee and technical-component implications shift with the site — a balloon sinuplasty performed in-office captures technical revenue a facility-based case cedes entirely.
  • 3Audiology as a service line inside a service line. Practices running in-house audiology (audiometry, tympanometry, vestibular testing) or allergy testing/immunotherapy are effectively operating a second ancillary business with its own component-split, unit-counting, and supervision rules layered on top of standard ENT billing — and those rules don't transfer from anything else in the practice's billing operation.

What changed in 2026

Three separate coverage-policy cycles landed on ENT in the same twelve months, and none of them made it into the guides we reviewed while researching this page. All three concentrate on the specialty's highest-growth, highest-scrutiny service lines — allergy testing and immunotherapy, and hypoglossal nerve stimulation — which is exactly why practices building or expanding either service line in 2026 need this section more than a generic "check your MAC's LCD" reminder ever provides.

A practical note on identifying your own jurisdiction: each MAC administers Medicare Part B claims for a defined set of states, and which one applies to a given practice is determined by the practice's own service location, not by which MAC happens to be cited most often in a general coding reference. Confirm your practice's specific MAC through your own Medicare enrollment records or the CMS MAC jurisdiction lookup rather than assuming from geography alone, since MAC jurisdiction boundaries don't always track intuitively with state lines and have shifted with contract re-awards in the past. Every LCD document ID in this guide is tied to a specific MAC precisely because citing "the LCD" without naming which MAC's version applies to your claim is not a citation a payer or auditor can act on.

On balloon sinuplasty coverage specifically: we did not identify a dedicated, retitled Medicare LCD or NCD specific to balloon sinus dilation coverage criteria comparable to the allergy and HGNS policy waves described above — balloon dilation coverage for Medicare beneficiaries generally flows through the same medical-necessity standard applied to sinus surgery broadly, rather than a standalone balloon-specific coverage article, based on the coverage searches run for this build. Commercial payers, by contrast, frequently do publish balloon-sinuplasty-specific clinical policy bulletins with their own explicit criteria, which is why the prior-authorization documentation table later in this guide treats balloon sinuplasty as a distinct, commercially-driven prior-auth item even though it isn't governed by its own dedicated Medicare LCD in the way allergy testing and HGNS are.

Hypoglossal nerve stimulation: four LCDs refreshed, three still running on 2020 criteria

Four of the seven MAC-specific Hypoglossal Nerve Stimulation for Obstructive Sleep Apnea LCDs received substantive 2026 updates: CGS (L38307, effective 03/05/2026), WPS (L38528, effective 03/26/2026), Palmetto GBA (L38276, effective 05/14/2026), and Wellpoint Federal (L38387, effective 04/01/2026). Noridian reissued its version (L38310) on 10/16/2025, replacing a prior LCD (L38312) retired the same day. Novitas (L38385) and First Coast (L38398) are both still running on coverage criteria dated 03/15/2020, with no 2026 refresh confirmed as of this build. Full detail on what each version actually requires for medical necessity is in our sleep apnea surgery billing and prior authorization guide.

On the CPT/HCPCS side: effective January 1, 2026, CMS added new HCPCS Level II codes C8007, C8008, and C8009 for hospital outpatient and ASC billing of newer hypoglossal nerve stimulator devices that don't require a separate distal respiratory sensor electrode — open implantation, revision/replacement, and removal, respectively. The existing CPT code 64582 (open implantation of array, pulse generator, and distal respiratory sensor electrode) remains active and stays assigned to APC 5465 under the CY2026 OPPS final rule. ⚠️ This CPT/HCPCS detail was confirmed via secondary industry sourcing (AAO-HNS coding guidance and CMS OPPS commentary reported by billing-industry publications) rather than a direct CMS.gov fetch, which returned access errors during this build — confirm current descriptors and APC assignment against the CY2026 OPPS/ASC final rule and your CPT Professional edition before billing.

On the diagnosis side: the ICD-10-CM code set used throughout this guide (otitis media, chronic sinusitis, impacted cerumen, obstructive sleep apnea, and nasal/sinus disorder families) was validated live against the FY2026 code set, the version in effect for dates of service from October 1, 2025 through September 30, 2026. We did not identify a major structural overhaul to the ENT-relevant diagnosis families for FY2026 comparable to what cardiology saw with its lower-extremity revascularization codes — the codes cited throughout this guide are the current, stable versions. What does change every October 1 regardless is which specific codes are valid for a given date of service, so any code selected for a claim should be checked against the fiscal year covering that specific encounter date, not just against whatever code set is current when the claim is coded.

E/M coding for ENT

Established-patient office visits (99212–99215) follow the same 2021 MDM-based framework as every other specialty, leveled on either total time or medical decision making — not both, and not split between them. What makes ENT E/M distinctive is how often the presenting problem looks simple on the surface but carries real complexity in the note: a chronic sinusitis follow-up with a patient who has failed two prior medication trials and is now being worked up for surgery is a different MDM level than a straightforward acute sinusitis visit, even though both might get typed up in three sentences if the documentation habit is thin.

The three MDM elements — number and complexity of problems addressed, amount and complexity of data reviewed, and risk of complications or morbidity from management — apply to ENT exactly as they do everywhere else, but the specialty's typical data element is different from a primary-care visit's: where primary care often documents complexity through lab review and specialist coordination, ENT visits more often build data complexity through the interpretation of a test the physician performed personally that same visit (a scope, an audiogram) or ordered and is independently reviewing (imaging, a sleep study). That in-house data element is real MDM credit, but only when the note documents the independent interpretation explicitly, not just the fact that the test was performed.

Modifier 25 is the recurring flashpoint in ENT because so much of the specialty's revenue comes from same-day office procedures — cerumen removal, nasal endoscopy, laryngoscopy, tube check. The threshold is a significant, separately identifiable E/M service beyond the work inherent to the procedure itself. A visit where the physician evaluates a new complaint, makes a distinct management decision, and then performs a scope to confirm it supports 25; a visit where the entire encounter is "here for scheduled scope, findings normal, no other complaints" does not, because there's no separately identifiable E/M work beyond the pre- and post-procedure assessment the procedure code already accounts for. Full detail on the modifier 25 decision and every other modifier that carries weight in this specialty is in our ENT modifier guide: 50, LT/RT, 25, and 59/XS explained.

New-patient consult referrals carry their own trap that has nothing to do with clinical complexity: patient status. A patient referred by a primary care physician for a first ENT evaluation is new to the ENT practice regardless of how thoroughly the referring physician already worked up the problem, and the new-patient code set (99202–99205) applies as long as the three-year lookback confirms no prior face-to-face service by any physician of the same specialty in the same group. Two failure modes recur constantly around this:

  • 1Billing established-patient codes for a genuinely new consult because the referral packet made the visit feel like a continuation of someone else's workup. Patient status is about the billing practice's own history with the patient, not the referring physician's.
  • 2Missing a same-group, different-location conflict. A large multi-location ENT group where the patient saw a different physician in the same specialty at another office within the past three years is established, even if this is the patient's first visit to this particular office or this particular physician — a scheduling system that doesn't check group-wide history routinely miscodes this as new.

Time-based leveling remains available as the alternative to MDM whenever total time is the stronger-documented pathway — a lengthy new-patient dysphonia workup with substantial counseling on differential and next steps often qualifies faster under total time than by fighting to document MDM elements that weren't the actual driver of the visit's length.

Diagnostic testing: audiometry, vestibular, and allergy testing

ENT runs three genuinely distinct diagnostic testing operations under one roof, and each has its own component-split and unit-counting logic that doesn't transfer from the others: audiometry and vestibular testing run on the physician/technical component-split model diagnostic imaging uses everywhere else in medicine, while allergy testing and immunotherapy run on a per-test and per-aliquot unit-counting model that has no real analog elsewhere in a typical ENT practice's billing. Treating all three as a single "diagnostic testing" billing workflow, rather than three distinct sets of rules, is where practices new to running an in-house allergy or audiology program tend to make their first several months of billing errors.

Audiometry

Two operational distinctions matter before any of the individual codes: whether the test is diagnostic or screening-level, and who's performing it under what supervision. A screening audiogram performed by clinical staff to triage whether a diagnostic referral is needed is a fundamentally different service, and often a different code, than a full diagnostic audiogram performed or supervised by the physician or a licensed audiologist — billing a screening-level encounter as if it were the comprehensive diagnostic code is both a coding and a supervision problem, not just a documentation shortfall. Most payers also expect diagnostic audiometry to be ordered with a specific clinical indication (hearing loss complaint, tinnitus workup, pre-surgical baseline, ototoxic medication monitoring) rather than performed as a routine annual screen with no documented complaint, which is a distinction worth building into scheduling and order-entry workflow rather than catching after the claim denies.

Tympanometry (92567) is billed constantly alongside an E/M visit for a child with suspected middle-ear effusion, and it's a frequent same-day bundling point — confirm your payer's specific edit before assuming it always pays separately from the E/M on the same date; documentation of medical necessity distinct from the visit's E/M reasoning strengthens the claim regardless.

Worked component-split scenario: a solo ENT practice sends a patient to a hospital audiology suite for a comprehensive audiogram, and the physician reads and interprets the results in the office the following week. The hospital owns the booth and employs the technician, so the hospital bills 92557-TC. The physician's interpretation and report is billed 92557-26 by the ENT practice — never the global code, because the practice didn't perform the technical component. Billing 92557 globally here creates the same conflicting-claim problem covered in the modifiers section: the hospital's technical claim and the practice's global claim both hit the payer for the same date and patient, and one of them denies.

Vestibular function testing

Vestibular testing has its own internal bundling structure that trips up practices billing it for the first time. 92540 is the comprehensive basic vestibular evaluation, and it bundles spontaneous nystagmus (92541), positional nystagmus (92542), optokinetic nystagmus (92544), and oscillating tracking (92545) into a single code — billing 92540 alongside any of its four components on the same date is a documented unbundling error, not a legitimate itemization. Bill 92540 only when all four components were genuinely performed; bill the individual component codes only when fewer than all four were done. Sinusoidal vertical-axis rotational testing (92546) and computerized dynamic posturography (92548) are separate, non-component tests billed on their own when performed.

Frequency matters as much as component-bundling for vestibular testing: several of the individual component codes (92542, 92544, 92545, 92546, 92548) are described in coverage guidance as billable once per day regardless of how many times the underlying measurement is repeated or re-analyzed within that session — a technician re-running a positional test three times to get a clean reading still bills 92542 once, not three times, because the repeated attempts within one session are part of obtaining one billable result, not three separate billable tests.

This is one of the areas where citing a MAC by name actually matters, because vestibular testing coverage articles are genuinely MAC-specific rather than boilerplate: Palmetto GBA (L34537, "Vestibular Function Testing"), Novitas Solutions (L35007, "Vestibular and Audiologic Function Studies"), and First Coast Service Options (L33966, "Vestibular Function Tests") each publish their own coverage article, confirmed live in the CMS Coverage Database. Supervision matters here too: the component tests (92541–92542, 92544–92548) may be performed by a licensed audiologist under physician order, by a licensed physician (preferably board-certified in otolaryngology, neurology, or otology/neurology), or by another appropriately licensed provider under a physician's personal supervision — a practice running vestibular testing without confirming which supervision tier its payer mix actually requires is exposed on more than just the coding. Full code-by-code detail and worked billing scenarios are in our audiometry and vestibular testing billing guide.

Allergy testing and immunotherapy

Allergy skin and intradermal testing (95004–95028) is billed per test performed, not per visit — the unit count on the claim has to match the actual number of individual allergen tests documented, and a common denial pattern is a unit count that doesn't reconcile against the testing worksheet in the chart. Immunotherapy splits into two distinct services that both have to appear on a complete claim: 95165 covers the professional supervision and preparation of the antigen itself, billed in units of 1 cc aliquots drawn from a multi-dose vial, capped at 10 billable doses per vial for Medicare regardless of how many aliquots the vial could technically yield. The injection administration is billed separately — 95115 for a single injection, 95117 for two or more injections in the same visit. Billing 95165 units that exceed the 10-per-vial cap, or omitting the separate injection-administration code entirely, are the two most common immunotherapy billing errors we see.

In vitro testing — specific IgE blood testing, distinct from the in vivo skin and intradermal panel described above — is a separate coding pathway some ENT allergy programs use instead of, or alongside, skin testing, particularly for patients on medications that interfere with skin test results or with dermatologic conditions that make skin testing impractical. Because in vitro testing is a laboratory service, it follows laboratory billing conventions rather than the per-allergen in-office testing units described above, and a practice running both pathways needs separate documentation logic for each rather than treating them as interchangeable line items on the same encounter.

Every allergy testing and immunotherapy LCD cited above and in the "what changed" section — both the outgoing and incoming versions, across all seven MACs — is broken down MAC by MAC, with what actually changed inside each policy's covered-diagnosis and frequency limits, in our allergy testing and immunotherapy billing guide.

Build-up versus maintenance phase changes the billing pattern for immunotherapy even though the underlying codes stay the same. During build-up, patients typically return frequently (often weekly) for escalating doses, generating a steady cadence of 95115/95117 claims alongside periodic 95165 antigen preparation billing as new vials are compounded. Once maintenance is reached, visit frequency typically drops to every few weeks, and the antigen preparation cadence slows correspondingly — a sudden mismatch between injection-visit frequency and antigen-preparation billing (many 95115/95117 claims with no corresponding 95165 activity, or vice versa) is a pattern worth reconciling internally before a payer's own utilization review flags it first.

Worked unit-counting example: a patient undergoes percutaneous (prick) testing to 40 allergens and intradermal testing to 12 allergens in the same visit. The prick panel is billed under 95004 at 40 units, one unit per allergen tested; the intradermal panel is billed under the appropriate intradermal code at 12 units. On the immunotherapy side, a multi-dose treatment vial that yields 12 usable 1 cc aliquots is still capped at 10 billable units under 95165 for a Medicare claim — the extra two aliquots exist clinically but aren't separately reimbursable, and billing all 12 is the overpayment pattern payers flag on post-payment review. Diagnosis coding on the testing and immunotherapy claim should reflect the specific allergic condition being managed — J30.1 (pollen), J30.5 (food), J30.81 (animal dander), or the applicable code from the family — rather than the unspecified J30.9, wherever the chart supports the more specific trigger.

Ear procedures: tympanostomy tubes, myringotomy, and cerumen removal

The 69433-versus-69436 distinction is the single highest-volume coding decision in general ENT, and it turns entirely on anesthesia depth, documented explicitly.

  • 1Anesthesia type, stated explicitly. "General anesthesia" or "local/topical anesthesia," named directly — not inferred from a medication list.
  • 2Laterality. Which ear(s) had tubes placed, matching what's billed and what any facility or anesthesia claim independently documents.
  • 3Indication. The specific reason for tube placement — recurrent acute otitis media with a documented episode count, chronic effusion with a documented duration, or a hearing-loss indication — tied to the ICD-10 code billed.
  • 4Setting. Office versus OR/ASC, consistent with the anesthesia type documented and the code selected.
69433
  • Tympanostomy with local or topical anesthesia — the office-based procedure.
  • Note should reflect topical/local anesthetic used and the office setting; no anesthesia-provider documentation needed.
69436
  • Tympanostomy requiring general anesthesia — almost universally the pediatric OR case.
  • The operative note must explicitly state "general anesthesia" — sedation-drug lists alone don't establish it. The anesthesiologist or CRNA bills their own anesthesia code separately; 69436 doesn't include it.

Bilateral billing on either code follows the true-bilateral rule: both ears are a genuinely paired structure, so modifier 50 is appropriate when tubes are placed in both ears in the same session, subject to the specific payer's preference for 50 versus two lines with LT/RT — both patterns exist across payers, and defaulting to the wrong one for a given payer is a common source of a unit-cap or duplicate denial rather than an outright rejection.

Cerumen removal has its own bundling trap. 69210 (removal of impacted cerumen requiring instrumentation, unilateral) is billable only when the documentation supports genuine impaction requiring instrumentation — irrigation or a simple cerumen sweep performed as an incidental part of an exam is bundled into the E/M, not separately billable under 69210. The documentation has to establish three things: that the cerumen was genuinely impacted (not just present), that instrumentation (curette, suction, or similar) was actually used rather than irrigation alone, and, when billed alongside an E/M, that the removal was either the sole reason for the visit or a significant, separately identifiable service beyond the E/M's own scope. A note that says "cerumen removed" without describing impaction or the instrument used is the single most common reason 69210 gets bundled back into the E/M on review. Diagnosis specificity matters here too: impacted cerumen is coded with laterality using H61.21 (right), H61.22 (left), or H61.23 (bilateral) rather than the unspecified-ear H61.20 wherever the exam actually documents which ear, and 69210 itself is reported per ear (append 50 or bill bilaterally per payer convention when both ears are genuinely impacted and instrumented).

Global period traps around tube placement mostly run the other direction from what practices assume: tympanostomy tube placement carries a short (typically 0-day) global period, meaning the routine post-op tube check is separately billable as its own E/M, not bundled the way a 90-day major-surgery follow-up would be. Practices that assume every post-surgical visit is non-billable leave that revenue on the table; the actual trap is failing to document a distinct reason for the visit (tube patency check, hearing recheck) rather than billing it as bundled follow-up by habit.

Pediatric tube placement carries an operational wrinkle worth building into the billing workflow even though it's not a coding decision by itself: because 69436 requires general anesthesia and the vast majority of pediatric tube placements are done exactly that way, the anesthesia claim, the facility claim (when performed at an ASC or hospital), and the surgeon's professional claim for 69436 all have to reconcile on date of service and laterality. A mismatch between what the anesthesia record documents and what the surgical operative note documents — different stated laterality, a facility claim showing a different procedure code than what the surgeon billed — is a common source of a payer coordination-of-benefits delay even when each individual claim is internally correct.

Nasal and sinus procedures: septoplasty, endoscopy, and balloon sinuplasty

This is the densest coding territory in the specialty, and where the biggest revenue is won or lost on documentation and bundling logic.

Septoplasty medical necessity

30520 (septoplasty) denies on medical necessity more than almost any other elective ENT procedure, because payers scrutinize the functional-versus-cosmetic line hard. The chart needs a documented functional obstruction on exam — ideally graded and side-specific — tied to the patient's actual symptoms (chronic mouth breathing, sleep disruption, recurrent sinusitis linked to the anatomic obstruction), and typically a record that conservative management was tried first and failed. Nasal endoscopy findings that visually corroborate the septal deviation strengthen the case further. A chart that notes "deviated septum" on exam without linking it to a functional complaint, or one that reads primarily as an appearance concern, is the exact profile that gets denied as cosmetic.

  • 1Graded, side-specific obstruction on exam. "Nasal obstruction" alone is weaker than "70% obstruction of the left nasal airway secondary to septal deviation," documented with an exam technique (anterior rhinoscopy, nasal endoscopy) named in the note.
  • 2Symptom correlation. The functional complaints driving the visit — mouth breathing, disrupted sleep, exercise intolerance, recurrent sinus infections — documented as connected to the anatomic finding, not listed as an unrelated review-of-systems item.
  • 3Conservative management trial and outcome. What was tried (nasal steroid spray, antihistamine, a defined trial period) and why it didn't resolve the obstruction, not just "failed medical therapy" as an unsupported one-line statement.
  • 4ICD-10 linkage. J34.2 (deviated nasal septum) correctly linked to the 30520 line on the claim form — a mismatched or missing diagnosis-to-procedure link on the claim itself, separate from the clinical documentation being sound, is its own frequent cause of denial.

CT imaging documentation deserves its own mention because it does double duty in sinus billing: a CT report showing genuine sinus disease (mucosal thickening, opacification, anatomic obstruction specific to the sinus being treated) both supports the surgical decision clinically and is frequently a named prior-authorization requirement in its own right, particularly for balloon sinuplasty. A CT report that's stale relative to the date of surgery, or that doesn't specifically address the sinus the operative plan targets, weakens the medical-necessity case even when the surgery itself is clinically appropriate — confirm the imaging in the chart is current and specific to the planned procedure before scheduling, not just present somewhere in the record.

Diagnostic versus surgical endoscopy

31231 (diagnostic nasal endoscopy) and 31237 (nasal/sinus endoscopy, surgical, with biopsy, polyp removal, or debridement) are frequently performed in the same visit — scope to look, then treat what's found — and NCCI generally bundles the diagnostic look into the surgical code when both address the same side and the same problem in the same session. It becomes separately billable, with modifier 59 or the more specific XS, only when the diagnostic endoscopy documents a genuinely separate site or an unrelated problem, not simply because the physician looked before acting.

Balloon sinuplasty: the code pairs that bundle, and the unbundle path

31295 (maxillary), 31296 (frontal), and 31297 (sphenoid) balloon dilation, plus 31298 (multiple sinuses), bundle into their corresponding FESS code when both are performed on the same sinus in the same session — industry coding guidance confirms 31295 is treated as a component of 31256 and 31267, and 31296 is not separately reported alongside 31276 for the same sinus, because the balloon is functioning as an access tool for the surgical work rather than as its own standalone procedure. When a balloon is used purely to establish a pathway before tissue is removed in the same sinus, the surgical FESS code alone is reported and the dilation isn't billed on top of it.

The unbundle path exists, and it's exactly what none of the competitor guides we reviewed spell out: when FESS is performed on one sinus and balloon dilation alone (no tissue removal) is performed on a genuinely different sinus or the contralateral side, both are separately billable, with LT/RT on each line documenting which side each code applies to. That contralateral-side documentation is what turns a bundled pair into two legitimate lines — it has to be explicit in the operative note, not inferred from the code selection.

Worked scenario: a patient undergoes right-sided FESS with maxillary antrostomy and tissue removal (31267-RT) plus balloon dilation of the left frontal sinus alone, with no tissue removal on that side (31296-LT). Both lines are billable, because the FESS and the balloon dilation were performed on opposite sides — the LT/RT split is what documents that these were not the same bundled event. Contrast that with a claim reporting 31267 and 31295 both unmodified for the same right maxillary sinus in the same session: that's the bundled pair, and the balloon line denies as a component of the FESS code unless the record independently shows a genuinely separate sinus.

31298 covers balloon dilation of multiple sinuses (frontal and sphenoid, via a single transnasal approach) when performed as the sole procedure without tissue removal, and follows the same same-sinus bundling logic against FESS as 31295–31297 individually — each sinus dilated within the combined code still has to be checked against whether FESS was also performed on that specific sinus in the same session.

Hypoglossal nerve stimulation implant coding

Sleep apnea surgical management runs a spectrum from tonsillectomy and UPPP through to implanted hypoglossal nerve stimulation, and this is the fast-growing, prior-auth-heavy revenue line the introduction flagged. The pattern is consistent across all four families: side and sub-type, not just the broad diagnosis category, is what a payer's coverage or medical-necessity check screens against. Diagnosis-to-procedure linkage is worth checking as its own step, separate from whether the diagnosis code itself is specific enough. A claim can carry a perfectly specific diagnosis — J32.1, chronic frontal sinusitis — and still deny if that code isn't the one actually linked to the corresponding procedure line (31276, frontal sinusotomy) on the claim form, particularly on multi-line claims covering several procedures with several different supporting diagnoses. Practices running high procedure-line-count claims (a combined FESS-plus-balloon-plus-septoplasty case, for instance) should treat diagnosis-to-line linkage as its own quality-control step before submission, not an assumption that the clearinghouse or scrubber will catch a mismatch automatically.

Every diagnosis family covered in this section shares the same underlying fix, which is worth stating plainly rather than leaving implicit across five separate examples: build the specificity prompt into the point of documentation, not into a downstream coding review. A physician documenting laterality, sub-type, and acuity in real time during the encounter produces a chart a coder can translate into the correct specific code without guesswork; a coder trying to infer laterality or chronicity from a thin note after the fact, working from a template that never prompted for it, is reconstructing information that should have been captured once, correctly, at the source.

Acute versus chronic isn't a formality — it changes what a claim can support. ENT's two highest-volume diagnosis families each carry a clean acute/chronic split, and defaulting to the wrong side of it undermines the claim even when the specific code selected is technically valid. Acute sinusitis (the J01 family) supports an episodic office visit and short-course treatment; chronic sinusitis (J32.0–J32.9) is what supports repeat imaging, a surgical referral, or FESS, because payer coverage policy for those escalations is generally written around a chronic, persistent, or recurrent condition, not a single acute episode. Coding a patient's first sinusitis visit as chronic to pre-justify an anticipated referral, before the duration and recurrence criteria are actually met, is a documentation-integrity problem that surfaces on audit even if it never trips a same-day denial. The same logic applies to acute versus chronic otitis media (H65/H66 acute versus chronic variants) — a tympanostomy tube referral needs the chart to actually show the recurrent or chronic pattern (a specific count of documented episodes within a defined period, or persistent effusion over a stated duration) that payer policy for tube placement typically expects, not just a diagnosis code that happens to say "chronic."

G47.33 and the sleep-study linkage payers actually expect. The OSA diagnosis code itself isn't the documentation gap — G47.33 is specific and billable on its own. What trips claims for tonsillectomy, UPPP, and especially hypoglossal nerve stimulation is the chart failing to reference the sleep study that establishes the diagnosis: the study date, the diagnostic AHI or RDI value, and how that value maps to the severity criteria the payer or MAC's coverage policy requires. A claim carrying G47.33 with no referenced study, or a study result that doesn't clear the payer's stated threshold, is functionally an unsupported diagnosis even though the code itself is correctly selected.

The "other specified" trap, both directions. Codes like J34.89 (other specified disorders of the nose and nasal sinuses) exist precisely because ICD-10 can't name every clinical finding, and they're genuinely billable when the chart documents a specific finding that doesn't map to one of the named categories — nasal septal perforation without a more specific code, for instance, or a specific structural finding the physician has clearly characterized. The trap runs in both directions: defaulting to J34.89 (or J34.9, its unspecified sibling) as a habitual first choice instead of checking whether a more specific named code already fits is the wrong direction; but avoiding "other specified" codes altogether out of a mistaken belief that they always read as vague is the opposite error, since a well-documented other-specified code with a clear clinical description in the note is a legitimate, billable choice a payer has no basis to deny on specificity grounds alone.

Worked 59/XS scenario: a patient presents with a suspicious lesion on the external ear and separately reports nasal obstruction under evaluation for possible FESS. Worked 78 scenario: a patient develops persistent frontal sinus obstruction six weeks after an index FESS procedure, still within the FESS code's global period, and returns to the OR for a revision frontal sinusotomy addressing the same disease process. Billing the revision without modifier 78 at all, as if it were an unrelated first-time procedure, both misrepresents the clinical situation and typically overstates the payable amount.

NCCI edits, MUEs, and bundling

This is the section every competitor guide treats as a one-line reminder to "check NCCI edits before billing." None of them publish the actual code pairs. Here's what we found, with the caveat on indicator values stated plainly.

Balloon dilation versus FESS — the pairing that costs the most when it's wrong. Industry coding guidance (AAPC-sourced, cross-checked against multiple ENT billing references) consistently describes 31295 (maxillary balloon dilation) as bundled into 31256 and 31267 when performed on the same sinus in the same session, and 31296 (frontal balloon dilation) as not separately reportable alongside 31276 for the same sinus. The unbundle path is the contralateral-side or different-sinus exception described in the nasal and sinus procedures section above: perform FESS on one sinus and balloon dilation alone on a different sinus or the opposite side, document it explicitly, and append LT/RT to each line.

Diagnostic-versus-surgical nasal/sinus endoscopy. The same logic that governs balloon-versus-FESS governs 31231-versus-31237: performed on the same side for the same clinical problem in the same session, the diagnostic look is part of the surgical work-up and isn't separately payable. The distinguishing question for whether 59/XS legitimately applies is genuinely anatomic or clinical separation, not sequence — "the physician looked first, then treated" describes the normal internal workflow of a single surgical encounter, not two distinct services, regardless of which code technically got entered first on the claim.

Audiometry component bundling. As covered above, 92540 bundles its four component tests (92541, 92542, 92544, 92545); billing the comprehensive code alongside any individual component for the same date is an unbundling error, not a legitimate itemization of a bundled service.

The "comprehensive sinus surgery" unbundling mistake, named specifically. Where competitor guides say "don't unbundle sinus surgery" without naming the pattern, the actual mistake we see most often is one of two things: billing overlapping ethmoidectomy-family codes (31254 alongside 31255, or either alongside 31256/31267 as if maxillary work were double-counted within the ethmoid codes) for what was actually one continuous procedure on one sinus system, or billing diagnostic nasal endoscopy (31231) separately from surgical endoscopy (31237) on the same side for the same problem without a documented distinct site.

Understanding why some of these pairs can be split with a modifier and others can't requires the same two-value framework every specialty's bundling logic runs on, even though CMS's specific values for ENT pairs couldn't be confirmed directly in this build:

  • 0NCCI modifier indicator 0 — never bypassable. No modifier, however well-documented, overrides an edit carrying this indicator. If a balloon-dilation/FESS pair or a diagnostic/surgical endoscopy pair carries a 0, the second code simply isn't separately payable on that sinus or that side, full stop — the only path to billing both is a genuinely different anatomic site.
  • 1NCCI modifier indicator 1 — bypassable with documentation. An NCCI-associated modifier (59 or the specific X-modifier) can override the edit, but only where the operative note actually shows the second service was distinct — separate sinus, separate side, separate structure.
  • ?MUE and the MUE Adjudication Indicator (MAI). Separately from the pairing edits, individual codes carry a per-day unit cap (the MUE) and an indicator (MAI 1, 2, or 3) that determines whether exceeding it is appealable at all — MAI 1 is a claim-line edit units can sometimes be split across, MAI 2 is treated as clinically implausible with no appeal path, and MAI 3 is appealable with documentation. This is the framework to check before spending staff time on an allergy-testing or immunotherapy unit-cap appeal.

Worked MUE-versus-appeal walkthrough. A practice bills 12 units of 95165 against a single multi-dose immunotherapy vial and the claim denies above unit 10. Before drafting anything, the first question is whether this is even an MUE the payer allows to be contested at all — Medicare's own stated cap is 10 doses per vial regardless of actual yield, which functions closer to a hard policy limit than a clinically-implausible-unit edit that might otherwise be appealable with documentation. In that scenario, the correct response isn't an appeal arguing the extra two aliquots were real (they likely were) — it's adjusting the billing practice going forward to cap submitted units at 10 per vial prepared, since arguing against a stated policy cap wastes staff time a genuine MAI-3-style appeal wouldn't. Contrast that with a denial on a code where the practice can't tell from the remittance advice alone whether the cap is a hard policy limit or an appealable clinical-implausibility edit — that's exactly the case for confirming the MAI before committing staff time, rather than assuming every unit-cap denial is either always appealable or never worth trying.

Pairing the denial reason with the actual appeal argument — not just "submit documentation" — is the difference between this table and what else is published on ENT denials. Pro tip

Before writing any medical-necessity appeal for septoplasty or hypoglossal nerve stimulation, confirm which version of the payer's or MAC's coverage policy was in effect on the date of service — several of the LCDs cited in this guide were mid-revision in 2025 and 2026, and citing the wrong version number in an appeal undermines the argument even when the underlying clinical case is sound.

Appeal timelines matter as much as appeal content, and they differ by payer type. For traditional Medicare, the first-level redetermination request generally has to be filed within 120 days of the remittance advice date, though filing sooner rather than at the deadline preserves more of the underlying documentation trail while it's still fresh. Commercial payers set their own timely-filing windows for appeals, typically stated in the provider contract or the denial notice itself, and they vary meaningfully by payer — a practice tracking ENT denials without a payer-specific appeal-deadline calendar risks losing a winnable appeal purely to a missed filing window, independent of whether the underlying clinical argument was sound.

A usable appeal for a medical-necessity denial follows a consistent structure regardless of which procedure triggered it: name the specific policy and document ID the payer is applying (the LCD number and MAC, or the commercial plan's clinical policy bulletin number), state plainly which of that policy's specific criteria the chart supports, and attach the exact chart excerpts — not a summary — that establish each one. A septoplasty appeal that cites "Palmetto GBA's coverage policy" without a document ID reads as unresearched; a septoplasty appeal that names the graded obstruction percentage, the specific symptoms documented, the conservative-therapy trial dates, and includes the actual exam note reads as a claim the payer's reviewer can approve without further back-and-forth. The same structure applies to an HGNS appeal: name the specific MAC's current LCD, quote its AHI/RDI range and BMI cutoff, and attach the sleep study report itself rather than a physician's summary of it.

Payer-specific nuances and prior authorization

"Check with your MAC" is close to useless without naming which policy and which version. Two service lines carry the heaviest MAC variance in ENT: allergy testing/immunotherapy and hypoglossal nerve stimulation, both detailed with full document IDs above.

Commercial payers layer their own medical policy on top of, and sometimes independently of, whatever the applicable MAC's LCD says — a commercial plan's own published clinical policy bulletin for balloon sinuplasty, septoplasty, or HGNS is the operative document for that plan's members, not the Medicare LCD, even when a practice's workflow was originally built around Medicare's coverage language. The practical implication is that a single practice serving a mixed payer panel may need three or four different documentation checklists live simultaneously for the identical procedure, keyed to the specific payer rather than a single "our septoplasty checklist" applied uniformly. Building the payer-specific variance into the scheduling and prior-auth workflow up front is materially cheaper than discovering it at the denial stage, procedure by procedure.

Prior authorization friction concentrates in three procedures:

  • 1Balloon sinuplasty. Commercial payers frequently require documented failure of medical management (nasal steroids, saline irrigation, antibiotics where appropriate) and imaging confirming sinus disease before authorizing, mirroring the functional-necessity standard septoplasty faces.
  • 2Septoplasty. As detailed above — graded functional obstruction, symptom correlation, and typically a documented conservative-management trial, whether or not the specific payer requires formal prior authorization.
  • 3Hypoglossal nerve stimulation. The heaviest prior-auth burden in the specialty: commercial payers and Medicare LCDs alike typically require a diagnostic sleep study with a specific AHI/RDI range, a BMI cutoff (implants are generally not indicated above a threshold BMI), and documented failure of or intolerance to CPAP therapy. All three thresholds vary by payer and, on the Medicare side, by which of the seven MAC-specific LCDs applies — confirm the current version for your MAC before submitting.

Medicare Advantage plans are not required to follow traditional Medicare's local coverage determinations, and frequently apply their own, sometimes stricter, medical-necessity criteria on top of or instead of the MAC's LCD — treat the LCD as the traditional-Medicare baseline and confirm the specific MA plan's own policy separately rather than assuming LCD compliance guarantees MA coverage. This matters most for HGNS and balloon sinuplasty specifically, where MA plans commonly layer their own step-therapy and imaging requirements on top of whatever the underlying MAC's LCD already specifies, and a prior-auth packet built only around the LCD's criteria can still come back denied for missing an MA-specific requirement the LCD never mentioned.

Taken together, the payer variance described throughout this guide — MAC-specific LCDs on allergy testing and HGNS, commercial-specific clinical policy bulletins on balloon sinuplasty and septoplasty, and Medicare Advantage's own layer on top of both — is why a single, generic "our ENT prior-authorization checklist" rarely holds up across a mixed payer panel. The practices that keep denial rates lowest on these procedures maintain payer-specific documentation checklists, refreshed against the current policy version at least annually and whenever a MAC-specific LCD update lands, rather than a single static reference document.

Frequently asked questions

What's the actual difference between CPT 69433 and 69436 for tympanostomy tubes?

Anesthesia depth, not location. 69433 is tympanostomy with local or topical anesthesia, typically an office procedure. 69436 requires general anesthesia, which is why it's billed for the OR cases common in young children. The operative note has to say "general anesthesia" explicitly for 69436 to hold up — a note that only lists sedation medications without stating the anesthesia type is the single most common reason this code gets downcoded or denied on audit. The anesthesiologist or CRNA bills their own anesthesia code separately; 69436 itself doesn't include it.

When do we use modifier 50 versus LT/RT on ENT procedures?

Modifier 50 is for a genuinely paired, bilateral organ or structure treated on both sides in the same session — both ears for tympanostomy tubes, both maxillary sinuses for balloon dilation. LT and RT identify a single side and are used when a payer's claims system prices bilateral work as two separate lines rather than one bilaterally-adjusted line, or when only one side was actually treated. The trap is appending 50 to a midline structure: septoplasty (30520) is not a paired organ, so modifier 50 doesn't apply to it no matter how the payer's edits happen to allow it through — that's a documented overpayment finding waiting to happen, not a legitimate bilateral claim.

Can balloon sinuplasty be billed with functional endoscopic sinus surgery (FESS) on the same visit?

Only on a different sinus, or a different side. NCCI bundles the balloon dilation codes (31295–31297) into the corresponding FESS code when both are performed on the same sinus in the same session, because the balloon dilation is considered part of accessing that sinus surgically — 31295 is a component of 31256 and 31267, for example. If FESS is performed on one sinus and balloon dilation alone (no tissue removal) on a genuinely separate sinus or the contralateral side, both are billable, and LT/RT on each line documents the distinction for the payer. Billing both on the identical sinus, same side, same session, is the unbundling error that draws the CO-97 denial.

What documentation actually supports medical necessity for septoplasty (30520)?

Payers are checking for a functional, not cosmetic, rationale. The chart needs a documented nasal obstruction on physical exam — ideally graded and side-specific — correlated with the patient's symptoms (mouth breathing, sleep disruption, recurrent sinusitis), and usually a record that conservative management (nasal steroids, antihistamines, a trial period) was tried and didn't resolve it. Nasal endoscopy findings supporting the septal deviation strengthen the claim further. A chart that documents a deviated septum on exam but never ties it to a functional complaint, or that reads primarily as an appearance concern, is the profile that gets denied as cosmetic and non-covered.

How are allergen immunotherapy units counted for CPT 95165?

One unit equals one 1 cc aliquot drawn from a multi-dose vial, not one injection and not one vial. Medicare caps a single vial at 10 billable doses regardless of how many 1 cc aliquots are actually obtainable from it, so billing more than 10 units per vial prepared is a frequent audit finding even when the extraction count is technically accurate. 95165 covers the antigen preparation and supervision only — the actual injection administration is billed separately under 95115 (single injection) or 95117 (two or more injections), and both halves have to appear on the claim for a complete immunotherapy visit.

What changed with the CGS allergy immunotherapy LCD, and does it affect other MACs?

CGS Administrators retired its long-standing Allergy Immunotherapy LCD (L32553) on 10/25/2025 and replaced it the next day with a retitled policy, Allergen Immunotherapy (AIT) with Subcutaneous Immunotherapy (SCIT), L40056, effective 10/26/2025. The same retitling and republication happened at Palmetto GBA (L40046, 10/26/2025), WPS (L36408, updated in place, 10/26/2025), and Noridian (L40050, 11/06/2025, after a brief interim version). Wellpoint Federal's version followed on 04/01/2026 as L40048. First Coast and Novitas have not retitled their immunotherapy LCDs as of this guide — they're still active under the older "Allergen Immunotherapy" title. Confirm which naming convention and document ID is current for your MAC before citing it in an appeal or a prior-auth request.

Is diagnostic nasal endoscopy (31231) billable the same day as surgical nasal/sinus endoscopy (31237)?

Generally no, when they're performed on the same side for the same problem — the diagnostic scope is considered part of the pre-surgical work-up bundled into the surgical code. It becomes separately billable, with modifier 59 or the more specific XS, only when the diagnostic endoscopy addresses a genuinely distinct site or a separate, unrelated problem documented at the time of the encounter, not simply because the physician looked before cutting. Routinely appending 59 to 31231 whenever it's billed alongside 31237 without that separate-site documentation is one of the more heavily scrutinized modifier patterns in ENT billing.

Which Medicare LCDs govern hypoglossal nerve stimulation coverage, and do they differ by MAC?

Yes, materially. Seven MACs each publish their own Hypoglossal Nerve Stimulation for Obstructive Sleep Apnea LCD, and both the document IDs and the currency of the coverage criteria differ: CGS (L38307), WPS (L38528), Palmetto GBA (L38276), and Wellpoint Federal (L38387) all received substantive 2026 updates, with effective dates between March and May 2026. Noridian's LCD (L38310) was reissued 10/16/2025, replacing a prior version. Novitas (L38385) and First Coast (L38398) are both still running on coverage criteria dated back to 03/15/2020 as of this guide, with no 2026 refresh yet. Pull your practice's specific MAC's current LCD before building a prior-authorization packet — AHI thresholds, BMI cutoffs, and required failed-therapy documentation are not uniform across all seven.
