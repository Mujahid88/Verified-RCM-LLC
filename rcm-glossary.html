The medical billing and RCM glossary: 57 terms, explained the way you'll actually hear them.

Revenue cycle management runs on its own vocabulary, and a payer remittance or a denial letter uses that vocabulary without ever pausing to explain it. This is a working reference for the terms that come up most, grouped by where they show up in the billing cycle rather than alphabetically, since that is how you actually encounter them. Where a term has its own deeper guide on this site, we've linked it.

Claims and submission

Clean claim
A claim with no defect, error or missing information that would prevent it from being processed on first submission. The percentage of claims that go out clean the first time is one of the best leading indicators of revenue cycle health, since every claim that isn't clean means extra staff time and a slower payment.
Claim scrubbing
Automated pre-submission checks that catch structural, coding and payer-specific errors before a claim leaves your system, so the fix happens before submission instead of after a denial. See our claims scrubbing guide.
Rejection
A claim refused before it enters adjudication, usually for a structural or formatting reason such as a mismatched patient ID or invalid NPI. Rejections can be corrected and resubmitted freely and are not appeals, but a high rejection rate almost always points to a fixable problem in how claims are built, not the payer.
Clearinghouse
An intermediary that translates and routes electronic claims between a practice's billing system and each payer, validating formatting along the way, and returns rejection and acknowledgment reports. Most rejections are caught here, before the payer ever sees the claim, which is why clearinghouse reports are worth checking daily.
270/271 eligibility transaction
The HIPAA-standard eligibility check: your system sends a 270 inquiry, the payer returns a 271 response with active coverage, plan type and remaining benefits. When a front-desk eligibility check fails silently, it's usually because the 271 came back with a status the software didn't surface clearly, not because the check didn't run. See our eligibility verification guide.
837P / 837I
The HIPAA-standard electronic claim transactions: 837P for professional claims (CMS-1500), 837I for institutional claims (UB-04). Nearly every claim submitted electronically today travels as one of these two formats, whether or not the biller ever sees the raw file. See our CMS-1500 vs UB-04 guide.
Timely filing limit
The payer-set deadline, measured from the date of service, after which a claim will not be accepted regardless of accuracy. It varies by payer, commonly 90 days to a year, and missing it is one of the few denial reasons that usually cannot be appealed.
Superbill
An itemized form documenting a patient encounter, including diagnosis and procedure codes, used as the source document for claim creation. In an EHR-integrated workflow it's often generated automatically from the note, but any gap between what was documented and what made it onto the superbill is a charge that never gets billed.
Charge capture
The process of recording every billable service performed during an encounter before it is coded and submitted; a missed charge here is revenue that never gets billed at all, not a claim that gets denied. It's the easiest category of revenue loss to overlook, because unlike a denial, a missed charge never generates a form telling you it happened.

Coding

CPT code
Current Procedural Terminology: the code set describing procedures and services performed by a provider, maintained and updated annually by the American Medical Association. Payment rules and most denial logic are built around the specific CPT code billed, which is why an imprecise-but-defensible code still costs money in edits and appeals.
ICD-10-CM
The code set describing diagnoses and reasons for a visit; it justifies why a procedure was medically necessary. A CPT code paired with an ICD-10 code that doesn't support medical necessity under the payer's coverage policy is one of the most common, and most preventable, causes of a denial.
HCPCS Level II
A code set covering supplies, equipment, drugs and services not included in CPT, such as durable medical equipment, injectable drugs and ambulance services. It updates on its own schedule, separate from CPT, and is easy to overlook if a coding workflow was built around CPT alone.
Modifier
A two-character code appended to a CPT or HCPCS code to indicate a service was altered in some way without changing its core definition, for example performed bilaterally or as a distinct procedure on the same day as another. Modifiers 25 and 59 are among the most frequently misused, and both draw disproportionate payer audit attention.
Global period
The fixed number of days after a surgical or procedural CPT code during which related follow-up care is bundled into the original payment rather than billed separately. Billing an unrelated E/M visit during a global period without modifier 24 or 25 is a common reason a legitimate charge gets denied as included.
NCCI edit
A National Correct Coding Initiative rule identifying code pairs that should not normally be billed together for the same patient on the same date, because one is considered part of the other. An edit can sometimes be overridden with the correct modifier when the services were genuinely distinct, but appending one just to force payment is exactly what audits are built to catch.
MUE (Medically Unlikely Edit)
The maximum number of units of a given code that would be reported for one patient on one date of service under most circumstances. Billing above the MUE threshold doesn't just risk denial of the excess units; in some cases it flags the entire claim line for review.
RVU
Relative Value Unit: the unit Medicare uses to measure the resources a service requires, combined across work, practice expense and malpractice components and multiplied by a conversion factor to calculate payment. Most commercial fee schedules are still built as a percentage of the Medicare RVU-based rate, so RVU changes ripple well beyond Medicare claims alone.
Bundling / unbundling
Bundling is billing a group of related services under one comprehensive code; unbundling, billing the components separately when a bundled code applies, is a common cause of denial and audit exposure. NCCI edits exist specifically to catch unbundling, whether it was intentional or not.
HCC coding
Hierarchical Condition Category coding: risk-adjustment coding used mainly for Medicare Advantage, where diagnosis coding accuracy affects capitated payment rather than a per-claim payment. Under HCC, an undercoded chronic condition doesn't just miss one claim's worth of revenue, it can lower the risk-adjusted payment for that patient for the entire year.

Medical necessity and coverage policy

MAC (Medicare Administrative Contractor)
A private contractor Medicare pays to process claims, issue coverage policies and handle enrollment for a defined multi-state jurisdiction. Your MAC, not Medicare directly, is who actually adjudicates the claim and who you appeal to first.
LCD (Local Coverage Determination)
A coverage policy issued by your MAC defining when a service is considered medically necessary in that jurisdiction, including which ICD-10 codes support it. Two practices in different MAC jurisdictions can bill the identical CPT code and get different medical-necessity outcomes because their LCDs differ.
NCD (National Coverage Determination)
A Medicare coverage policy issued nationally by CMS, which every MAC must follow without variation. Where no NCD exists for a service, the MAC's own LCD fills the gap, which is why coverage for the same code can vary by state.

Payment and adjustments

Adjudication
The payer's internal process of evaluating a submitted claim against the patient's coverage, the provider's contract and its own coverage policies to decide whether, and how much, to pay. A claim can be adjudicated to a full payment, a partial payment with a contractual adjustment, or a full denial; the ERA is simply the output of that decision.
EOB
Explanation of Benefits: the payer's summary sent to the patient (and often the provider) showing what was billed, what was covered and what the patient owes. It is a patient-facing document; the provider's operational equivalent, built for posting rather than reading, is the ERA.
ERA (835)
Electronic Remittance Advice: the electronic equivalent of an EOB sent to the provider, in the standard 835 transaction format, used to auto-post payments. Posting manually from a printed EOB instead of an electronic 835 is avoidable work and more prone to posting errors.
Allowed amount
The maximum a payer will pay for a covered service under its contract with the provider, regardless of the amount actually billed. The gap between billed charge and allowed amount can look alarming on a statement, but for an in-network claim it is expected and contractual, not a sign anything went wrong.
Contractual adjustment
The difference between the billed charge and the allowed amount, which the provider agreed to write off as a condition of being in-network. It should reconcile exactly against the fee schedule in your payer contract; if it consistently doesn't, that's worth escalating as a contract or configuration issue, not writing off as normal.
Coordination of benefits (COB)
The process of determining which payer is primary when a patient has more than one insurance plan, so claims are billed in the correct order. Billing the secondary payer first is a common, entirely avoidable denial reason that COB verification at check-in is meant to prevent.
Capitation
A payment model where a provider receives a fixed amount per patient per period regardless of how many services that patient uses, shifting utilization risk to the provider. Under capitation, accurate diagnosis coding through HCC matters more than procedure coding, since it drives the risk-adjusted rate rather than any individual claim.
Fee-for-service
A payment model where the provider is paid for each individual service rendered, the more common alternative to capitation. Most of the denial, coding and appeal mechanics on this page assume fee-for-service, since that's where a per-claim decision actually happens.
Coinsurance
A percentage of the allowed amount the patient owes after the deductible is met, splitting further cost with the plan. Because it's a percentage rather than a flat amount, it varies with each claim, which is part of why patient balances are harder to predict than plan summaries make them sound.
Copay
A fixed dollar amount a patient owes per visit or service, set by the plan and due regardless of where the deductible stands. It's the easiest patient cost-share to collect at time of service because the amount is already known before the visit ends.
Deductible
The amount a patient must pay out of pocket before the plan starts sharing costs at all. A claim applied entirely to an unmet deductible still pays a normal contractual amount, it's simply owed by the patient instead of the payer, which is why deductible season tends to raise patient A/R even when payer performance hasn't changed.

Denials and accounts receivable

Denial
A claim that entered adjudication and was refused payment, in whole or in part, for a specific reason the payer must state. Unlike a rejection, a denial requires a formal appeal within a set window; treating a denial like a simple resubmission wastes that window instead of using it.
CARC (Claim Adjustment Reason Code)
A standardized code on a remittance explaining why a payment was reduced or denied, such as CO-50 for services not deemed medically necessary. The CARC is the first thing to read on a denial, since it determines whether you're appealing, correcting and resubmitting, or writing the balance off. See our denials management guide.
RARC (Remittance Advice Remark Code)
A supplemental code that rides alongside a CARC to add detail the CARC alone doesn't convey, such as which specific document was missing. Reading the CARC without its paired RARC often leaves out the exact reason needed to fix and resubmit a claim correctly.
Appeal
A formal request asking a payer to reconsider a denial, typically requiring supporting documentation and submitted within the payer's specific deadline. Most payers allow multiple appeal levels, and the documentation bar usually rises at each level, so the first appeal is the one worth getting right.
Peer-to-peer review
A phone conversation between the treating provider and a payer's medical director, requested after a medical-necessity denial, to argue the clinical case directly rather than through paperwork alone. It's usually the fastest path to overturning a denial when the record supports the service but the payer's initial reviewer didn't see it that way. See our prior authorization guide.
Gold-carding
A payer policy that exempts a provider from prior authorization for specific services once their approval history shows a consistently high rate of medically appropriate requests. Not every payer offers it, and the ones that do usually revoke it if the approval rate drops, so it isn't a permanent exemption. See our prior authorization guide.
Days in A/R
The average number of days between billing a claim and receiving payment; a rising number signals a slowing revenue cycle before it shows up anywhere else. It's worth tracking separately by payer, since one slow payer can quietly drag the whole average without the rest of the book actually deteriorating.
Aging bucket
A grouping of outstanding claims by how long they have been unpaid, typically 0–30, 31–60, 61–90 and 90-plus days, used to prioritize collection follow-up. A healthy A/R report is weighted heavily toward the 0–30 bucket; a growing 90-plus bucket is usually the earliest visible sign of a collections process falling behind.
Claim adjustment group codes (CO, PR, OA, PI)
Codes on a remittance identifying who is responsible for an unpaid amount: CO is a contractual obligation the provider cannot bill the patient for, PR is patient responsibility, OA and PI cover other adjustment and payer-initiated categories. Billing a patient for a CO-coded amount is a compliance problem, not just a billing mistake.
Write-off
An amount removed from a patient or payer balance because it will not be collected, whether contractually required, deemed uncollectible, or approved as a courtesy adjustment. A rising small-balance write-off trend is worth watching even when each individual write-off looks trivial, since it usually points to a process gap rather than genuinely uncollectible accounts.

Credentialing and compliance

NPI
National Provider Identifier: a unique ten-digit number for individual providers (Type 1) or organizations (Type 2), required on every claim. Using the wrong type in the wrong claim field is a surprisingly common, and entirely avoidable, rejection cause.
EIN
Employer Identification Number: the federal tax ID for a practice as a business entity, distinct from any individual provider's NPI or Social Security number. Payer enrollment applications and CAQH both require it, and a mismatch between the EIN on file and the one on a current W-9 is a common, avoidable cause of enrollment delay.
CAQH ProView
A centralized credentialing data profile most commercial payers pull from rather than requesting documents individually; it must be kept current and re-attested on a schedule, typically every 120 days. An expired attestation can quietly stall a credentialing application or a revalidation with no obvious error message pointing back to CAQH as the cause. See our CAQH guide.
PECOS
The Medicare Provider Enrollment, Chain and Ownership System used to submit and manage Medicare enrollment applications. It's a separate system from CAQH, and being current in one does not mean anything about status in the other. See our Medicare credentialing guide.
Credentialing vs. enrollment
Credentialing is a payer verifying a provider's qualifications; enrollment is registering that credentialed provider to actually bill the payer. They are sequential, not interchangeable, and a provider can be fully credentialed and still unable to bill a specific payer if the enrollment step hasn't been finished.
Delegated credentialing
An arrangement where a payer authorizes an organization, often a hospital system or a large group, to credential its own providers using the payer's standards, instead of the payer credentialing each provider individually. It can shorten a new provider's time to in-network status considerably, but only if the delegated entity's own internal process is actually fast. See our credentialing overview.
Revalidation
A periodic requirement, common with Medicare and Medicaid, to reconfirm enrollment information on file; missing the deadline can deactivate billing privileges. Reactivating a deactivated enrollment is slower and more disruptive than simply filing the revalidation on time, which is why it's worth tracking deadlines proactively rather than waiting for a payer notice.
DEA registration
A federal registration required to prescribe, administer or dispense controlled substances, separate from state medical licensure and from payer credentialing. A lapsed DEA registration doesn't just block prescribing; it can also stall enrollment applications that ask for it as a supporting document. See our DEA registration guide.
CLIA certificate
A federal certificate required to perform even simple in-office lab testing, such as a rapid strep or urine dipstick, under the Clinical Laboratory Improvement Amendments. Billing a lab CPT code without an active CLIA certificate on file is a compliance exposure independent of whether the claim happens to pay. See our CLIA guide.
HIPAA
The federal law setting privacy, security and breach-notification requirements for protected health information, along with the transaction-standard rules (like the 837 and 835) that make electronic billing interoperable across payers. Most of what a billing team does daily, from EHR access controls to how remittance data is stored, sits inside a HIPAA requirement whether or not anyone names it that way. See our HIPAA compliance guide.
PHI
Protected Health Information: any individually identifiable health information tied to a patient, whether it's a chart note, a claim, or a remittance line item. It's the thing every BAA exists to govern, and it's why a billing vendor without a signed BAA is a compliance problem no matter how good their collection results are.
BAA
Business Associate Agreement: a HIPAA-required contract between a covered entity and any vendor that handles protected health information on its behalf. Signing one should happen before any PHI changes hands, not after a vendor relationship is already underway.

Practice technology

EHR
Electronic Health Record: the clinical documentation system a provider uses to chart the encounter; it is the primary source for the charge capture and coding that follow. How well an EHR is set up, templates, charge triggers, coding prompts, has a direct and often underestimated effect on how much revenue actually gets billed. See our EHR selection guide.
PM system
Practice Management system: the software that handles scheduling, registration, claim generation and payment posting, usually integrated with, but distinct from, the EHR. A weak PM-EHR integration is a common, invisible source of missed charges and coding delays, since data that doesn't flow automatically has to be re-entered by hand, and hand entry is where charges get dropped.
