Sleep medicine billing and coding: the complete 2026 guide.

Sleep medicine runs on a coding decision most other specialties never have to make: the same clinical service — an unattended home sleep test — is billed with an entirely different code set depending on whether Medicare or a commercial payer is on the other end of the claim, and getting that one decision wrong is one of the most common, most preventable denial patterns in the specialty. This guide covers the full revenue cycle: the code family from home testing through in-lab titration, the CPAP compliance window that decides whether a rental converts to a purchase, split-night criteria, ICD-10 specificity, and the denials nobody else in this niche explains with the actual MAC-by-MAC citations.

Reviewed by Phares A., MD · Physiotherapist, CPC (AAPC) · Updated September 2026

Key takeaways

  • The CPT-versus-G-code decision for home sleep testing is MAC-specific, not a single Medicare-wide rule. CGS Administrators' current local coverage article requires the most specific G-code (G0398/G0399/G0400) for a home study performed with a portable monitor, even though it also lists CPT 95800/95801/95806 as generally allowed — other MACs currently permit either code set. Most competitor guides flatten this into one universal rule and get it wrong in one direction or the other.
  • A home sleep apnea test cannot diagnose narcolepsy, REM sleep behavior disorder, or periodic limb movement disorder. None of the HSAT codes or channels record EEG, EOG, or EMG, so none of them can stage sleep or detect REM onset — all three diagnoses require attended, in-lab polysomnography, and narcolepsy specifically requires a same-day multiple sleep latency test immediately following it.
  • CPT 95800, 95801, and 95806 are scheduled for deletion effective January 1, 2027, replaced by a six-code family that splits technical and professional work by complexity tier. A charge master or scrubber rule that isn't updated before that date will start denying on invalid code, not medical necessity — and 2026 is the year to prepare for it.
  • CPAP compliance is a hard, calendar-driven coverage gate, not a soft goal. Continued coverage after the initial trial depends on documented adherence and a face-to-face reassessment — miss the window or the documentation, and the DME MAC's KX-modifier attestation becomes indefensible on audit, regardless of how the patient is actually doing clinically.

Sleep medicine billing at a glance

Four structural problems drive most of sleep medicine's denial volume: a home-versus-facility, Medicare-versus-commercial code selection that isn't standardized across Medicare Administrative Contractors; dense same-night bundling between diagnostic and titration studies; a durable medical equipment benefit with a hard compliance gate that most practices track manually if they track it at all; and diagnosis specificity that determines not just medical necessity but which testing modality is even clinically appropriate. What makes sleep medicine distinct from most other diagnostic specialties is that these four problems compound each other across two separate benefit categories — the testing claim and the DME claim — run by two sets of payer rules that don't always move in sync, sometimes billed by two entirely different organizations, and connected only by documentation that has to survive the handoff between them. Ordering, the clinical evaluation, and E/M coding around a sleep study

A sleep study doesn't start at the technologist hookup — it starts with a clinical evaluation that has to be documented well enough to support both the decision to test and, later, the specific test ordered. Medicare's CPAP national coverage determination requires that the qualifying sleep test "have been previously ordered by the beneficiary's treating physician and furnished under appropriate physician supervision," which puts the ordering encounter itself, not just the interpretation report, inside the compliance chain for everything that follows. A chart that jumps straight to "refer for sleep study" without documenting the reported symptoms, exam findings, and the clinical reasoning for the specific modality ordered (home test versus in-lab) is a weaker foundation for every claim downstream of it, including the DME claim that depends on the diagnosis the study establishes.

The office visit where that evaluation happens is billed using standard evaluation-and-management coding (new or established patient office visits, typically 99202–99215), leveled on either total time or medical decision making exactly as in any other specialty — sleep medicine doesn't get its own E/M rules. What does matter clinically is what the visit needs to establish to support what happens next: symptom pattern and duration, comorbidities relevant to testing-modality selection (see the prior authorization section below), prior treatment attempts, and, for a follow-up visit after CPAP initiation, the adherence conversation that eventually has to line up with the DME supplier's own compliance documentation. A growing share of sleep medicine follow-up happens by telehealth, particularly for straightforward CPAP adherence check-ins, and that visit is billed the same E/M codes with the appropriate telehealth place-of-service and modifier (commonly 95 for synchronous audio-video, subject to the specific payer's current telehealth policy) — the underlying leveling rules don't change, only the delivery modality and its own separate billing requirements do.

The Medicare code-selection trap: CPT versus HCPCS G-codes for home sleep testing

This is the single most consequential coding decision in sleep medicine, and it is genuinely more complicated than the flat rule most billing content repeats. The short version many practices operate on is: "Medicare requires G0398/G0399/G0400 for home sleep testing; CPT 95800/95801/95806 will deny." That was true, unambiguously, for years. It is no longer uniformly true, and treating it as a single Medicare-wide rule is itself a source of denials in both directions — some practices avoid CPT codes a MAC would actually accept, others use CPT codes a MAC will reject outright. We pulled the current billing-and-coding articles directly from five Medicare Administrative Contractors to see what each one actually says today.

The practical read: CGS is the clearest documented example of the trap still functioning exactly as described in industry lore — bill 95800/95801/95806 for a CGS-jurisdiction home study using a portable monitor, and current CGS guidance treats that as the wrong code family, not just a suboptimal choice. Noridian, once equally strict, updated its article following advocacy from the American Academy of Sleep Medicine and currently reads as permissive of either code set for a home study. WPS, Palmetto, and First Coast currently read as permissive as well, though none of them explicitly guarantee that a commercial-style CPT claim won't draw scrutiny from a claims-processing system tuned to the old rule.

What this means operationally: find your practice's Part B MAC (not just DME MAC) before defaulting to either code family for a home sleep test, and re-pull that MAC's current article at least annually, because this is one of the least standardized corners of Medicare coding and it has already changed once in the last several years following specialty-society advocacy. Don't build a universal scrubber rule that assumes one code set is safe everywhere — build a jurisdiction-aware rule instead.

It's worth understanding how this got as inconsistent as it is, because it explains why the rule keeps tripping practices up even after they think they've learned it once. The American Academy of Sleep Medicine successfully advocated for changes to both Noridian's and CGS's local coverage articles specifically because the original, stricter language was blocking legitimate home-testing claims — Noridian's update took effect in November 2021, CGS's in January 2022. That's a genuine, documented policy win for the specialty. But it also means any billing reference, internal payer matrix, or staff training material written before those dates describes a rule that's now out of date in at least two major jurisdictions, and any reference written since then that flattens the outcome into "Medicare now allows either code set everywhere" is wrong in CGS's jurisdiction specifically, where the current 2026 article still requires the G-code for a portable-monitor home study. A rule this specific, that has already changed once, needs a re-verification habit built around it, not a one-time training memo.

Financially, this is not a small category of denial. Home sleep testing is the highest-volume sleep study performed in most practices, precisely because it's cheaper and more convenient than an in-lab study for the large majority of straightforward OSA cases payers and AASM guidance both prefer to test that way first. A code-selection error at that volume compounds fast: a practice billing the wrong code set for its jurisdiction isn't looking at one denied claim, it's looking at every home sleep test claim submitted since the last time someone checked the rule, all denying in a predictable, entirely preventable pattern.

The full sleep study code family

Sleep testing codes are organized around three variables: attended versus unattended, the number of physiologic channels recorded, and whether CPAP or bilevel titration happens during the same study. Getting any one of the three wrong is a coding error, independent of medical necessity.

The channel counts in that table aren't arbitrary billing trivia — they describe what the equipment actually has to be capable of recording, and payers audit against them. EEG, EOG, and EMG (electroencephalography, electro-oculography, electromyography) are the three channels that let a technologist or an automated scoring algorithm stage sleep — distinguish wake from light sleep, deep sleep, and REM — which is exactly the capability a Type III or Type IV home monitor doesn't have and a Type I or Type II device does. Airflow and respiratory effort are typically two separate channels (nasal pressure or thermal airflow sensing, and a belt-based chest/abdominal effort measurement), because distinguishing an obstructive event (effort continues, airflow stops) from a central event (both stop together) requires seeing them independently rather than inferring one from the other. Oxygen saturation and heart rate/ECG round out the standard channel set. A study billed under a code that implies more channels than were actually recorded, or interpreted as though sleep staging occurred when the device used couldn't perform it, is a coding problem and a clinical-validity problem at the same time — and it's exactly the mechanism behind the key takeaway above that none of the HSAT-tier devices in this table can stage sleep or detect REM sleep, no matter how the resulting report is worded.

⚠️ 2027 is a hard cutover. The AMA CPT Editorial Panel approved deletion of 95800, 95801, and 95806 effective January 1, 2027, replacing them with a new six-code family that separates technical (equipment setup, data acquisition, scoring) from professional (physician interpretation and report) work and sorts each study into a low, moderate, or high complexity tier based on the physiologic signals captured. This build confirmed the deletion and 2027 effective date directly, but could not confirm the final published descriptors or numbering for the replacement six-code family against CMS's or the AMA's primary source, since that set had not been finalized in publicly available CPT files at the time of this build — treat the replacement code numbers reported by third-party sources as provisional and verify against the AMA's CPT Editorial Summary of Panel Actions and the 2027 CPT Professional edition before building 2027 charge masters. 2026 dates of service are unaffected; this is a planning item, not an immediate action item.

Full code-by-code documentation requirements, plus the specific channel and parameter counts payers audit against, are covered in our polysomnography vs. home sleep test billing guide.

One structural detail that trips up practices that don't own their own sleep lab: every code in the table above can be split into a technical component (equipment, facility, and the technologist's time) and a professional component (physician interpretation and report) using modifiers TC and 26, exactly as in other diagnostic specialties. A sleep physician who reads studies acquired at a hospital-owned lab bills 26 only; the hospital bills TC for the same code and date. Billing the global code (no modifier) when the practice doesn't own the equipment produces the same duplicate-claim conflict described in the modifiers section below, and it's just as easy for a payer to catch, because the facility's own claim shows a competing technical charge for the same patient and date.

Multiple sleep latency and maintenance of wakefulness testing

Narcolepsy and idiopathic hypersomnia workups depend on a second, daytime test that most billing guides mention only in passing, and it has its own sequencing rule that's easy to miss when a lab schedules it as a stand-alone service.

95805 covers multiple sleep latency testing (MSLT) or maintenance of wakefulness testing (MWT) — a day-long protocol of four or five scheduled nap opportunities, each scored for how quickly the patient falls asleep and whether REM sleep appears, with staff monitoring the patient between naps throughout the day. The MSLT is the test that actually establishes a narcolepsy diagnosis at the physiologic level, because it's built to detect sleep-onset REM periods, and a short mean sleep latency plus multiple sleep-onset REM periods across the day's naps is the diagnostic signature the specialty is testing for. MWT, using the same code, answers a different clinical question — whether a patient can stay awake, relevant to occupational and driving-safety evaluations — and the two shouldn't be conflated in documentation even though they share a CPT code.

The sequencing rule matters for both clinical validity and billing defensibility: an MSLT has to immediately follow an overnight, attended, in-lab polysomnogram (95810), not stand alone as an isolated daytime test. The overnight study rules out other causes of daytime sleepiness (most importantly untreated obstructive sleep apnea) and establishes that the patient got an adequate, representative night of sleep before the daytime latency test is interpreted — an MSLT interpreted without that overnight context is clinically weaker and, from a coverage standpoint, harder to justify as medically necessary on its own. Billed correctly, a narcolepsy workup typically generates two line items on consecutive dates of service: 95810 for the overnight PSG, and 95805 for the following day's MSLT, both supported by the same underlying clinical indication and, once confirmed, the appropriate narcolepsy diagnosis code (G47.411 with cataplexy or G47.419 without) rather than a placeholder hypersomnia or sleepiness code once the diagnosis is established.

Split-night studies

A split-night study lets a single overnight recording serve as both the diagnostic study and the CPAP titration, avoiding a second night in the lab — but only when the diagnostic portion establishes severe enough disease, early enough in the night, to leave adequate recording time for titration. Getting the timing wrong produces a study that's neither a complete diagnostic study nor an adequate titration, and CPT 95811 is the only code billed for it either way once titration has started.

Sleep medicine's technical guidance for converting a diagnostic study to titration mid-night generally supports the switch once an apnea-hypopnea index of at least 40 has been documented during a minimum of two hours of diagnostic recording, with some protocols supporting conversion at an AHI of roughly 20 to 40 based on clinical judgment when repetitive long obstructions and major oxygen desaturations are also present. Below that threshold, a split-night protocol risks an inaccurate pressure determination compared with a full-night titration, because there isn't enough diagnostic data to be confident about disease severity before committing the rest of the night to titration. The titration portion itself then needs to run more than three hours, since respiratory events and the pressure required to control them commonly worsen later in the night, particularly during REM sleep and the supine position — a titration that's cut short because too little of the night remained rarely produces a defensible therapeutic pressure.

⚠️ These specific thresholds — the AHI-40 conversion criterion, the AHI 20–40 clinical-judgment range, and the greater-than-three-hour titration minimum — are drawn from sleep technologist and AASM-aligned technical guideline summaries located during this build; this build could not open the current American Academy of Sleep Medicine clinical practice guideline PDF directly to confirm the exact current wording, so treat these as reported figures and confirm them against the live AASM clinical practice guideline for titration before encoding them into a lab protocol or a payer-facing medical necessity argument.

Billing-wise, the rule is simple even though the clinical criteria aren't: when a study starts diagnostic and converts to titration the same night, only 95811 is billed for that date of service. First Coast Service Options' current billing article states this directly — 95811 is inclusive of 95810 for a split-night study, so the two are not separately payable for the same night regardless of how the record documents the two phases. Full split-night protocol documentation requirements and denial patterns specific to this scenario are in our split-night and titration billing guide.

Titration studies: 95811 versus a separate diagnostic-and-titration pair

There are three ways a patient ends up titrated to an effective CPAP or bilevel pressure, and each has a different billing signature.

  • 1Split-night (95811 alone). Diagnostic and titration happen on the same night, converting when the AHI/timing criteria above are met. Bill 95811 only, for the one date of service.
  • 2Separate diagnostic and titration nights (95810, then 95811 on a later date). The diagnostic study didn't meet split-night conversion criteria, so a second, dedicated titration night is scheduled. Both codes are billable because they're on different dates of service — the NCCI same-day bundling issue below only applies when both happen on the same calendar day.
  • 3Home diagnostic test, then in-lab titration only. A positive HSAT (G0398/G0399/G0400 or, per your MAC's current article, the equivalent CPT code) established the diagnosis; the patient is then brought into the lab solely for CPAP titration and billed 95811 for that visit, since there's no same-night diagnostic component to bundle against.

The failure mode we see most often is scenario 2 billed as if it were scenario 1 — both 95810 and 95811 submitted for the same date of service because two "studies" happened to be scored from the same recording, or because a scheduling error double-booked what should have been sequential nights. Confirm the actual calendar dates on the sleep lab's own study log before submitting, not just the coder's read of the interpretation report. Worked scenarios, sample documentation, and a decision tree for all three paths are in our split-night and titration billing guide.

Titration billing also has to account for which device is actually being initiated, because the therapy and the diagnosis have to match. Standard CPAP titration supports a single fixed pressure for straightforward obstructive sleep apnea. Bilevel titration, using a device later billed as E0470 or E0471, is indicated when a patient can't tolerate a single CPAP pressure or needs independently adjustable inspiratory and expiratory pressures. Adaptive servo-ventilation, a bilevel-based therapy with breath-to-breath algorithmic adjustment aimed at central sleep apnea and Cheyne-Stokes respiration, carries its own well-documented clinical contraindication: since the SERVE-HF trial results became public in 2015, ASV is contraindicated in patients with chronic heart failure and reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF, ejection fraction 45% or less) with predominant central sleep apnea, because of an observed increase in cardiovascular mortality in that population. A titration order for ASV in a heart-failure patient with reduced ejection fraction is a documentation and safety flag before it's ever a billing question, and a payer's medical review is increasingly likely to ask for the ejection fraction on file. ⚠️ ASV device billing itself is commonly reported against the same bilevel-with-backup-rate code (E0471) used for other backup-rate bilevel therapy; this build did not find a dedicated, distinct HCPCS code specific to ASV algorithmic devices, so confirm current PDAC (Pricing, Data Analysis and Coding) coding guidance for the specific ASV device model before billing, since correct coding decisions for this device class are made at the model level by the DME MAC's Pricing, Data Analysis and Coding contractor, not by therapy category alone.

A related titration-night finding worth planning documentation around: treatment-emergent (or "complex") central sleep apnea, where a study that was primarily obstructive on the diagnostic portion shows new or persistent central apneas once CPAP resolves the obstructive events, commonly defined clinically as a central apnea index of 5 or more per hour with more than half of the residual events being central. It shows up in a meaningful minority of patients during PAP titration and resolves on its own within the first few months of therapy more often than not, but a persistent case may need a change in device class (toward bilevel with backup rate or ASV) rather than simply continuing standard CPAP. ⚠️ This build did not locate a distinct ICD-10-CM code specific to treatment-emergent or complex sleep apnea during verification; the primary OSA code (G47.33) or the other-sleep-apnea code (G47.39) are the most plausible candidates depending on payer coding guidance, but confirm the correct code with your own coding resources and the payer's own guidance before submitting, rather than treating either as a confirmed dedicated code for this specific finding.

Pediatric sleep study billing

Pediatric sleep medicine runs on a different age cutoff, different severity thresholds, and a different dominant clinical driver than adult sleep medicine, and billing it like a smaller version of the adult workflow produces both coding and medical-necessity errors.

The age-6 cutoff embedded in the CPT code set is a hard coding line, not a documentation nicety: 95782 and 95783 apply to polysomnography in patients younger than 6 years (with 95783 adding CPAP or bilevel titration during the study), while 95810 and 95811 apply at age 6 and older. A study on a 5-year, 11-month-old patient billed as 95810 is billed under the wrong code purely on age, independent of how the study was actually performed or scored.

Severity thresholds also differ from the adult AHI framework used throughout the rest of this guide. Pediatric obstructive sleep apnea is commonly graded using an AHI-based scale of roughly 1–4.9 events per hour for mild disease, 5–9.9 for moderate, and 10 or more for severe — materially lower cut points than the adult AHI/RDI thresholds (15, or 5–14 with qualifying symptoms) built into Medicare's CPAP national coverage determination, which in any case applies to the adult Medicare population rather than pediatric patients. ⚠️ This build sourced the pediatric AHI severity bands from published pediatric sleep medicine literature rather than a single primary payer or AASM policy document during this build; confirm the current pediatric scoring criteria (commonly referenced against the AASM Scoring Manual) and your specific pediatric payer's own coverage policy before using these bands as a coverage argument, since pediatric sleep apnea coverage runs through commercial and Medicaid payer policy rather than the Medicare NCDs cited elsewhere in this guide.

Clinically, adenotonsillar hypertrophy is the dominant driver of pediatric OSA, and adenotonsillectomy is the standard first-line treatment rather than CPAP — which changes the billing picture entirely for most pediatric cases, since there's no DME compliance-monitoring cycle to manage when the treatment is surgical. Post-adenotonsillectomy persistent or recurrent OSA is well documented in the clinical literature, particularly in children with obesity or severe pre-operative disease, which is exactly the population where a follow-up polysomnogram (95782, or 95810 if the patient has since turned 6) needs a diagnosis code establishing that this is a re-evaluation of persistent or recurrent disease, not a routine repeat of an already-answered question, to survive a medical-necessity review. Full pediatric-specific coding and documentation detail is covered alongside the adult code family in our polysomnography vs. home sleep test billing guide.

CPAP and DME billing: the compliance trap

CPAP therapy is billed as durable medical equipment, not as a professional service, and DME follows an entirely different set of rules than the sleep study that diagnosed the need for it — capped rental, a documented adherence requirement, and a modifier structure that fails the claim outright if it's missing.

Medicare's CPAP national coverage determination (NCD 240.4) covers an initial 12-week trial for adult OSA patients who show either an AHI/RDI of at least 15 events per hour, or an AHI/RDI of 5 to 14 events per hour with documented excessive daytime sleepiness, impaired cognition, mood disorder, insomnia, hypertension, ischemic heart disease, or history of stroke. That trial has to be built on a positive diagnosis from an attended in-lab PSG, or an unattended home sleep test using a Type II, Type III, or Type IV (measuring at least 3 channels) monitor — a diagnostic test outside that list does not support CPAP coverage under the NCD, full stop. Continued coverage past the initial trial requires the beneficiary to have benefited from CPAP during that period, which is where the compliance-monitoring requirement comes in.

⚠️ The compliance number everyone cites, verify it against the primary LCD before building a workflow on it. Industry-standard guidance widely published by both DME MACs describes adherence as at least 4 hours of nightly CPAP use on 70% of nights (roughly 21 of 30 nights) within a consecutive 30-day period, assessed sometime in the first three months of therapy. This build confirmed that the compliance threshold specifically lives in DME MAC local coverage determination L33718 (CGS Administrators and Noridian Healthcare Solutions, the two DME MACs, jointly), and confirmed the KX/GA/GZ modifier structure directly from the LCD's companion policy article (A52467) — but could not pull the exact numeric adherence clause from L33718's primary text during this build. Confirm the current 4-hour/70%/30-day language directly in L33718 before hard-coding it into a compliance-tracking workflow or a denial appeal.

The modifier structure is unambiguous and directly confirmed from the DME policy article: claim lines for PAP devices billed without a KX, GA, or GZ modifier are rejected as missing information. Append KX only when every coverage criterion in the LCD's coverage-indications section is met — for an initial claim (months 1–3), the qualifying diagnostic test and AHI/RDI threshold; for continued coverage (month 4 onward), both the initial criteria and documented evidence of benefit and adherence. Append GA when coverage criteria are not met but a valid Advance Beneficiary Notice is on file, and GZ when they're not met and no ABN was obtained — GZ claims are expected to deny and the patient cannot be billed for them. The single most expensive version of this trap: a supplier attaches KX on a month-4 rental claim without the adherence download actually being reviewed and documented in the chart first. That claim is indefensible on audit even if the patient really was compliant, because the file doesn't prove it.

Rental-versus-purchase status has its own modifier layer, separate from KX/GA/GZ: RR marks a monthly rental claim line, NU marks a purchase of new equipment, UE marks a purchase of used equipment, and RA marks the first rental claim for a replacement item following loss, theft, or irreparable damage (and must be dropped from every subsequent month's claim for that same item). E0601 is a capped-rental item — billed RR monthly for up to 13 paid rental months, after which ownership transfers to the patient automatically and rental billing stops on its own; a supplier that keeps billing RR past month 13, or that bills NU up front for a capped-rental item, is billing outside the item's payment category regardless of whether the compliance criteria were met.

Every CPAP claim starts with a Standard Written Order (SWO), required for dates of service on or after January 1, 2020, and it has to reach the supplier before the claim is billed, not after. The order has to identify the beneficiary, describe the item with enough specificity to bill correctly (for a mask, the DME MAC's documentation expects either the specific mask type on the order itself or the medical record supporting the mask actually dispensed), carry an order date, and be reviewed and signed by the treating practitioner even when someone else in the practice completes the paperwork. A supplier has to keep the SWO and its supporting documentation on file and produce it on request — a compliant device and a compliant patient don't save a claim if the order behind it doesn't meet the SWO standard.

⚠️ Remote monitoring of PAP compliance is not separately payable to a DMEPOS supplier. CMS's positive airway pressure policy article states directly that there is no benefit category and no separate payment to DMEPOS suppliers for remote monitoring services. That matters because remote physiologic monitoring codes (99453, 99454, 99457, 99458) and the older remote monitoring code 99091 are billed by physicians and qualified healthcare professionals under the Medicare Physician Fee Schedule for genuine clinical monitoring of physiologic data — not by the DME supplier reviewing a compliance download to decide whether to attach KX. Practices that assume the adherence-tracking work itself is separately billable to Medicare under an RPM code, when it's actually a DMEPOS supplier reviewing its own equipment's usage data to support a coverage attestation, are conflating two different billing relationships. Whether a genuine RPM code applies at all depends on who is doing the monitoring, under what benefit category, and for what clinical purpose — confirm this against the current article and your own arrangement before billing either code family for CPAP-related monitoring work.

Modifiers

Sleep medicine's modifier set is smaller than most specialties', but each one gates either a claim's payability or its audit defensibility outright — there's very little gray area in how they're supposed to be used, and unlike a specialty where a modifier decision is often a judgment call about clinical distinctness, most of the modifiers below are triggered by an objective, checkable fact (a recording duration, a rental month number, an ABN's presence or absence) rather than a subjective read of the encounter.

52
  • Reduced services — a sleep study recording less than 6 hours, or otherwise falling short of a complete study. First Coast's current billing article states this directly: report modifier 52 for a study under 6 hours of recording, or in other cases of reduced services.
  • The chart needs to state why the study ended early — equipment failure, patient intolerance, an adverse event — to make the modifier defensible on review, not just the shortened duration by itself.
76 / 77
  • 76 — repeat procedure or service, same day, by the same physician or qualified healthcare professional (CMS treats physicians of the same group and specialty as the same physician for this purpose).
  • 77 — repeat procedure by a different physician or QHP after the original. Documentation must show the repeat was a genuinely identical procedure repeated, not a follow-on service.
KX
  • DME compliance attestation — append only when every criterion in the PAP device LCD's coverage-indications section is actually met and documented, including, for continued coverage, the adherence review itself.
  • Per the PAP device policy article, a claim line submitted without KX, GA, or GZ is rejected outright as missing information — one of these three modifiers is mandatory on every PAP device claim line, not optional.
26 / TC
  • 26 — professional component only; the interpreting physician doesn't own the equipment or employ the technologist. Default for a physician reading studies acquired at a hospital-owned or third-party sleep lab.
  • TC — technical component only; billed by whoever owns the equipment and staffs the study, regardless of who interprets it.
RR / NU / UE / RA
  • DME rental-versus-purchase status — RR for a monthly capped-rental claim line, NU for a new purchase, UE for used equipment, RA on the first claim only for a loss/theft/damage replacement item.
  • Independent of KX/GA/GZ; a claim needs the correct combination of a coverage-status modifier and a rental-status modifier, not one or the other.
KX without a reviewed adherence download
  • Attaching KX on a month-4-or-later rental claim before the adherence data has actually been downloaded, reviewed, and documented in the chart is indefensible on audit — even if the patient turns out to have been compliant, the record has to show that determination was made before the claim went out, not reconstructed afterward.

GA (Advance Beneficiary Notice obtained, coverage criteria not expected to be met) and GZ (coverage criteria not expected to be met, no ABN obtained) round out the DME modifier set — GZ claims are expected to deny and the beneficiary cannot be billed for a GZ-modified line. GY applies more narrowly, to items or services statutorily excluded from the Medicare benefit altogether rather than merely failing a coverage criterion. NCCI edits, MUEs, and bundling

Same-night bundling is sleep medicine's dominant NCCI pattern, and unlike some of the values in this space, the core bundling rule for the specialty's highest-volume code pair is directly confirmed against a primary MAC source rather than reported secondhand. The specialty doesn't carry anywhere near the bundling density of a procedure-heavy specialty like cardiology or orthopedics — there simply aren't that many services performed together in a single sleep medicine encounter — but the one bundling pair that does exist is high-volume and easy to get wrong precisely because it looks like two legitimately separate services (a diagnostic study and a titration) rather than an obvious duplicate.

95810 and 95811, same date of service. First Coast Service Options' current billing article states plainly that CPT 95811 is inclusive of CPT 95810 for a split-night study, and that only 95811 should be billed in that scenario. Submitting both codes for the same date, rather than 95811 alone, is a bundling error the claims system is built to catch — not two separately payable services. The two codes remain separately billable when the diagnostic and titration nights genuinely fall on different calendar dates (see the titration-studies section above).

The general NCCI modifier-indicator structure works the same way here as everywhere else in Medicare coding:

  • 0Never bypassable. No modifier overrides it; the Column 2 code isn't separately payable regardless of documentation.
  • 1Bypassable with documentation. An NCCI-associated modifier (59 or the specific X-modifier) can override the edit only where the record shows the second service was genuinely distinct.
  • 9Edit deleted. The pair no longer applies.

Two other bundling patterns show up often enough in sleep medicine to plan around. First, an evaluation-and-management visit billed the same day as a diagnostic sleep study is a significant, separately identifiable service edit case in the general sense that applies across specialties — if a genuinely separate E/M service occurred the same day as a procedure, modifier 25 supports billing both, but a routine pre-study check-in that's really just part of getting the patient hooked up to the equipment isn't a separately billable E/M on its own. Second, the technical and professional component split described in the modifiers section above is itself an NCCI-adjacent risk: billing the global (unsplit) code when the practice doesn't actually own the equipment or employ the technologist produces the same duplicate-claim conflict as billing 26 and TC together elsewhere in diagnostic medicine, and a payer's system catches it exactly the same way, by comparing it against the facility's own competing technical claim for the same date.

⚠️ On MUE and PTP indicator specifics beyond the 95810/95811 pair: this build could not open CMS's primary NCCI PTP Edits file or the MUE table directly to confirm current per-code unit caps for 95810, 95811, G0398, G0399, or G0400 — secondary billing-industry sources describe a 1-unit-per-day MUE on 95810, which is directionally consistent with how these codes are used clinically (one study per patient per night), but treat that specific number as reported-but-unconfirmed and verify it in the CMS NCCI MUE Lookup Tool before building a scrubber rule or an appeal argument around it. Where MUE denials do show up in sleep medicine, they're most often a duplicate-order problem — two studies scheduled for the same patient on the same date because of a scheduling error — rather than a genuine attempt to bill medically necessary repeat units. Worked billing scenarios

The rules above are easiest to apply correctly when they're walked through against an actual patient path rather than read as a list of independent facts. Three scenarios cover most of the real-world variation this guide has addressed.

Scenario 1: Medicare patient, CGS jurisdiction, suspected uncomplicated OSA. The physician documents excessive daytime sleepiness and witnessed apneas at the office visit (billed as a standard E/M code), and orders a home sleep test. Because the DME MAC and the Part B MAC in this jurisdiction is CGS, and the study will use a portable Type III monitor, the claim should be billed as G0399, not 95801 — per CGS's current article, a home study performed with a portable monitor must be billed with the most specific G-code, regardless of the more general permissive language elsewhere in the same article. The test returns an AHI of 22. CPAP (E0601) is ordered under a Standard Written Order referencing the qualifying AHI, billed E0601-RR monthly with KX once the initial coverage criteria are documented. At the month-4 mark, the supplier reviews and documents the adherence download before resubmitting with KX for continued coverage — not after.

Scenario 2: Commercial payer, step-therapy requirement, split-night conversion. The payer's sleep-testing policy requires a home sleep test before authorizing an in-lab study for this patient's presentation. The HSAT is inconclusive (a common outcome that doesn't necessarily mean OSA is absent, just that the study didn't answer the question), so prior authorization is requested and obtained for an in-lab study. During the study, the patient's AHI reaches 45 within the first two hours of diagnostic recording, meeting split-night conversion criteria, and more than three hours remain for CPAP titration. The claim is billed as 95811 alone for that date of service — not 95810 plus 95811 — because 95811 is inclusive of the diagnostic portion for a split-night study.

Scenario 3: Established CPAP patient, compliance failure, requalification. A patient midway through the initial 90-day trial hasn't met the 4-hour/70%-of-nights adherence standard when the supplier reviews the download. Rather than attaching KX anyway, the supplier documents the shortfall, and per the requalification pathway, a repeat in-lab polysomnogram is ordered before a new trial period is authorized — billed 95810 if purely diagnostic, or 95811 if titration is repeated in the same encounter. Coverage of the CPAP rental during the gap between the failed trial and the new qualifying study is not supported without a fresh determination; billing straight through the gap on the original KX attestation is exactly the indefensible-on-audit pattern described earlier in this guide.

Scenario 4: CPAP intolerance, switch to oral appliance therapy. A Medicare patient with mild-to-moderate OSA (AHI 18) initiated CPAP successfully from a coding standpoint but cannot tolerate the mask after a genuine, documented trial. The treating physician evaluates the patient, documents the intolerance, and orders a custom oral appliance instead. Because oral appliance coverage runs through its own policy rather than automatically following the CPAP authorization, the practice confirms separately that the original qualifying sleep test and the G47.33 diagnosis on file satisfy the oral appliance LCD's own requirements, obtains a new written order specific to the appliance, and bills the completed, fitted device as E0486-KX-NU once the qualifying test, diagnosis, and order are all confirmed on file — not assumed to already be satisfied because CPAP had previously been authorized for the same diagnosis.

AR follow-up workflow for sleep medicine denials

Sleep medicine's denial mix is unusually front-loaded toward errors that are preventable before submission rather than genuinely disputable after the fact, which changes what an efficient AR follow-up workflow should prioritize. Working the highest-dollar claim in the queue first is the wrong instinct here; working the categories that are actually correctable first is the right one.

  • 1Sort code-set mismatches out of the appeals queue entirely. A CPT-versus-G-code denial in a jurisdiction where the MAC's article requires the other code family isn't an appeal candidate — it's a same-day rebill once the correct code is identified. Routing it into a standard appeals workflow with its multi-week turnaround wastes the exact number of days a rebill would have resolved instantly.
  • 2Confirm the calendar dates before disputing a 95810/95811 bundling denial. If both codes were genuinely billed for the same date of service on what was actually a split-night study, correct and resubmit as 95811 alone; there's no appeal to win. If the dates were actually different (a diagnostic night and a separate titration night), the correction is the opposite one — resubmit with the distinct dates clearly reflected, since that's a data-entry correction, not a clinical dispute.
  • 3Route DME compliance denials to whoever holds the adherence data before deciding whether to appeal. The billing team can't evaluate a KX-modifier denial without pulling the device download and its review date first — if the adherence review demonstrably happened and met criteria before the original claim date, appeal with that documentation attached; if it didn't, the claim was correctly denied and the priority shifts to fixing the workflow gap, not fighting the outcome.
  • 4Track authorization denials back to the scheduling decision, not just the claim. A missing prior authorization on an in-lab study that should have gone through a home-test-first step therapy is a scheduling and intake process failure recurring at the point where the study was booked — fixing the individual claim doesn't fix the next one behind it in the queue.

The throughline across all four: sleep medicine AR follow-up pays off more from tightening the intake-to-submission workflow than from aggressive post-denial argumentation, because so much of the denial volume here is genuinely preventable rather than genuinely disputable. That doesn't mean appeals have no place in the workflow — a genuine medical-necessity denial where the AHI/RDI value and qualifying comorbidity were both documented and simply weren't cited correctly on the original claim is a legitimate, winnable appeal, and it's worth distinguishing clearly from the categories above precisely so it doesn't get lost in a queue dominated by simple rebills. The distinction that matters for staffing an AR team efficiently is between claims that need a coding or data correction and claims that need an actual clinical argument, and sorting a denial into the right bucket on first touch, rather than after a week of investigation, is most of what separates a fast AR cycle from a slow one in this specialty.

Who owns each step: a workflow ownership map

Most of the denial patterns in this guide trace back to a single root cause: a step that needed a specific person to own it didn't have one, and it fell into a gap between two roles that each assumed the other was handling it. A short ownership map, reviewed against your own practice's actual staffing, catches those gaps before a denial does.

  • 1Front desk / intake: confirms eligibility for both the testing benefit and the DME benefit separately, checks whether the payer requires a home-test-first step therapy, and flags any comorbidity that might route the patient straight to an in-lab study instead.
  • 2Ordering physician: documents the clinical evaluation supporting the specific test ordered, signs the order before the test is performed, and later reviews and signs off on the qualifying diagnostic result before a DME order is generated from it.
  • 3Sleep technologist / lab: acquires the study with the channel set the intended code requires, flags early terminations for modifier 52, and gets the completed interpretation report to both the coder and, when CPAP is indicated, the DME side of the workflow without a manual hand-off delay.
  • 4Coder/biller: confirms the Part B MAC and DME MAC jurisdictions before selecting a home-test code family, checks same-date bundling before submitting a split-night claim, and confirms the diagnosis code reflects the specific, established diagnosis rather than a placeholder symptom code.
  • 5DME coordinator or supplier liaison: holds the Standard Written Order, tracks the compliance-monitoring window against the calendar (not against when someone happens to remember to check), and owns the KX/GA/GZ decision on each claim based on documented adherence review, not assumption.

In a smaller practice, several of these roles collapse onto one or two people, which makes the map more important, not less — a single biller wearing the coder, DME coordinator, and AR follow-up hats needs a checklist standing in for the cross-checks that a larger, more specialized team would otherwise provide informally. Whichever way the roles are actually distributed in a given practice, the underlying question worth asking periodically is simple: for each of the five steps above, is there a named person who would notice if it didn't happen, or does everyone assume someone else is watching it? The gaps that produce sleep medicine's most common denials tend to live in exactly that second category.

Eligibility verification and benefit checks before the study

Sleep testing sits across two benefit categories that don't always live in the same place on an eligibility response — the sleep study itself is typically a diagnostic/outpatient benefit, while the CPAP device and supplies that follow are a separate durable medical equipment benefit, frequently carrying its own deductible, coinsurance structure, and network of contracted DME suppliers distinct from the practice's own in-network status. Verifying eligibility for the office visit and the sleep study alone, and assuming the DME benefit will simply follow along the same terms, is a common gap: a patient can be fully in-network for the physician encounter and the diagnostic test, and still face an out-of-network DME supplier if the practice doesn't separately confirm which suppliers the payer actually contracts with for CPAP equipment in that patient's plan.

Three things are worth confirming at intake, before the study is scheduled rather than after a denial: whether the payer requires a home sleep test first for this patient's clinical presentation (see the prior authorization section below), whether the specific testing facility or interpreting physician is in-network for this plan (a common gap for freestanding sleep labs that contract separately from the referring physician's own practice), and whether the DME benefit has its own separate authorization requirement distinct from the testing authorization. Confirming the CPAP supplier's network status at the time of the diagnostic test — rather than after a positive diagnosis, when the patient is already expecting same-week equipment — avoids a scramble that too often ends in an out-of-network claim or a self-pay conversation nobody wanted to have.

It's also worth confirming, at the same intake step, whether the plan differentiates its authorization or benefit rules by testing location the way several MACs differentiate their coding rules — some commercial plans apply different medical-necessity documentation standards to a freestanding sleep lab than to a hospital-based one, and a plan's DME benefit occasionally carries a separate annual maximum or a distinct supply allowance from its general equipment benefit. None of that shows up on a single eligibility screen the way a deductible or copay does; it takes an actual read of the plan's own sleep-testing and DME policy documents, which is exactly the kind of front-loaded verification work that prevents the denial categories covered throughout this guide rather than reacting to them after the fact.

Documentation that survives an audit

Sleep medicine's claims are unusually dependent on documentation that lives outside the claim itself — an interpretation report, a device download, a signed order — which means a clean claim and a clean chart are not the same thing, and a payer's post-payment review is checking the chart, not the claim form.

  • 1The interpretation report has to state the numbers that justify the code and the diagnosis, not just a narrative impression. Total sleep time, sleep efficiency, AHI or RDI, oxygen saturation nadir, and the specific channels recorded all need to be in the report, because that's what a reviewer checks against the code billed and the ICD-10 code submitted — a report that only says "consistent with moderate OSA" without the AHI number that puts the patient in that category is difficult to defend if the coding is questioned later.
  • 2The order has to precede the study, and the study has to precede the device claim. A physician's order for a sleep study dated after the study was performed, or a CPAP order written before the qualifying diagnostic test was completed, breaks the sequence Medicare's coverage policy depends on — the order, the test, and the diagnosis have to happen in that order, on the record, not just in fact.
  • 3The adherence download has to be reviewed and dated before the KX-eligible claim goes out, not reconstructed afterward. A usage report pulled from the device weeks after the fact, timestamped to look contemporaneous with the claim, is exactly the kind of documentation gap a program integrity review is built to catch — the review date needs to genuinely precede the claim date.
  • 4A split-night or repeat study needs its own stated clinical reason, not just a second entry in the scheduling system. "Repeat PSG" without a note explaining why — incomplete first study, treatment failure, new symptom, post-surgical reassessment — reads as a duplicate order on review, even when the clinical reason was sound.
  • 5Records need to be retrievable on the timeline an auditor actually uses, not just retained somewhere. A DME supplier or sleep lab that can produce the SWO, the interpretation report, and the adherence review within the requested response window avoids the additional penalty of a non-response denial layered on top of whatever the underlying documentation review finds — knowing where each document lives, and who can pull it quickly, is itself part of being audit-ready.

None of these five items shows up as a denial reason on a remittance advice; they show up months later, in a post-payment audit or a recoupment letter, which is exactly why they're easy to deprioritize against the claims that are actively denying today. Building them into the intake and interpretation workflow up front is cheaper than defending their absence later, and it's considerably cheaper than a recoupment demand that reaches back across every claim submitted under a documentation gap nobody noticed until an auditor pointed it out.

Coordination between the sleep lab and the DME supplier

In a meaningful share of practices, the entity that performs and interprets the sleep study and the entity that supplies the CPAP device are different organizations entirely — a hospital-based or freestanding sleep lab on one side, a separate DME company on the other, sometimes with no shared billing system or even a shared electronic record. That organizational split is exactly where the compliance-documentation chain described throughout this guide is most likely to break, because no single party has visibility into the whole sequence: the ordering encounter, the qualifying diagnostic test, the DME order, the device dispensing, and the adherence review all need to connect into one defensible record, and each handoff between organizations is a place that connection can silently fail.

The practical fix is a documentation handoff checklist rather than an assumption that the other side has it covered: the sleep lab needs to get the qualifying AHI/RDI value and the interpreting physician's report to the DME supplier before the device is dispensed, not after; the DME supplier needs to get its Standard Written Order signed by the treating practitioner before billing, referencing the specific qualifying test; and the adherence data, once collected, needs to make it back to a clinician who can document the reassessment for continued coverage — which sometimes means the DME supplier is the party holding the compliance data that the ordering physician's practice needs for its own records, and the handoff has to run in that direction too. A practice that outsources DME supply entirely still owns the ordering physician's side of this chain, and "the supplier handles that" is not a defense on an audit of the ordering physician's own claims.

A short service-level agreement between the two organizations — even an informal one, if a formal contract isn't in place — that names who sends what, by when, and through what channel, converts an assumed handoff into a documented one, and gives both sides a concrete artifact to point to if a claim from either organization is later questioned.

Credentialing and accreditation for sleep labs

This is the gap most competitor content skips, and it's directly analogous to the IDTF accreditation trap in other diagnostic-heavy specialties: a sleep lab's Medicare billing privileges can depend on facility accreditation and physician board certification that are separate from, and layered on top of, standard payer enrollment. Palmetto GBA publishes a dedicated accreditation-and-credentialing article for polysomnography (A55958) requiring sleep centers to hold accreditation from one of three bodies: the American Academy of Sleep Medicine (AASM), the Accreditation Commission for Health Care (ACHC), or Joint Commission ambulatory care accreditation. Critically, since October 1, 2018, an outpatient sleep center affiliated with a hospital needs its own separate ambulatory care accreditation — the hospital's general Joint Commission status does not automatically cover it.

Physician credentialing runs in parallel: the interpreting physician needs either board certification in sleep medicine through the American Board of Sleep Medicine (ABSM) or a member board of the American Board of Medical Specialties (ABMS) or American Osteopathic Association (AOA), or completion of an ACGME-accredited sleep medicine fellowship followed by certification through one of those boards within two examination cycles. Neither requirement is optional paperwork layered on for its own sake — a lab or an interpreting physician that doesn't meet it can find claims denied on a credentialing basis that has nothing to do with whether the study itself was medically necessary or correctly coded.

The overlap with independent diagnostic testing facility (IDTF) enrollment, which shows up in other diagnostic-heavy specialties this firm covers, is worth checking rather than assuming away: a freestanding sleep lab that is not affiliated with a hospital and bills Medicare independently of a physician's own claim may fall under IDTF enrollment requirements in addition to the sleep-specific accreditation described above, depending on its ownership and billing structure. That's a separate CMS-855B enrollment track with its own supervising-physician listing and facility requirements, layered on top of, not instead of, the AASM/ACHC/Joint Commission accreditation and physician board-certification requirements already described. Confirm which enrollment category your specific facility structure falls into before assuming sleep-specific accreditation alone covers the enrollment side of the picture.

⚠️ Palmetto GBA's accreditation requirement is the one this build confirmed directly against the primary article text; whether every other MAC in this guide's coverage table has adopted an equivalent requirement was not independently confirmed for each jurisdiction during this build — check your own MAC's current polysomnography-related articles for an accreditation requirement specific to your jurisdiction before assuming Palmetto's rule does or doesn't apply to you. The Palmetto article's stated timeline gives a sense of how long adoption can take once a MAC does move on this: Jurisdiction M facilities had operated under the accreditation requirement since October 2015, while Jurisdiction J facilities weren't required to comply until July 2018 — nearly a three-year gap between two jurisdictions under the same contractor, which is itself a reminder that "requirements are converging across MACs" is not the same as "requirements are already uniform."

⚠️ Technologist-level credentialing (commonly referenced in the industry as registration through the Board of Registered Polysomnographic Technologists, or an equivalent respiratory or EEG technologist credential) is widely treated as part of AASM's facility accreditation standards, but Palmetto's own credentialing article, as fetched during this build, did not specify separate technologist credentialing requirements distinct from the accreditation and physician-certification requirements above. Confirm your accrediting body's current technologist credentialing standard directly rather than assuming a specific certification is mandated by Medicare itself, since the enforceable Medicare-side requirement documented here is facility accreditation and physician board certification, not a named technologist credential.

Taken together, the pattern across this entire guide is consistent: sleep medicine's revenue leaks less from genuinely disputable medical necessity fights and more from a small number of structural, well-documented, entirely preventable coding and workflow gaps — the CPT/G-code jurisdiction trap, same-night bundling, an undocumented compliance attestation, and a handoff between the testing side and the DME side that nobody explicitly owns. Fixing those four things captures most of the recoverable revenue in this specialty before a single appeal letter ever needs to be written.

Frequently asked questions

Why does Medicare deny a home sleep apnea test billed with CPT codes instead of HCPCS G-codes?

It depends on which Medicare Administrative Contractor processes the claim, and that nuance is exactly what most billing guidance leaves out. CGS Administrators' current local coverage article states that a home study performed with a Type II, III, or IV portable monitor must be billed with the most specific G-code, not the equivalent CPT code, even though the same article lists CPT 95800, 95801, and 95806 as generally allowed. Other MACs, including Noridian and First Coast, currently permit either code set for a home study that meets the code's definition. The safe rule is to pull your own MAC's current polysomnography billing article before defaulting to either code set, because this is one of the least standardized corners of Medicare sleep testing coding.

What is the difference between CPT 95810 and 95811?

95810 describes attended, in-lab polysomnography with sleep staging and four or more additional parameters for a purely diagnostic study. 95811 describes the identical attended, in-lab study plus initiation of CPAP or bilevel therapy during the same recorded night. First Coast Service Options' current billing article states that 95811 is inclusive of 95810 for a split-night study, so only 95811 is billed when both the diagnostic and titration portions happen on one night — billing both codes for the same date is a bundling error, not two separately payable services.

What AHI does a split-night study need to switch from diagnostic to titration?

Sleep medicine's technical guidance generally supports converting to titration once an AHI of at least 40 has been documented during a minimum of two hours of diagnostic recording, with a lower range of roughly 20 to 40 sometimes supported by clinical judgment when repetitive long obstructions and major desaturations are also present. The titration portion then needs enough remaining recording time, generally more than three hours, to reliably find an effective pressure, because respiratory events and required pressure can both worsen later in the night. Confirm the current criteria in the American Academy of Sleep Medicine's clinical practice guideline before building a protocol around a specific number, since this is where the two-hour and forty-event figures were drawn from.

How long does a patient have to use CPAP to keep Medicare coverage?

Medicare's CPAP national coverage determination covers an initial 12-week trial to identify beneficiaries who benefit from therapy, and continued coverage past that trial depends on documented adherence and physician reassessment. Industry-standard adherence guidance built into the DME MAC's local coverage determination for PAP devices defines adherence as at least four hours of nightly use on 70% of nights within a consecutive 30-day period, assessed within the first three months of therapy — but confirm the exact adherence language currently published in LCD L33718 before relying on the specific numbers, since this guide could not pull that clause from the primary LCD text directly during this build.

Can a home sleep test diagnose narcolepsy?

No. A home sleep apnea test records cardiopulmonary channels — airflow, respiratory effort, oxygen saturation, and usually heart rate — but not EEG, EOG, or EMG, so it cannot stage sleep or detect REM sleep onset. Narcolepsy, REM sleep behavior disorder, and periodic limb movement disorder all require attended, in-lab polysomnography (CPT 95810/95811) to establish sleep architecture, and narcolepsy specifically requires a same-day multiple sleep latency test (CPT 95805) immediately following the overnight study. Ordering a home sleep test for a patient with suspected narcolepsy is a clinical-appropriateness error before it is ever a billing one.

When is modifier 52 appropriate on a sleep study claim?

Modifier 52 (reduced services) applies when a sleep study is terminated early or otherwise falls short of a complete study — First Coast Service Options' current billing article specifically instructs reporting modifier 52 when a study records less than six hours, or in other cases of reduced services. It signals to the payer that the documented result reflects a shortened study rather than a coding error, and the chart needs to state why the study ended early (equipment failure, patient intolerance, an adverse event) to support the modifier on audit.

Do payers require a home sleep test before approving an in-lab polysomnography?

Very commonly, yes, for adults with suspected uncomplicated obstructive sleep apnea — many commercial plans, Medicare Advantage plans, and Medicaid managed care plans require a home sleep apnea test first and will not authorize an in-lab study until it has been attempted or has failed to yield a diagnostic result. That step-therapy requirement does not apply to patients with the comorbidities the American Academy of Sleep Medicine considers unsuitable for home testing — congestive heart failure, significant lung disease, suspected central sleep apnea, or neuromuscular disease — and payer policies generally route those patients directly to an in-lab study instead. Confirm the specific payer's sleep-testing policy before scheduling, because the comorbidity list and the authorization workflow both vary by plan.

Does a sleep lab need its own accreditation separate from the hospital's?

Often, yes. Palmetto GBA's accreditation and credentialing article for polysomnography requires sleep centers to maintain accreditation from the American Academy of Sleep Medicine, the Accreditation Commission for Health Care, or Joint Commission ambulatory care accreditation — and since October 1, 2018, an outpatient sleep center affiliated with a hospital needs its own ambulatory care accreditation rather than relying on the hospital's general Joint Commission status. The interpreting physician also needs sleep medicine board certification through the American Board of Sleep Medicine or a member board of the ABMS or AOA, or an ACGME sleep medicine fellowship followed by certification within two examination cycles. Confirm which MACs in your jurisdiction have adopted this requirement before assuming it doesn't apply to your lab.
