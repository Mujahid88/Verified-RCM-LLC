Diabetic shoe and orthotic DME billing for podiatry: A5500 codes explained.

The Therapeutic Shoes for Persons with Diabetes benefit pays for exactly one pair of shoes and their inserts per patient per calendar year — and Medicare's own error-rate reviews keep finding that most of the claims that don't get paid never had a chance, because the paperwork was wrong before the shoes were ever fitted. This guide covers the A5500 series codes, who's eligible, the certifying-physician requirement that trips up more claims than any coding error, the separate DMEPOS enrollment a podiatry practice needs before it can bill this benefit at all, and the frequency and denial patterns that decide whether the claim survives.

Reviewed by Phares A., MD · Physiotherapist, CPC (AAPC) · Part of our podiatry billing and coding guide

Key takeaways

  • Diabetes alone doesn't qualify a patient. Medicare Part B requires diabetes mellitus plus at least one of six specific foot conditions, all supported in the chart with dates and findings — not a general diabetic diagnosis.
  • The certifying physician usually isn't the podiatrist. CMS requires the M.D. or D.O. managing the patient's diabetes to certify the need — a role generally separate from the supplier who fits and bills for the shoes, outside a narrow rural/health-professional-shortage-area exception.
  • Billing these codes needs a second enrollment. A podiatry practice's Part B enrollment doesn't cover DMEPOS — the CMS-855S application, a $50,000 surety bond, and (absent an exemption) accreditation are a separate track.
  • The benefit is one package per calendar year — a depth shoe with up to three insert pairs, or a custom-molded shoe with up to two — not separate annual allowances for shoes and inserts.

What the benefit covers and who actually qualifies

The Therapeutic Shoes for Persons with Diabetes benefit is a distinct Medicare Part B benefit category under Social Security Act §1861(s)(12) — separate from the orthotics and prosthetics benefit and separate from routine footwear, which Medicare doesn't cover at all. Coverage exists because diabetic peripheral neuropathy and vascular disease turn ordinary shoe pressure points into ulcers, and ulcers are the leading path to amputation in this population. That's also why the eligibility bar isn't just "the patient has diabetes."

Per the CMS national Policy Article for this benefit, coverage requires diabetes mellitus and at least one of the following documented conditions, verified through Medicare's national coverage database (CMS Policy Article A52501, Therapeutic Shoes for Persons with Diabetes):

Qualifying conditions for the diabetic therapeutic shoe benefit — diabetes plus at least one of the six below (CMS Policy Article A52501).
Qualifying conditionWhat the chart needs to show
Previous lower-extremity amputation (partial or complete foot)Site and date of amputation, current residual limb status
History of previous foot ulcerationLocation, approximate date(s), and healed status of prior ulcer(s)
History of pre-ulcerative callusesCallus location and that it was assessed as pre-ulcerative, not incidental
Peripheral neuropathy with evidence of callus formationDocumented sensory loss (e.g., monofilament testing) plus a current callus finding
Foot deformitySpecific deformity named — e.g., hammertoe, Charcot foot, bunion — not "deformity" alone
Poor circulationVascular findings (diminished pulses, ABI results, or a vascular diagnosis) tied to the affected foot

The pattern in denied claims is almost always the same: the condition is real and the shoes are genuinely indicated, but the note states the conclusion ("patient has neuropathy and needs diabetic shoes") without the underlying finding that a reviewer can verify. A monofilament test result, a specific callus location, or a named vascular study attached to the same visit note is what separates a covered claim from a documentation denial on an otherwise legitimate case.

The HCPCS codes: shoes, inserts, and modifications

The A5500 series covers three categories of items — the shoe itself, the inserts that go inside it, and optional modifications — and getting the combination wrong is a coding error independent of medical necessity. A5500 and A5501 are mutually exclusive: one shoe code per shoe, never both on the same claim line for the same foot.

Core A5500 series HCPCS codes and what each one describes (CMS Policy Article A52501; PDAC coding verification required before billing A5500).
HCPCS codeItemKey documentation trigger
A5500Off-the-shelf depth-inlay shoe (¼" or 3/16" additional depth, per PDAC spec), fitted and dispensed by the supplierPDAC-verified product listing; billed per shoe
A5501Custom-molded shoe, fabricated over a positive model or cast of the patient's footDocumented foot deformity a depth shoe can't accommodate; impression/cast or CAD-CAM record on file
A5512Multi-density, prefabricated (heat-moldable) removable insert directly molded to the patient's footMolding method and base-layer specs met per code descriptor
A5513Multi-density, custom-fabricated removable insert made from a model of the patient's footCustom fabrication from an impression or model, not off-the-shelf
A5514Custom-fabricated insert milled from a rectified virtual (CAD-CAM) modelDigital scan/model workflow documented
A5503–A5507Shoe modifications — rigid rocker bottoms, wedges, metatarsal bars, offset heels, other repairsBilled only in addition to a covered A5500/A5501 base shoe, with the specific modification named

Inserts are billed per insert, not per pair — three covered pairs with a depth shoe is six insert line items, not three. A5510 (a compression-molded insert) and A5508 (deluxe features) are statutorily noncovered and should never appear on a Medicare claim expecting payment. Every A5500 depth shoe billed also has to carry a PDAC coding verification on file for that specific manufactured product; a depth shoe without PDAC verification denies as a coding error regardless of how well the medical necessity is documented.

Modifiers matter as much as the code itself. KX goes on the claim when all coverage criteria are met and documented; GY signals the item is statutorily excluded or criteria aren't met; and RT/LT are mandatory on any bilateral item, billed as separate lines with one unit each rather than two units on a single line.

The certifying physician split — the trap that isn't a coding problem

This is where most denials that look like documentation problems are actually a role problem. The benefit requires three distinct participants, and conflating them is the single most common structural error in a podiatry practice's diabetic shoe program:

  • 1The certifying physician. An M.D. or D.O. who is managing the patient's diabetes under a comprehensive plan of care — typically the primary care physician or endocrinologist, not the podiatrist. This physician must have an in-person visit with the patient addressing diabetes management within the six months before the shoes are delivered, and must sign a dated statement of certifying physician within three months before delivery, on or after that visit date, attesting to the diabetes diagnosis, the qualifying condition, the comprehensive care plan, and the need for diabetic shoes.
  • 2The prescribing practitioner. Writes the actual signed, dated order for the shoes before the claim is submitted. This can be a podiatrist, M.D., D.O., physician assistant, nurse practitioner, or clinical nurse specialist — anyone knowledgeable in diabetic shoe fitting. A podiatrist filling this role is standard and expected.
  • 3The supplier. Performs the in-person foot evaluation, takes measurements (or impressions/casts/CAD-CAM images for custom items), fits and dispenses the shoes, and bills the claim. A DMEPOS-enrolled podiatry practice can absolutely be the supplier.

The rule that catches practices off guard: the certifying physician generally cannot be the same person or entity as the supplier. A podiatrist can be the prescribing practitioner and the supplier on the same claim — that's normal — but stepping into the certifying-physician role as well creates the exact self-certification conflict CMS built this three-way split to prevent. The one documented exception is a certifying physician who practices in a designated rural area or health professional shortage area, where the same physician may also serve as the supplier. Outside that exception, a podiatry practice that dispenses diabetic shoes needs a genuine, separate certifying statement from the patient's diabetes-managing physician on every claim — not a form the podiatrist's office fills in and routes back for a signature of convenience.

DMEPOS supplier enrollment: the second enrollment podiatry practices miss

A podiatry practice's standard Medicare Part B enrollment authorizes billing E/M visits, nail debridement, and surgical procedures under its own specialty. It does not authorize billing DMEPOS codes like the A5500 series — that requires a separate DMEPOS supplier number obtained through its own enrollment track.

What a podiatry practice needs before its first A5500 series claim can be billed.
RequirementWhat it involves
CMS-855S enrollmentSeparate DMEPOS supplier application submitted to the practice's DME Medicare Administrative Contractor (the National Supplier Clearinghouse stopped processing these applications as of November 7, 2022; regional DME MACs handle 855S intake now)
Surety bondA minimum $50,000 surety bond per NPI, required under the Balanced Budget Act of 1997, with an additional $50,000 layer per adverse legal action in the prior ten years where applicable
AccreditationAccreditation through a CMS-approved accrediting organization is required for DMEPOS suppliers as a general rule; certain supplier types carry a narrow exemption limited to their normal scope of practice — confirm current exemption status with the DME MAC rather than assuming one applies
DMEPOS quality standardsPhysical location, business hours, licensure, and recordkeeping standards specific to DME suppliers, verified as part of enrollment and subject to site visit

The failure mode here isn't subtle once it happens: a practice bills A5500 claims under its regular Part B provider number, or under a DMEPOS number that lapsed on revalidation, and every claim denies for lack of supplier billing privileges — a denial that has nothing to do with the patient's eligibility or the documentation quality, and that no amount of appeal-letter clinical detail fixes. If diabetic shoe volume is more than occasional, confirm the DMEPOS enrollment is active, tied to the correct practice location, and revalidated on its own separate cycle from the group's Part B enrollment before scaling the program.

Frequency limits and the denials that follow from missing them

The benefit allows one package per beneficiary per calendar year — not separate annual counts for shoes and inserts. A patient gets either a depth shoe (A5500) with up to three pairs of inserts, or a custom-molded shoe (A5501) with up to two additional insert pairs, in a calendar year. Anything beyond that combination in the same year is denied as noncovered absent a documented change in the patient's foot (a new deformity, significant edema change) or a lost, stolen, or irreparably damaged item that independently justifies a replacement.

Common denial patterns on diabetic shoe claims and where the fix actually lives.
Denial patternRoot causeWhere to fix it
Missing or improperly dated certifying statementStatement absent, undated, signed outside the 3-month pre-delivery window, or the underlying visit fell outside the 6-month windowPhysician workflow — build the statement request into the diabetes-management visit itself, not as a follow-up fax
Billing without active DMEPOS enrollmentClaim submitted under the wrong provider number, or DMEPOS privileges lapsed on revalidationEnrollment/credentialing — verify active DMEPOS status before scheduling fittings, not after a denial posts
Exceeding the annual limitA second shoe/insert package billed in the same calendar year without a qualifying replacement reasonFront-desk eligibility check — confirm no prior diabetic shoe claim this calendar year before scheduling

Pro tip

Build a standing fax or portal request to each patient's PCP or endocrinologist that goes out the same day a diabetic shoe fitting is scheduled, not after the shoes are dispensed. CMS's own Comprehensive Error Rate Testing reviews have repeatedly identified the certifying physician's documentation — not the podiatrist's fitting note — as the leading source of improper payments in this benefit. Getting that statement moving early is the highest-leverage fix available, because it's the one piece of the file the podiatry practice doesn't fully control.

Frequently asked questions

Can the podiatrist who dispenses the diabetic shoes also be the certifying physician?

Generally no. CMS requires the certifying physician — the M.D. or D.O. who documents the patient's diabetes management and signs the statement of certifying physician — to be a different party from the supplier who furnishes and bills for the shoes, unless the certifying physician practices in a designated rural area or health professional shortage area. A podiatrist can still be the prescribing practitioner who writes the shoe order and, if the practice holds DMEPOS supplier billing privileges, the entity that fits and dispenses the shoes — those two roles are not restricted the way the certifying-physician role is.

Does a podiatry practice need separate DMEPOS enrollment to bill the A5500 series codes?

Yes, in almost every case. Billing A5500, A5501, or the A5512-A5514 insert codes requires active DMEPOS supplier billing privileges obtained through the CMS-855S enrollment application submitted to the practice's DME Medicare Administrative Contractor, plus a $50,000 surety bond per NPI and, unless a narrow supplier-type exemption applies, accreditation from a CMS-approved accrediting organization. A practice's Part B enrollment as a podiatry provider does not by itself authorize billing DMEPOS codes — the two enrollments are separate, and claims submitted without active DMEPOS privileges deny outright.

How often can a Medicare patient get diabetic shoes and inserts?

Once per calendar year, and the benefit is structured as a single package rather than separate annual allowances per item: one pair of depth shoes (A5500) with up to three pairs of inserts, or one pair of custom-molded shoes (A5501) with up to two additional pairs of inserts, within that calendar year. Additional pairs, or a request for the other shoe type in the same year, are denied as noncovered absent a documented replacement need such as a change in the patient's foot shape or a lost or irreparably damaged item.

What's the single most common reason a diabetic shoe claim gets denied?

A certifying physician statement that's missing, undated, signed outside the required window, or signed by someone other than the M.D. or D.O. managing the patient's diabetes. CMS Comprehensive Error Rate Testing (CERT) reviews have repeatedly found physician-side documentation — not the podiatrist's fitting record — as the leading source of improper payments in this benefit, because the statement has to reflect an in-person visit addressing diabetes management within the six months before delivery and be signed within three months before delivery, on or after that visit date.
