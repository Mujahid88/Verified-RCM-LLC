Denial management: work the cause, not the queue.

Most practices work denials in the order they arrive, which guarantees the same denials keep arriving. Denial management that actually changes the numbers sorts by root cause, reads the actual reason code instead of assuming the payer is simply wrong, fixes the process that produced each category, and treats the appeal as the secondary activity rather than the main one — all inside a deadline that is shorter than, and separate from, the original filing window.

Standard code sets
CARC & RARC (X12)

Every adjustment and denial on an 835 remittance is expressed as one of these. See our CARC/RARC guide for the codes that show up most.

Medicare appeal levels
Redetermination → Reconsideration → ALJ hearing

Defined levels, in order. Commercial payers run their own named process — check the EOB or plan documents for that payer's specific steps and deadlines.

Root-cause categories
Eligibility · Coding · Authorization · Documentation · Timely filing

The buckets almost every denial sorts into. See our root-cause analysis guide for how to build the report.

Key takeaways

  • Categorise by root cause before working anything. Date order tells you nothing; cause order tells you what to fix.
  • Read the CARC code before you decide anything. Treating every denial as "the payer is wrong" wastes appeal effort on claims that were correctly denied.
  • Appeal deadlines are shorter than filing deadlines, and separate from them. A denial sitting for a month may already be unappealable, even though the original claim was filed on time.
  • Not every denial is worth appealing. Some cost more to rework than they return; decide deliberately rather than by default.
  • A denial category that recurs is a process defect. Feed the finding back to the front end or it repeats indefinitely.

Sort by cause, then decide what to do with each group

The first act of denial management is classification. Group every denial by why it happened rather than when it arrived: eligibility, authorisation, coding, documentation, bundling, timely filing, coordination of benefits, or contractual. Once grouped, each category has a different answer. Eligibility and authorisation denials are front-end failures and largely preventable. Coding and bundling denials need a coder and often a corrected claim. Documentation denials need the clinical record pulled and reviewed against the payer's coverage policy. Timely filing denials are usually lost, and their only real value is as evidence that a submission process is broken somewhere upstream.

This ordering also reveals scale. A practice discovering that a third of its denials share one cause has found a project rather than a queue, and fixing that upstream is worth more than appealing every instance individually. Working denials without categorising them first is the reason many practices rework diligently for years without their denial rate ever improving — every individual denial gets touched, but the thing producing them never changes, so the queue simply refills at the same rate it's emptied.

Categorisation only works if it happens before anyone starts appealing, not after. A biller who opens a denial and immediately starts drafting a rebuttal has skipped the one step that would have told them whether an appeal is even the right move. Build the sort into the workflow itself — a required field on every worked denial, populated before any other action is taken — rather than treating it as a report generated after the fact from whatever notes happen to exist.

Most practice management systems and clearinghouses already carry the data needed to do this; the gap is almost never data availability, it's that nobody built a workflow that forces the categorisation step before work begins. A denial work queue sorted only by dollar amount or by age optimises for the wrong thing — it surfaces the biggest or oldest balance first, regardless of whether that denial is a five-minute corrected claim or a documentation appeal that needs an hour with the clinical record. Sorting by category first lets a practice batch similar work together, route it to the person best equipped to handle it, and measure recovery rate by category instead of one undifferentiated number that hides which categories are actually being solved.

Read the code before you decide anything

A remittance advice doesn't say "the payer disagrees with you." It says, in a specific coded field, exactly what adjustment was applied and, usually, why. That field is the Claim Adjustment Reason Code, or CARC — a standardised code maintained by X12, the same body that maintains the electronic transaction standards behind the 835 remittance itself. A Remittance Advice Remark Code, or RARC, often rides alongside the CARC on the same line to add detail the CARC alone doesn't capture. Reading both, in order, is the entire difference between an appeal that targets the actual reason a claim didn't pay and an appeal that guesses.

Practices that skip this step tend to fall into one of two failure modes. The first is treating every denial as a fight: drafting a generic appeal letter that restates the claim was billed correctly, without ever engaging the specific reason the payer gave. Payers read these as noise and deny them again, often faster the second time. The second failure mode is the opposite and just as costly: writing off a denial that was actually a simple, fixable submission error, because nobody read past "denied" to see that the fix was a corrected claim, not an appeal.

The table below covers the CARC codes practices run into constantly enough to be worth memorising. It is not the full code set — X12 maintains hundreds of active CARC and RARC codes, and the complete, current list is the only source to treat as authoritative when you hit one that isn't here.

CARCMeaningTypical category
CO-16Claim lacks information or a submission/billing error that prevents correct adjudicationCoding / claim data
CO-18Duplicate claimSubmission process
CO-27Expenses incurred after coverage terminatedEligibility
CO-29Timely filing expiredTimely filing

CO-97Payment adjusted because this service/procedure is not paid separately (bundled service)Bundling

Two of these are worth flagging specifically because they're easy to misread. CO-16 is a category, not a diagnosis — it tells you the claim was incomplete or contained an error, but the specific missing element usually shows up in an accompanying RARC, so CO-16 alone is a prompt to look further, not a finished answer. CO-29 is commonly cited as the timely-filing code, but "timely filing expired" can mean the original claim was late, or it can mean the claim was on time and the payer's system processed it incorrectly — which is exactly the kind of distinction that decides whether an appeal is worth filing at all.

The practical habit worth building is reading the CARC before opening the chart, not after. A biller who reads CO-97 already knows this is a bundling question and can go straight to the NCCI edit pair and the modifier documentation, instead of starting from a blank chart review as if the reason were unknown. A biller who reads CO-31 already knows this is an eligibility problem before touching the clinical record at all, because no amount of documentation fixes a claim sent to the wrong payer. That few seconds of reading, done consistently, is what separates a denials team that resolves a queue efficiently from one that re-investigates the same category from scratch every single time. Work denials on a schedule, inside the appeal window

Appeal deadlines are frequently much shorter than original filing deadlines, and they run from the remittance date rather than the date of service. That's a distinction practices routinely miss, because the original filing deadline is the number everyone learns first and the one most billing software surfaces prominently. A denial that sits in a queue for six weeks may be beyond appeal before anyone opens it, which converts a recoverable claim into a write-off through inaction alone — not because the appeal would have failed, but because it was never filed in time to find out.

The operational answer is a fixed cadence with ownership: denials worked within a defined number of days of receipt, categories assigned to the people equipped to handle them, and an escalation path for anything approaching a deadline. Practices that work denials "when there is time" systematically lose the ones with the shortest windows, which are often the most valuable, since a payer with an aggressive appeal deadline is frequently the same payer with a meaningful reversal rate for well-documented appeals.

Medicare is the one payer where the appeal structure is publicly documented and consistent: a defined sequence running from redetermination through reconsideration to an Administrative Law Judge hearing, each level with its own filing requirement. Commercial payers each run their own process under their own names, with deadlines that can run anywhere from a matter of weeks to a few months depending on the payer and the appeal level, and those deadlines change without much notice. Tracking the appeal clock as a distinct field, separate from the original filing deadline, and specific to each payer, is the operational habit that keeps a recoverable denial from expiring unnoticed.

Escalation is the other half of the schedule, not an optional extra. A denial approaching its deadline with no resolution in sight needs a defined path upward — to a supervisor, to whoever holds the payer relationship, or to a decision to file a placeholder appeal preserving the deadline while the supporting documentation is finished. Without that path, a denial simply expires quietly at whatever level it happened to be sitting when the clock ran out, and nobody finds out until a much later AR aging review turns up a balance that's no longer recoverable. The mechanics of building that tracking, and what a missed deadline actually costs versus what a missed filing deadline costs, are in our timely filing and appeal deadlines guide.

Turn denial data into a root-cause report, not just a longer log

Working every denial one at a time, however diligently, produces a log. It doesn't produce a fix. A practice that reworks two hundred CO-27 eligibility denials a month, recovers most of them, and repeats the exercise every month indefinitely has a functioning denial team and a broken front desk, and the functioning denial team is quietly compensating for the broken front desk instead of ever forcing it to get fixed.

The fix is a category report run on a schedule — monthly at minimum — that shows each root-cause bucket as a share of total denials and as a trend over time, not just a total count. A flat overall denial rate can hide one category collapsing while another grows, and the categories that persist month after month, unchanged, are reliably the ones where nobody ever routed the finding back to whoever owns the process that produces them. If forty per cent of a practice's denials are CO-27, the correct response isn't forty individual appeals; it's an eligibility-verification process change, made once, that prevents the next month's forty from existing at all.

Building that report requires the category to be captured at the point of work, not reconstructed later from free-text notes, and it requires each category to have an owner outside the billing team: eligibility findings go to whoever runs front-desk verification, authorisation findings go to scheduling, coding findings go to the coding team and the claim scrubber, documentation findings go to whoever owns clinical templates. A denial worked but never routed to its owner is a claim recovered and a process left exactly as broken as it was.

The report also has to close the loop, not just open it. A category flagged to the front desk in January that's still the largest bucket in April means the process change was never actually made, or was made and didn't work — and either way, the report should say so explicitly rather than quietly moving on to whatever category is largest that month. The practices that make real progress on denial rate treat the monthly category report the way a clinical team treats a quality metric: reviewed on a schedule, assigned an owner for anything not trending down, and revisited until it does.

Scale matters here too. A solo practice with a handful of denials a week can track root cause in a shared spreadsheet reviewed at a monthly staff meeting; a multi-provider group processing hundreds of claims a day needs the categorisation built into the practice management system or clearinghouse workflow itself, because a spreadsheet maintained by hand at that volume falls behind within weeks and the report quietly stops reflecting reality. The tooling should match the volume, but the discipline — categorise before working, report by category not by total, assign an owner outside billing — doesn't change with size. The full workflow for building the category report, assigning ownership by root cause, and proving a fix actually worked instead of assuming it did, is in our root-cause denial analysis guide.

Write appeals that actually get paid

Once a denial is categorised, its code is read, and it clears the bar of being worth reworking, the appeal itself still has to be built to be read and acted on, not filed and forgotten. A generic letter that restates "this claim was medically necessary and should be paid" without referencing the specific denial reason on the remittance reads to a payer's appeals reviewer exactly like every other template appeal they see, and it gets the same outcome: a second denial, often with less patience attached the second time.

An appeal that gets read cites the specific CARC code and denial reason, states the specific policy, documentation, or billing correction that supports payment against that exact reason, attaches the supporting chart documentation the reviewer needs to actually evaluate it, and states plainly what the practice is asking the payer to do — reprocess, pay a specific amount, or reconsider under a specific policy. Reviewers work volume, and an appeal that makes their job easy gets a faster, more favourable read than one that makes them hunt for the point.

The most common mistakes are avoidable: a vague appeal that never engages the actual denial reason, an appeal filed after the deadline because the appeal clock was never tracked separately from the filing clock, and an appeal filed reflexively on a claim that was, on the facts, correctly denied because the service genuinely wasn't covered. That last one costs more than the write-off would have, because it burns staff time on a claim with no realistic path to reversal, time that a genuinely recoverable denial elsewhere in the queue needed instead.

Consistency matters as much as any individual letter. A practice that builds a standard appeal template per denial category — one structure for medical-necessity appeals, another for bundling disputes, another for eligibility corrections — produces faster, more complete appeals than one starting from a blank page every time, because the template already prompts for the documentation that category typically needs. That doesn't mean sending a form letter; it means the skeleton is consistent while the specific facts, codes, and supporting record change every time. The structure of an effective appeal letter, section by section, with the mistakes that get appeals denied a second time, is in our appeal letters guide.

Step by step: working a denial from receipt to resolution

  1. Read the CARC and RARC togetherDon't skip to drafting a response. The code tells you the category before you decide anything else, and a RARC riding alongside it often supplies the specific missing detail a bare CARC doesn't.
  2. Categorise by root causeEligibility, authorisation, coding, documentation, bundling, timely filing, or coordination of benefits. Record the category as a required field, not an afterthought.
  3. Check the appeal clock, separately from the filing clockConfirm the specific payer's appeal deadline for this denial reason and this appeal level, counted from the remittance date, before doing anything else.
  4. Decide whether it's worth reworkingWeigh expected recovery against staff time. A confirmed, correctly-applied denial for a genuinely non-covered service is often a deliberate write-off, not a fight.
  5. Fix the claim or build the appealA submission or coding error gets a corrected claim, not an appeal. A disputed medical-necessity or documentation denial gets a targeted appeal citing the specific reason and the supporting record.
  6. Route the root cause back to its ownerSend eligibility findings to front-desk verification, authorisation findings to scheduling, coding findings to the scrubber and coding team — whether or not this individual denial was recovered.
  7. Track the category trend, not just this claim's outcomeLog the result against the category so next month's report shows whether the category is shrinking. A denial worked without this step teaches the practice nothing.
Pro tip

Report denials as a percentage of claims by category each month and show the trend, not just the total. A flat overall denial rate can conceal one category collapsing while another grows. The categories that persist month after month are the ones where the prevention was never implemented, and the trend line makes that visible in a way a monthly total never does.

Do and don't

Do
  • Read the CARC and RARC code before deciding how to work a denial.
  • Categorise every denial by root cause before working it.
  • Work denials on a fixed schedule, tracking the appeal deadline separately from the filing deadline.
  • Feed recurring categories back to the front end as process changes.
  • Cite the specific denial reason and attach supporting documentation in every appeal.
  • Decide deliberately which low-value denials are not worth reworking.
Don't
  • Don't work the queue in date order.
  • Don't assume every denial means the payer is wrong; some codes point to a real submission error.
  • Don't resubmit the same claim unchanged and expect a different outcome.
  • Don't let denials age past the appeal deadline while tracking only the original filing deadline.
  • Don't appeal everything reflexively; some cost more than they return.
  • Don't treat a recurring denial category as a cost of doing business.

Frequently asked questions

What denial rate should we be aiming for?

Low single digits at first submission is a reasonable target for most outpatient practices, though the achievable figure varies with payer mix and specialty. More useful than the headline number is the category breakdown: a practice at five per cent with denials spread evenly across many causes is in a very different position from one at five per cent where a single preventable category dominates. Fix the concentration first.

How quickly must denials be worked?

Inside the payer's appeal window, which is often considerably shorter than the original filing deadline and runs from the remittance date. A practical standard is working every denial within a couple of days of receipt, which keeps you comfortably inside even short windows. The risk is not that any single denial is urgent; it is that a queue worked irregularly systematically loses the ones with the tightest deadlines.

Is it worth appealing small-balance denials?

Not always, and pretending otherwise wastes staff time. Compare the expected recovery against the cost of the rework. Low-value denials with poor appeal odds are often better written off deliberately, with the effort redirected to preventing that category. What matters is that the decision is explicit and reviewed, rather than an unspoken habit of ignoring anything small.

How do we stop the same denials recurring?

Route the finding to wherever the cause lives. Eligibility denials go to the front desk process, authorisation denials to scheduling, coding denials to the scrubber and to coder education, medical necessity denials to order entry. A denial worked but not fed back is a claim recovered and a process unchanged, which is why practices can rework diligently for years while their denial rate stays flat.

What's the difference between a CARC and a RARC code?

A CARC states the category of a payment adjustment or denial, such as CO-97 for a bundled service or CO-50 for medical necessity. A RARC often rides alongside it on the same remittance line to add detail the CARC alone doesn't capture. Reading both before deciding how to work a denial is the difference between an appeal that targets the actual reason and one that guesses.

Do you work denials as part of your service?

Yes, and we categorise by root cause before working anything, so the monthly report shows which categories are shrinking and which are not. Every denial is worked within a defined window, and recurring causes are raised as process changes rather than simply reworked each month. The measure we hold ourselves to is whether the category stops appearing, not how many appeals we filed.
